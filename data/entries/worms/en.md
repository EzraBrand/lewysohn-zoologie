# Seventh class: Worms (pp. 334–341)

### ‎Seventh Class.

### ‎Worms.

### ‎§. 460.

‎On the generic names for worms see §. 9; here it need only be noted that שקצים ורמסים often stand side by side, of which שקצים means insects (§. 371) and רמסים worms, from רמס, “to move,” since the footless worms do not walk but merely move themselves along.

‎Worms are either free-living, like the ringed worms and the maggots that arise from the decay of various substances, or they are the so-called intestinal worms (helminths, also Entozoa), which live in other animal organisms. Of all these kinds the Talmud names several species, which we shall discuss according to the arrangement given.

### ‎§. 461.

### ‎The Water Snakelet.

‎Sebachim 22a it is said that on the sediment of a vessel filled with water יבחושין אדומין “red יבחושין” arise. On this Raschi's words read: כמין יחושין בלא כנפיים כדרך שגדל בשולי חביות של יין שלנו מבחוץ יבחושין דקין אף הן נבראים מן המים “like wingless gnats; just as the thin gnats which arise on the outer side of the bottoms of our wine casks, so those too are created from the water.”

‎Raschi here mentions two different species; the winged insects that arise on the bottoms of wine casks are the cellar flies, which were dealt with in §. 429; [p. 335] in our Talmud passage, on the other hand, the water snakelet (Nais tubifex, Voigt [=F. S. Voigt, Lehrbuch der Zoologie (Stuttgart 1835–40); the text names it (p. 9: "Voigt, Lehrb. d. Zool.")] l. c. [=loco citato: in the work cited above] 5. p. 441) is meant; this is brownish-red, for which אדומין is used.

‎Nidda 21a also speaks of יבחושין אדומין; it says there that a miscarrying woman brought forth little lumps of flesh in the form of those worms.

‎Chulin 67a also speaks of insects that arise in water; one may eat them as such together with the water; only when they leave the water are they regarded as שרץ על הארץ “land worm,” and in that case considered unclean; cf. Sch. Ar. [=Shulchan Aruch] Jore Dea §. 84, 1.

### ‎§. 462.

### ‎The Syrup Snakelet.

‎Chulin 67a date syrup, in which תולעים “worms” arise, is also subsumed under water. One may consume the drink unstrained, for the worms that have arisen in it are to be regarded as שרץ על הארץ “land worms” only when they have left the drink; cf. §. 461.

### ‎§. 463.

### ‎The Ringed Worm.

‎Also called earthworm, which is ringed and contracts itself. The Talmud (Chulin 67b) has the following passage on this: Among the creeping animals is the snake, and under this is included the שילשול (slow-worm, §. 301) and whatever resembles the שילשול. Raschi explains: It is a long worm resembling the snake, which is found in dunghills. This is the well-known earthworm, which like the slow-worm is long and contractile.

### ‎§. 464.

### ‎Related Genus.

‎Of the earthworm there are several species, which differ from one another chiefly in that some at times lack the so-called saddle, i.e., the girdle-shaped thickening; they are then naturally thinner than the species provided with the saddle; the former the Talmud (Rosch ha-Schana 24b; Chulin 40a and A. Sara [=Avodah Zarah] 43a) calls שילשול קטן “small earthworm.”

### ‎[p. 336] §. 465.

‎Continuation. Since earthworms, as is well known, are good food for chickens, we are to understand these worms by ניקרי מקילקתא (A. Sara 28a). Raschi explains these words: “worms which the chickens scratch out of dunghills.” Earthworms, as is known, prefer to live in moist earth, and are sought out by chickens.

‎The worms were used against bleeding wounds (ibid.).

### ‎§. 466.

‎Conclusion. Keritot 7b it is said that a miscarrying woman brought forth little lumps of flesh in the form of גוונים. Maimuni explains the word by: עלוקוה והתולעים “leeches (§. 467) and worms.” The pairing with leeches suggests the ringed worm under תולעים, since both kinds of worms are ringed and contractile.

### ‎§. 467.

### ‎The Leech.

‎עלוקה (A. Sara 12b), עלקא (Bechoroth 44b *); for which Aruch reads ערקא), ביני דמיא (Gittin 69b), נימא של מים (A. Sara 12b) and (according to some, though erroneously, see §. 134) יאלי (Sabbat 54b).

‎In the last-named passage it says: A hedgehog skin was tied around the udders of the cows so that the יאלי could not suck off the milk. Raschi explains the word by שנשואש, Sangsues, which is rejected by the Tosephot, since leeches never consume milk. The Tosephot give another, but likewise incorrect, explanation, which was dealt with at length in §. 134.

‎The former names, on the other hand, are the correct designations of the leech. ביני דמיא means literally “caterpillar of the water,” cf. ביניתא §. 458, where a certain resemblance to the caterpillar is indicated.

‎נימא של מים means “thread of the water,” from the elongated shape of the leech.

‎עלקא, and perhaps more correctly ערקא, is probably derived from blood-sucking; thus the blood-containing arteries are called עורקים (§. 73); moreover, the interchange of ל and ר is frequent; cf. my Aphorisms in the L.-Bl. des Orients 1849, col. 302.

‎It is dangerous to drink from brooks and [p. 337] meadow waters at night, since one could easily swallow a leech, the consequence of which is swelling of the body (Bechoroth 44b). The Tosephot (A. Sara 12b) add that the swallowed leech lives on in the human body and sucks out the moisture of the intestines. On the remedy against swallowed leeches see Appendix s.v. שבלול.

‎Against disease of the spleen the eating of roasted leeches is recommended (Gittin 69b). On גנונים, by which Maimuni also understands leeches, see §. 466.

### ‎§. 468.

### ‎The Apple Maggot.

‎Sanhedrin 108b it is related that Noah in the ark fed the chameleon (§. 275) with the maggots which he obtained from the moistened bran

‎חיורא

‎. Aruch reads פרי, and here we are to understand the maggot that develops from the rotting of fruits; the worm cannot be determined more precisely.

### ‎§. 469.

‎Conclusion. In the Talmud passage cited in the preceding article, mention is made of the maggot that arises from mixed bran. Since express reference is made here to the kneading or mixing (גביל) of the bran, what is meant here is not the well-known mealworms or flour mites that arise in old flour; rather, here too it is a kind of maggot that develops from the spoiled bran.

### ‎§. 470.

### ‎The Dung Maggot.

‎Since the black raven is unloving toward its young (on this erroneous belief see §. 205), Providence cares for the latter by letting maggots arise from their own dung (B. batra [=Bava Batra] 8a). Raschi on Erubin 22a explains it more simply, according to which Providence brings gnats to the young ravens, which is already logically more correct, since the ravens cannot excrete dung without having previously taken food, and where would this food come from, which was only supposed to arise from the dung? That worms arise from animal excrement is, moreover, common knowledge.

### ‎[p. 338] §. 471.

### ‎Helminths.

‎Whereas the Talmud mentions only a few species from the realm of parasites, such as the lice on the human body and the whale louse, from the realm of the helminths, i.e. those worms that live in other animal organisms, it mentions a large number of individual species. We shall therefore treat first the helminths in the human body, then those in the body of mammals, and finally those in the body of fish.

### ‎§. 472.

### ‎The Skin Worm.

‎Sometimes the worm מורנא is found between the foreskin and the glans of grown men, and it is removed by circumcision, through which the glans is laid bare. Many a heathen underwent circumcision as a consequence of this circumstance (A. Sara 26b). The name מורנא denotes yet another genus of helminths, on which see §. 482.

### ‎§. 473.

### ‎Phthiriasis.

‎The worm רמה sometimes comes out of the human body by the handful (Temura 31a); this is the well-known lousy disease, which has its seat either chiefly under the scalp or under the skin of the whole body.

### ‎§. 474.

‎From the tongues of the lying spies whom Moses sent to Canaan came forth תולעים, maggots (Sota 35a). This maggot is not to be confused with the phthiriasis mentioned in the preceding article, since the latter comes forth either from the skin, the folds of the skin, the ears, or the anus (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 409); it is rather the maggot that develops through a suppurating swelling.

‎A heretic had a discharge of maggots (מונק תולעים) from his nose (Jerusalem Talmud Joma 39a).

### ‎[p. 339] §. 475.

### ‎Corpse Maggot.

‎The maggot that develops from the human corpse is called רמה and תולע (Aboth 3, 1); the stouter the body, the more maggots it develops as a decayed corpse (ibid. 2, 7). In a corpse that had lain only a few days on a dunghill, maggots crept out of the nose (Joma 19b). In another corpse, which had remained unburied for a longer time, a worm (ריחשא) came out first from the ear (B. mez. [=Bava Metzia] 84b).

### ‎§. 476.

### ‎The Common Roundworm.

‎This worm, which is often present in the human intestines not singly but in great numbers, so that 56 roundworms were passed by a seven-year-old child (Voigt l. c. 6. p. 32), is called כירצא in the Talmud (Gittin 69b). As a remedy against it serves the consumption of ass's milk with laurel leaves (ארע = לורייר, Laurier, whose berry is called בייש, Baies, Gittin ibid.); further, the consumption of bread and salt taken on an empty stomach, and with it a drink of fresh water (B. mez. 107b).

### ‎§. 477.

### ‎The White Roundworm.

‎Since the common roundworm (§. 476) is reddish-brown, the white roundworm is called כירצא חיוורא (Gittin 69b). As a remedy against it serves the seed of spiny sea rocket (גילגולא = אורוגא, Spanish: Oruga, Latin: Eruca, French: Érucago; see Raschi on 2. Kön. [=Kings (German Könige)] 4, 39), which is wrapped in a cotton rag and softened in water, which is then drunk (Gittin ibid.). Might כירצא be related to Ascaris?

### ‎§. 478.

### ‎The Ascarid.

‎The small worms, very common especially in the rectum of children and passed in great numbers, are called כינים שבני מעיים (B. Kama [=Bava Kamma] 82a). The remedy against them is the consumption of garlic (ibid.), a household remedy that is still used today. The inhabitants of hot [p. 340] countries frequently eat garlic; see Jablonski: Allg. Lexicon s.v. Knoblauch.

### ‎§. 479.

### ‎The Tapeworm.

‎קוקיאני (Berachoth 36a), for which Aruch reads קקני. As a remedy against it is the consumption of raw barley meal (ibid.) and the consumption of hyssop (Sabbat 109b). The Mishna (Schebiith 7, 2) mentions a plant root רכפה, whose consumption kills the intestinal worms; see Bartenoro there.

### ‎§. 480.

### ‎The Human Liver Fluke.

‎Through frequent violations of diet, such as the consumption of roasted meat and water while one is still fasting, or through fat beef eaten before breakfast, worms arise in the human liver, which are called ארקתא (Sabbat 109b). The remedy against them is the consumption of יועזר, which is explained by פוחנק. Raschi explains פוחנק by פולאל, Polegion, French: Pouliot, fleabane (ibid.).

‎Mussaphia does not think of worms here, but translates ארקתא by Aurigo, jaundice.

### ‎§. 481.

### ‎The Liver Fluke.

‎Chulin 48a speaks of החליע כבד, the “liver that has become wormy” of cattle; this is the liver fluke, Distoma hepaticum, which is sometimes found in great numbers in the liver of sheep and cattle (Voigt l. c. 6. p. 35).

### ‎§. 482.

### ‎The Sheep Worm.

‎Chulin 49a states that if the worm מורנא (§. 472) comes out of the lung of an animal, the animal is permitted to be eaten, because it is to be assumed that the worm left the lung only after the death of the animal, since during life the lung, strongly moved by breathing, does not permit the exit of the worm, [p. 341] and so the worm can have eaten through the lung and penetrated it only after death.

‎This is the sheep worm, Strongylus filaria, which lives in the windpipe of sheep and causes the so-called sheep cough (Voigt l. c. 6. p. 33).

### ‎§. 483.

‎Conclusion. For מורנא in the preceding article, the Aruch reads מראנה and explains it as a worm that is found alive in the entrails of animals; this is the hydatid (Echinococcus veterinorum), which is found in the entrails of sheep, calves, pigs and others (Voigt l. c. 6. p. 46). The decisors (Sch. Ar. Jore Deah §. 36, 5) declare themselves in favor of Rashi's statement.

### ‎§. 484.

### ‎The Bladder Worm.

‎The pig, it is said (Kiduschin 49b), is subject to more diseases than any other animal, in which the bladder worm was surely in mind. This worm is found in the cellular tissue, between the muscles and even in the brain of domestic animals, especially of the pig, where it causes a loathsome disease in it and makes the meat repulsive (Voigt l. c. 6. p. 44). Cf. §. 170.

### ‎§. 485.

### ‎The Ribbon Worm.

‎The worms found in the intestines of fish may not be eaten, because it is to be assumed that they entered the fish from outside (Chulin 67b). This is probably the ribbon worm (Ligula cingulum, Voigt l. c. 6. p. 37), which is found in the abdominal cavity of fish, and which in some parts of Naples are even eaten as a delicacy.

### ‎§. 486.

### ‎The Fish Louse.

‎The worms that are located between the skin and the flesh of fish may be eaten, because they have arisen from the flesh of the fish itself (Chulin 67b).

### ‎[p. 342] §. 487.

‎Conclusion. Chulin ibid. it is said that the קוקאני are forbidden to be eaten. Rashi explains: they are worms that crawl into the lungs and the liver of cattle through the nostrils.

‎This is incorrect if only because the liver fluke (§. 481) arises in cattle when they must live in a damp region; they never, however, receive those worms from outside.

‎The Tosephot explain it more correctly as fish worms, with whom the decisors (Sch. Ar. Jore Deah §. 84, 16) also agree. The קוקאני are identical with the ribbon worm (§. 485).

### ‎§. 488.

### ‎The Double Worm.

‎The bream (§. 334) may not be eaten in all places (Succa 18a). Rashi gives the reason that small, barely noticeable worms sometimes cling to the fish (which Rashi does not explain correctly).

‎Nothing is known, however, of so-called fish lice in the bream, such as are encountered in the perch, salmon, etc.; but the so-called double worm (Diplozoon paradoxum) is indeed found on the gills of the bream (cf. Voigt l. c. 6. p. 36).
