# Sixth class: Insects (pp. 285–333)

### ‎Sixth Class.

### ‎§. 371.

‎The general name for insects is שקצים, with which רמשים (worms) frequently stands together; but by שקצים, naturally, only the unclean insects, forbidden to be eaten, are understood. On the division into land insects, water insects and winged insects see §. 8.

‎Of this class of animals many species of locust are permitted to be eaten, and the locusts are also discussed very extensively by the Talmud; yet it will never be possible to determine with precision the species mentioned there, since, as Winer (R.-Wb. [=G. B. Winer, Biblisches Realwörterbuch] 1. p. 488) already remarks of the locusts mentioned in the Bible, the ancient translators themselves wavered extraordinarily, and the names also often coincide entirely etymologically. Even Bochart [=Samuel Bochart, Hierozoicon, sive de animalibus Sacrae Scripturae (Lewysohn used the Frankfurt a. M. 1675 edition)]'s excellent work Rosenmüller [=E. F. K. Rosenmüller, Handbuch der biblischen Alterthumskunde, vol. 4, part 2 (Leipzig 1831)] (loc. cit. 4, 2. p. 388) considers largely unsuccessful in this respect.

‎We shall therefore, in those cases in which determination is not possible for us, content ourselves with merely listing the many species of locust, together with the properties ascribed to them by the Talmud, in order at least to prepare the collected material for later research.

### ‎[p. 286] §. 372.

‎In general the locusts are divided into clean and unclean; the Law permits only 4 kinds to be eaten, namely סלעם, חרגל, ארבה and חגב, but adds to ארבה the clause למינו (after its kind) and to the other three kinds the addition למינהו (after their kinds), whereby a large number of locust species can be subsumed under those 4 mentioned.

‎The rules for the cleanness of locusts are, according to the Mishnah (Chulin 59a), the following: The locust must have a) 4 legs, b) 2 leaping legs*), c) 4 wings, and d) the wings must be so broad and so long that they can cover the greater part of the breadth and length of the locust.

‎If the locust has these 4 properties, it is clean, regardless of whether it has a tail or no tail, whether it has an elongated or a round head (Sifte Kohen on Jore Dea §. 85a). In addition to the four signs, however, there is a further necessary element, namely that the locust must be known under the name חגב, which is to be explained as meaning that חגב is clean only when it has the 4 signs of cleanness, since there are also unclean locusts under the name חגב (§. 385). We now come to the individual species.

### ‎§. 373.

### ‎The migratory locust.

‎The Hebrew ארבה (Lev. [=Leviticus] 11, 22) Bochart (Hieroz. [=Samuel Bochart, Hierozoicon (Frankfurt a. M. 1675 edition)] 2, 4, 1, col, 442), following Ibn Ezra, derives from רבו "to be many", whereby that locust is meant which is found in fearful numbers and which is also called the migratory locust or swarming locust (Voigt [=F. S. Voigt, Lehrbuch der Zoologie (Stuttgart 1835–40); the text names it (p. 9: "Voigt, Lehrb. d. Zool.")] l. c. [=loco citato: in the work cited above] 5. p. 336). It is greyish green, the upper wings sometimes faintly spotted with brown. The pronotum has a strongly projecting keel, the breast is brick-red underneath and hairy; the hind thighs are banded dark green, the shins brownish red.

‎It belongs to the clean locusts (Chulin 65a), and it is still eaten today in Morocco by the Arabs (Voigt ibid. p. 339). The Talmud [p. 287] says: The ארבה is the גובאי, i.e. the biblical ארבה is in the vernacular also called גובאי (not to be confused with גוב, Nachum 3, 17)*). Also in Berachoth 40b it is said that on eating the גובאי the blessing "Blessed be He by whose word all came into being!" is recited. Aruch translates גובאי by ברוצי, i.e., Βρούχος [brouchos], but incorrectly, for the brouchos is a wingless species of locust.

‎The ארבה, it is said, has no גבחת (Chulin ibid.), i.e., the front part of its head does not rise into a hump between the antennae (see Funke's: Natur- und Kunstlexicon p. 1017).

‎This locust multiplies so innumerably that prayers were ordained for its extermination (Tanit 19a).

‎It has no tail (Tos. [=Tosafot] Chulin 65b s v. לא)

‎The name גובי is explained by the Jerusalem Talmud (Tanit C. 3. H. 6) as: דו גבו דינא דמריה "it collects the claim of its master", i.e., it appears as a punishment from God.

### ‎§. 374.

### ‎A migratory locust.

‎Since with the biblical ארבה (§. 373) the addition למינו (after its kind) stands, the Talmud includes under it the locust ציפורת כרמים (Chulin 65a). This latter insect is also mentioned in Sabbat 90b alongside חגב, and is thus nothing other than a species of locust; it must therefore appear a gross error when Landau, translating the name as "bird of the vineyards", assumes not a locust but the vine moth, the well-known hawk moth (Sphinx Celerio, Voigt l. c. 5. p. 72). The latter Talmud passage reads: He violates the Sabbath rest who carries out of his dwelling even the smallest amount of a living clean locust of the genus חגב (§. 384); of a dead one the quantity for that violation is the size of a fig; with the ציפורת כרמים, on the other hand, the smallest amount is in every case sufficient for that ritual matter. What, the question is raised, is the ציפורת כרמים? Rab answered: It is the פלאי ביארי. Abaja added: One finds it on trees of one bark (i.e. on young trees). It is used to increase [p. 288] the power of thought. One eats namely its right half, the left one places in a well-closed little copper tube, which one carries about on the left side*).

‎Both the juxtaposition with חגב and the mentioned (and, as one may presume, also permitted) eating of that insect leave no doubt that in both Talmud passages ציפורת כרמים is a clean species of locust of the genus ארבה.

‎Landau lets himself be misled into his error (which Brecher also follows: Das Transcendentale, Magie und magische Heilarten p. 212, note 84) because that moth is also called elephant by some, and פלאי, or (as Aruch writes) פלייה, might be related to פיל (§. 173); but against these weak conjectures the explicit statement of the Talmud, which takes the ציפורת כרמים as a species of locust of the genus of the ארבה, cannot be disregarded; we therefore believe we may rightly drop Landau's explanation**) and shall rather seek to identify a species of locust in our insect.

### ‎§. 375.

‎Conclusion. ציפורת כרמים seems to us to be the locust species Gryllus stridulus (Voigt l. c. 5, p. 340). It is dark brown, the wing covers with lighter spots, the hind wings splendidly red, with a brown border at the end. The hind tibiae black, at the base with a yellowish crust.

‎It is common in summer and autumn on mountains and in forests. It flies up quickly and makes a rattling noise in doing so.

‎From all these features the names attributed to it by the Talmud can be explained. Aruch already remarks that פליה ביארי is a species of locust with which children are wont to play; that beautiful mixture of colours certainly makes our locust a little creature pleasing to children.

‎It may be called ציפורת “bird” [p. 289] because of its quick flying up; כרמים means vineyards in particular, but already, like the biblical כרם, also any choice land laid out on rising ground; ציפורת כרמים thus means “bird of the mountains,” since our locust is frequently found on mountains*). The other name פלאי ביארי I translate: “piercing forests,” i.e. “devastating forests”; פלא means to ruin, to destroy (cf. the lexica) and בירי means forest (Buxdorf [=Johannes Buxtorf, Lexicon Chaldaicum, Talmudicum et Rabbinicum (Basel 1640)] p. 1741); and indeed the Gryllus stridulus is just as destructive as the parent locust ארבה (§. 373).

### ‎§. 376.

### ‎A Species of Locust.

‎סלעם (Lev. 11, 22) is רשון (Chulin 65a), i.e. the biblical סלעם is called רשון in the vernacular. Aruch identifies the insect with רשנא, by which Onkelos renders סלעם; Jonatan has רשונא for it.

‎Ibn Esra derives the word from סלע “rock,” according to which סלעם is a rock locust, i.e. a locust dwelling on rocks.

‎Bochart (loc. cit. col. 446) takes סלע in the sense of בלע “to devour” and considers סלעם a voracious species of locust; Gesenius [=Wilhelm Gesenius, Hebrew lexicon (probably Thesaurus linguae Hebraeae et Chaldaeae Veteris Testamenti, 1829–58)] and Joh. Simon follow him. Accordingly סלעם is a migratory locust.

‎It should be noted that some call this locust not רשון but ניפול (Chulin 66a). Both names indicate the smallness of the locust. ניפול is also the name of the small bird, like Pullus, cf. §. 247, and רשון appears precisely as a designation of the smallness of an object; thus it is said: the human abortus is at the beginning of its formation the size of a רשון (Midr. [=Midrash] R. Wajikra fol. 181, col. 1 and on this Tos. Nidda 25a. s.v. תחלת), and so we would have to think of a small voracious species of locust.

### ‎§. 377.

‎Conclusion. Here let the distinction be mentioned which the Tosephot make (Chulin 65b s.v. לא); according to it the ארבה (§. 373) is tailless and not humped on the forehead, the חרגול (§. 380) is humped and tailed, whereas the סלעם is tailless and humped.

‎The by-names רשון and ניפול (§. 376) are in some places also assigned to the חרגול; see §. 389 and Jonatan on Lev. 11, 22.

### ‎[p. 290] §. 378.

### ‎Related Genus.

‎To the genus of the סלעם the Talmud reckons the locust יוחנא ירושלמית (Chulin 65a); it is therefore, like סלעם, tailless, but humped on the forehead. As a clean locust it is permitted to be eaten. The epithet ירושלמית suggests that יוחנא too is gen. fem., and one might then think of the woman's name יוחני (a notorious sorceress; Sota 22a). It is not rare that animals are named after human names; one thinks of the glow-worm (Johanniswürmchen, "St. John's worm"), the St. Peter's fish, the Surinam Aeneas etc. Perhaps in this sense the bird names שבור אנדרפטא, פירח אנדרפתא (§. 185) and פרוא (§. 223) are also to be explained, since the Talmud (Chulin 62b) connects these names with men's names; פרוא too, it says there, was a sorcerer.

‎On the formation of human names from animal names see §. 109. Incidentally let it be remarked here that above p. 62 note ** we asserted that the Talmud only adds geographical epithets to the actual animal names but does not form the former after the homeland; this assertion is not contradicted by the name שפן (§. 152), since it is altogether improbable that the Hebrew word was formed from Spain; cf. above p. 110.

### ‎§. 379.

### ‎Related Genus.

‎To the genus of the סלעם the Talmud (Chulin 65b) further reckons the locust אושכף; like that one it is tailless and humped on the forehead between the antennae. It is permitted to be eaten.

‎Perhaps אושכף is corrupted for אוכף and is the saddle locust (Ephippigera). On the forehead there is a conical hump; however, the saddle locust is wingless and therefore cannot be clean.

### ‎§. 380.

### ‎The Grasshopper.

‎Bochart (loc. cit. col. 445) compares the Hebrew חרגל (Lev. 11, 22) with the Arabic حَرْجَلٌ [ḥarjalun], which in Arabic means a swarm of locusts. حَرْجَل [ḥarjal] means to hop, to gallop, which is why Gesenius translates it as grasshopper (Heupferd, "hay-horse"). This is the common grasshopper [p. 291] (Locusta viridissima). Green, with wings lying parallel, twice as long as the body, the wing spot of the back brown, the ovipositor straight. With the wings over 2 inches long (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 332).

‎This locust is called ניפול (§. 377) in some places, in others רשין (ibid.; Chulin 65a). It is tailed and humped on the head (§. 377).

‎The egg of the חרגול was worn in the ear as a remedy against earache (Sabbat 67a). It may be eaten.

‎The pl. of חרגול is חרגולי; thus it says in the Sulat for the 1st Sabbath of Chanuca (s.v. אין צור): טסו חרגולי ילק “The Chargole and the Jelek came flying.”

### ‎§. 381.

### ‎Related Genus.

‎To the genus of the חרגול the Talmud (Chulin 65a) assigns the locust ערצוביא; it therefore shares with the former the characteristics relating to bodily structure, cf. §. 380.

### ‎§. 382.

### ‎Related Genus.

‎To the genus of the חרגול the Talmud (Chulin 65b) further assigns the locust כרספת; like the former it has the same bodily structure (§. 380) and is permitted to be eaten.

‎Is our word perhaps meant to be the Greek Χρυσοφαής [chrysophaēs], designating an insect of a gleaming golden colour? Thus in Arabic a locust is named for its red colour: Djerad achmar, red locust.

### ‎§. 383.

### ‎Related Genus.

‎The locust שחלנית (Chulin 65b) also belongs to the genus of the חרגול. According to an explanation of Rashi's, however, כרספת and שחלנית designate one and the same species, only it is called by different names in different places.

### ‎§. 384.

### ‎Gryllus Coronatus.

‎Bochart (loc. cit. col. 444) derives the Hebrew חגב (Lev. 11, 22) from the Arabic حَجَبَ [ḥajaba] "to veil", because this locust, in its fearful numbers, draws a veil before the sun, and this [p. 292] is supposed to be the Gryllus coronatus named by Linnaeus (Rosenmüller loc. cit. 4, 2, 389). In the Talmud, however, חגב is a collective name for many families of locusts, for there are, it says (Chulin 63b), 800 kinds of חגבים; moreover, the natural-historical characteristics are of too general a nature for a particular kind of locust to be determined from them. The palps of the חגב are called קרני חגבים (Pesachim 48b*); its neck resembles that of cattle, i.e. the head is separated from the trunk by a neck, whereas in many insects the head is attached to the trunk by a thread (A. Sara [=Avodah Zarah] 51a). It is blinded in thawing weather, so that it can easily be caught (Sabbat 106b); it also arrives in such dense masses that one need only reach out to catch it (ibid.). Its extraordinary proliferation led to the ordaining of prayers for its extermination (Tanit 14a). It sometimes devastates individual tracts of land, sometimes the entire fruit-bearing region (B. mez. [=Bava Metzia] 105b).

‎Some חגבים (probably brightly coloured ones, see §. 375) served children for play and amusement (much as butterflies and beetles do now, Sabbat 90b). A regular trade was carried on with this locust; it was caught in masses and then, after wine had been poured over it, sold in baskets, for which reason the Israelite was forbidden to buy such; he was permitted to do so only in the dealer's house, before the latter had poured wine over the locusts (A. Sara 39b).

‎In the vernacular the חגב is also called גדיאן (the Aruch reads גדיין).

### ‎§. 385.

### ‎The Unclean Chagab.

‎The Mishnah (Terumoth 10, 9) also mentions the unclean חגבים, which thus do not possess all the characteristics necessary for a locust to be clean (§. 372).

‎They are salted and yield a juice which may be consumed (ibid., and on this above §. 95).

### ‎§. 386.

### ‎Related Genus.

‎To the genus of the חגב the Talmud also assigns the locust רזבנית (Chulin 65a); it is accordingly permitted to be eaten; on the bodily structure of the חגב, in which the רזבנית must resemble it, see §. 377.

### ‎[p. 293] §. 387.

### ‎Related Genus.

‎To the genus of the חגב the Talmud further assigns the locust צרצור, which, however, may not be eaten; perhaps it is identical with the kind of locust mentioned in §. 385, the unclean Chagab. Landau translates צרצור as "comb locust" (Kammheuschrecke), but gives no reason for this. The word is rather the Syriac ܨܪܨܘܪܐ [צרצורא], which Castellus explains thus: Species locustae repens, non volans, percutit radices arborum et exarescunt, "a creeping, wingless kind of locust which bores through the roots of trees so that they wither."

‎The wingless צרצור is therefore rightly determined to be unclean (§. 372). With the Syriac word the Syriac translator also renders the Hebrew צלצל (the letters ל and ר frequently interchange; cf. my Aphorisms in the L. Bl. des Orients [=Literaturblatt des Orients (Leipzig 1840–51), the literary supplement of Der Orient] 1849, col. 302), which word (5. B. M. [=Buch Mose: book of Moses (5. B. M. = Deuteronomy)] 28, 42) is, according to Rosenmüller (loc. cit. 4, 2. p. 397), supposed to be the whirring locust, Gryllus stridulus; but the latter locust is winged, cf. §. 375.

‎צלצל seems rather to be the Ephippigera perforata (Voigt l. c. 5, p. 334), of which, despite the lack of wings, the males nevertheless produce sounds, which happens through the spiracles. Accordingly צרצור and צלצל would be identical. It has only 4 legs (Chulin 65b); in the Jerusalem Talmud Sabbat 3b our locust is called חלזון.

### ‎§. 388.

### ‎A Related Genus.

‎Of the סקאה, with which the Chaldean [Targum] renders the biblical צלצל (§. 387), the plural is סקאין (Tanit 6a). From this passage it emerges merely that the insect is voracious and destructive; it is brought by the latter rain (מלקוש). Perhaps סקאה means "the one brought." נסק means "to go up", like עלה, then "to bring"; the locust brought by the rain would therefore be called סקאה.

### ‎§. 389.

### ‎The Mantis.

‎איל קמצא (Idejoth 8, 4). Maimuni explains: איל קמצא is a kind of locust that resembles a stag.

‎If one observes the mantis when it holds its raptorial legs aloft, [p. 294] particularly when it creeps out of the egg, the head does indeed bear some resemblance to the head of a stag, since the raptorial legs rise up like antlers on both sides of the head (see the illustration in Oken vol. 2, plate 40, fig. 3b).

‎Accordingly the name אייל means "stag" and קמצא is from קמץ "to grasp".

‎Maimuni says it is מין הארבה “of the kind of the ארבה” (§. 373); Rashi, on the other hand (on A. Sara 37a), considers it מין חגב “a kind of the חגב” (§. 384). It says there: The אילא קמצא is a clean locust. Some call it שושיבא, others סוסביל. According to Rashi, however, two different species are meant here. The שושיבא has an elongated head, the סוסביל has no elongated head, and its wings barely cover the greater part of the body. In fact, the statement about סוסביל fits the praying mantis. Its head is thick, tapering somewhat narrowly toward the mouth, but not long; its wings lie flat and end before the anus, whereas in many other kinds of locust they extend beyond the entire trunk of the body; cf., however, §. 391, note.

### ‎§. 390.

‎Conclusion. Buxdorf (Lex. p. 71) says: אייל קמצא means, as it were, stag-locust, and seems to be the beetle Scarabeus Lucanicus, also called Cervus volans, Greek Κάραβος [karabos]. This is the well-known stag beetle, which, however, certainly could not have counted as a clean insect; for the beetle genus is expressly designated as unclean (§ 408). Still more curious is Landau's error; he copies Buxdorf, but takes the other meaning that the Greek word has, namely sea crab(!), which can be regarded as clean even less; Landau was probably misled by Locusta, as the sea crab is also called.

### ‎§. 391.

### ‎A Kind of Locust.

‎שושיבא (A. Sara 37a). Some designate the praying mantis by this name (§. 389*); Rashi, however, considers שושיבא a distinct [p. 295] species which has an elongated head. It is also mentioned elsewhere (Sabbat 90b) as a distinct kind. It says there: A שושיבא flew into someone's mouth; he took it and gave it to another, so that he would not come under suspicion of having eaten a living insect; from this it thus also follows that the שושיבא is a clean kind of locust. Buxdorf (Lex. p. 2535) translates the word as “fat locust,” but gives no reason for this.

### ‎§. 392.

### ‎A Kind of Locust.

‎The Talmud frequently mentions the locust קמצא.

‎Sabbat 77b it says: Why are the antennae of the קמצא soft? Because it lives among stinging nettles (חילפי Rashi explains, ibid. 152 a, by אורטיאש, Ortie; cf. also §. 393), and therefore the antennae, if they were hard, could easily be damaged. Here Rashi says that קמצא is explained by some as a locust, by others as an ant*).

‎Rashi likewise translates the word קמצא in two ways on Jebamoth 121b, where, however, the translation as locust is to be preferred, since there the subject is an insect with which children played (cf. §§. 375 and 384). On Tanit 21b, too, קמצי are probably only locusts. It is related there: Once the קמצי arrived, whereupon Rabbi Jehuda had a fast day proclaimed; when soon afterward it was reported that the קמצי were doing no damage, he asked ironically: Did they perhaps bring their food along with them? Here the ants cannot be meant, since these do not come migrating from afar.

‎Against jaundice the juice of the קמצי was used (Sabbat 110b), where perhaps ants are rather to be thought of after all, whose juice, as is well known, yields the spirit of ants still in use in medicine today.

‎Salomon b. Aderet also erroneously translates the word קמצא on Berachoth 54b as locusts, whereas the subject there is the white ant (§. 457).

‎The word is derived from קמץ and means “to pinch,” “to grasp,” which alludes to the catching of the locusts with the raptorial legs.

‎The Ἀκρίδες [akrides] occurring in Mat. 3, 4 and Apoc. 9, 3 the Syriac translates by: ܓܒܐ [גבא].

### ‎[p. 296] §. 393.

### ‎A Kind of Locust.

‎כרוי דבי חילפי “locusts that live among stinging nettles” (Chulin 62b); חילפי Rashi here too translates by אורטאיש, Ortie, and our locust seems to be identical with the one in the preceding article.

‎It may be eaten. Perhaps כרוי is identical with כרוכא, by which Jonathan [=Targum (Pseudo-)Jonathan] translates the biblical חגב (Lev. 11, 22). The Aramaic word seems to be composed of בא and כרז and means: “The crier comes,” since the migrating swarm of locusts causes a whirring noise, and so כרוי too may allude to that whirring.

‎The Tosephot (ibid. s.v. כרוי) incorrectly explain the animals as small birds, since there is not the slightest analogy for this.

‎For כרוכא some editions of that paraphrase have כרוכא, i.e. the leaf-roller caterpillar, from כרוך.

### ‎§. 394.

### ‎A Kind of Locust.

‎כרוי דבי קרבי “locusts that live on cabbage” (Chulin 62b); they may not be eaten. We have spoken about כרוי in the preceding article; קרבי is the Greek Κρύμβη [krymbē]; the μ [m] dropped out, as in many words given below*).

‎Aruch reads כירבי for כרבי, which is supposed to mean “rubbish heaps,” and כרוי are supposed to be amphibians, which is incorrect for the simple reason that amphibians do not, in fact, live under rubbish heaps: cf. §. 110.

### ‎§. 395.

### ‎The Stick Insect.

‎The Hebrew חסיל (Joël 1, 4) the LXX translate by Βρούχος [brouchos]; this is a wingless kind of locust, and Bochart derives the word from חסל “to devour”**).

‎The stick insect (Bacillus)

‎[p. 297] is wingless, the body cylindrical, like a thin stick, with simple long legs. The antennae are thick and short; the forelegs project far beyond the head (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 349). It multiplies to such an extent that prayers were ordained for its extermination (Tanit 19a). Of these locusts, each gathers its own supply in summer (Sanhedrin 94b); cf. also Rashi on Jes. [=Isaiah] 33, 4.

### ‎§. 396.

### ‎A Kind of Locust.

‎To the genus חגב (§. 384) the Talmud also assigns the locust זחל, which is also called אסקרין; it is born without leaping legs, but acquires them later and is therefore permitted to be eaten (Chulin 65a). Evidently אסקרין is the Greek ἀσκαρής [askarēs] “not leaping,” and is merely the translation of זחל “to creep”; accordingly we should perhaps think of a species of locust from the third family, whose legs are formed sometimes as leaping legs, sometimes for digging.

‎On the alpha privativum see §. 330.

### ‎§. 397.

‎The מין גוזי occurring in Bechoroth 7b and Sabbat 106b is explained by Raschi as מין חגב “a locust belonging to the genus חגב (§. 384),” but it more correctly means wasp; on this in detail §. 404.

‎Pseudoraschi on Tanit 6a incorrectly takes מלקוש as a locust; the word means only the latter rain; cf. §. 388.

‎Raschi (Bechoroth 7b) also erroneously counts צירעין (hornet) among the locusts*).

### ‎[p. 298] §. 398.

### ‎The Scorpion.

‎עקרב, pl. עקרבי (Sanhedrin 67b) and עקרבים (Chagiga 3. a). On ערקבנין see Bartenoro on Schebiith 7, 2. Its dangerous weapon, the sting, is called עקרב עקיצת (Aboth 2, 10); its bite too is dangerous (Sanhedrin 97a*). It leaves poison behind in the puncture wound (Raschi on Aboth 2, 10). This insect is all the more fearsome in that it wounds people unexpectedly and unprovoked (Sanhedrin ibid.). It lives in waterless cisterns (Chagiga 3a), and also on dunghills, heaps of stones, in holes and in cracks in walls (Pesachim 8a). A remedy against the scorpion's sting is said (according to Raschi) to be the spider, of which the scorpion is also said to be afraid (Sabbat 77b); but סממית in these two statements does not mean spider, but lizard, which was discussed in detail earlier (§. 273).

‎Another remedy against the scorpion's sting is the urine of a child 40 days old (Sabbat 109b; cf. Tos. on Nidda 63a s.v. תנא). For the illness resulting from the scorpion's sting, catching cold is especially dangerous (A. Sara 28b); against the bite wound of this insect the gall of the stork was used (Ketuboth 50a, and on this §. 204). Without a thorough cure, a six-year-old child struck by the bite of a scorpion would inevitably have to die (ibid.). Yet the dangerous insect also has a medicinal use. It is said: “Against cataract one should take עקרבא דשב חומרי, dry it in the shade; then grind two parts of eye paint and one part of the scorpion, and pour three equal quantities of this mixture (as much as goes onto the surgical instrument מכחול) into each eye; but no more, since otherwise the eye may be completely destroyed" (Gittin 60a). That expression עקרבא דשב חומרי Raschi erroneously explains as: “sevenfold colored on account of age"; more correctly Buxdorf (s.v. חמר, p. 790), following Aruch: “sevenfold jointed"; for the abdomen and tail of the scorpion together form 12 segments (Voigt [p. 299] l. c. 4, p. 114), of which the tail usually has 6, sometimes 7 segments; cf. Aelian (l. c. 6, 20). Plinius (h. n. [=Pliny the Elder, Natural History (Naturalis historia)] 11, 25) says: It is certain that the scorpions with seven segments in the tail are more malignant; most have six segments.

‎Since the scorpion cannot swim, in order to get across the water it sat on the back of a crocodile, which carried it to the other bank (Nedarim 41a, and on this §. 271*). It was regarded as a miracle that in Jerusalem no one was ever wounded by a scorpion (Aboth 5, 5). According to the statement of the Midrasch (R. Wajikra fol. 179, col. 4 and ibid. Bereschit fol. 49, col. 2), the female of the scorpion is said to produce 60 young at one time; according to another passage (ibid. Schemoth fol. 116, col. 3) even 70, which is probably a printing error. Voigt too (l. c. 4, p. 116) gives the number as 50—60. The scorpion has two pairs of legs (Chulin 67b).

‎On the water scorpion see §. 453 at the end.

### ‎§. 399.

### ‎The Adiabenian Scorpion.

‎עקרב שבחדייב (Sabbat 121b). Aruch reads הדייב, a reading which Mussaphia prefers. Adiaba was a Syrian region, and the scorpions in it were so dangerous that one was permitted to kill them even on the Sabbath, without their first threatening to attack.

‎On חדיב see Targum on Ezech. [=Ezekiel] 27, 23 and Kiduschin 72a, and in detail vol. 5 of Kerem Chemed, p. 210 ff.

### ‎§. 400.

### ‎The Spider.

‎בוכיא (Succa 52a); Raschi explains: It is the עכביש or אוריניא, Araignée; in the parallel passage (Sanhedrin 99b) כוביא; further: שממית (ibid. 103. b) and (according to Raschi, but erroneously; see §. 273) סממית (Sabbat 77b**). In the first two passages it says:

‎[p. 300] Passion at first resembles the thread of the spider; later it becomes strong like the ropes on a wagon; for this the Midrasch (R. Bereschit fol. 25, col. 4) has: the cables of a ship*).

‎In the third passage it says: Amon burned the Law and abolished the divine service, so that the spiders could calmly spin their webs on the forsaken altar.

‎Besides this explanation, Aruch also has the one that Amon let a lizard climb onto the altar, so that he desecrated it by means of an animal designated by the Law as defiling. He refers to Jonathan, who translates לטאה (§. 272) at Lev. 11, 30 by שממיתא. But the former explanation appears far more appropriate, and one may also ask why Amon should have chosen precisely a lizard, and not some other unclean and more common animal**).

‎The thread is called חוט, the web קורי (Raschi, Sanhedrin ibid). The spider is the creature most hated by human beings (Jalk. Schim. [=Yalkut Shimoni] Proverb. [=Proverbs] fol. 145, c; see the following article at the end).

‎On the tarantula see §. 414.

### ‎§. 401.

‎Conclusion. B. Kama [=Bava Kamma] 80a says: One may raise fox-spitzes (§. 124), cats (§. 118), monkeys (§. 112) and large weasels (§. 129), so that they keep the house clear of mice.

‎The ape, however, is not a carnivorous animal; Bochart (Hieroz. 1, 3, 36, col. 1036) therefore has the reading קופדים (hedgehog, §. 134) instead of קופין, apes. The reading קופין could, however, be retained if, with regard to the ape, the clearing away of spiders and flies was meant. Voigt (l. c. p. 238) says of apes: “Their natural food is fruit, sweet roots, insects and, above all, eggs. They constantly snatch at flies in order to eat them, and an ape that I once let loose in an uninhabited hall [p. 301] was in an instant busy catching all the spiders from the windows and swallowing them." Thus perhaps in earlier times people may have kept apes in order to catch spiders, flies and other troublesome insects.

‎The aversion to spiders is very old, cf. Jalkut Schim. part 2, fol. 145, c.: אין בכל השרצים שנואה כשממית.

### ‎§. 202.

### ‎The Bee.

‎דבורה, pl. דבורים (Kelim. 16, 7) and דבריאתי (Sota 48b). Beekeeping has always been a lucrative branch of husbandry. The Talmud gives the following method of promoting beekeeping. At the beginning of summer the first brood emerges and flies onto trees; this brood is caught and brought into a new hive. After 9 or 10 days the third generation already leaves this hive, which is in turn caught in a new basket, and which after just as many days produces the fourth generation. These can be obtained 7 to 8 times in one summer, but the later they are, the less they are worth (B. batra [=Bava Batra] 80a).

‎Only in summer do the bees produce honey; in winter pieces of honeycomb are placed in the basket (hive), on which the bees feed (Sabbat 43b). Their food in summer is water (ibid. 155b) and the juices of flowers, which they seek out in the fields and on the highest mountains (Sota 48b). They are also fond of mustard, which, however, so irritates their palate that they then consume the honey themselves, and heavily, for which reason mustard is not left standing near the beehive (B. batra 18a).

‎One sees from these statements that the nature of this wonderful insect was not precisely known to the Talmud. As is well known, the whole swarm of bees has only one origin, in that it arises from the eggs of the so-called queen, which after her fertilization lays about 12,000 eggs in the first days. On the twentieth day after the egg was laid, the worker bee (sterile) emerges, on the twenty-fourth day the male (the drone), and the queen (Weisel) is said to appear as early as the sixteenth day (Voigt l. c. 4, p. 273). Of the continually repeated broods, one reckons four per year to a hive; according to the Talmud there would have to be many more.

‎The swarm of bees is called נחיל של דבורים; it sometimes flies out and settles on a tree, [p. 302] in which case one should not catch the bees individually, but rather saw off the tree, since otherwise the bees could fly away (B. Kama 81b). נחיל is the Arabic نَحْلَة [naḥla].

### ‎§. 403.

‎Conclusion. The beehive is protected both from the heat of the sun and from rain (probably only for the sake of the honey; but, if it is fastened with clay, from the rain for its own sake) by a cover stretched over it (Sabbat 43b). The beehive, which is called כוורת (Kelim 22, 10), was made of straw or of reed (Oholoth 8, 1) and fastened to the ground with clay or nails (Uxin 3, 10). When one wished to take the honeycombs (חלות דבש) out of the hive, the bees were first driven away by burning cattle dung, the smoke of which the bees cannot bear (§. 30), and so they withdraw (Kelim 16, 7). From the honeycombs the honey was obtained (Uxin 3, 11). One takes 10, indeed 20, such combs from a hive (B. batra 80a). Two of them are left in the hive for the winter as food for the bees (ibid.). When the hive is full, the honey flows out of it, and this honey mixed with wax is used especially for medicinal purposes (Sabbat 110b). Honey is usually yellow, sometimes also white (Gittin 69a). Swindlers adulterate it with water (Sota 48b); such adulterated honey is called דבש הזיפין; if it is mixed with flour, yet such that the honey still remains liquid and thin, then it is called דבש הצפחת (Machschirin 5, 9). In ritual respects honey is regarded as a beverage (ibid. 6, 4*). Wax is called שעוה, explained more precisely by קירותא (Κηρὸς [Kēros]) (Sabbat 20b), and קירה (Cera, Jebamoth 102a). Spoiled honey was smeared on the sore (איישרא, ichorous) hump of the camel (B. mez. 38b); it is also given spoiled honey to eat (Sabbat 154b).

‎The bee dies as soon as it has stung someone (Rabba Bamidbar fol. 269a), because it thereby loses its sting.

### ‎[p. 303] §. 404.

### ‎The Wasp.

‎גוין usually stands next to צירעין (hornets), but is explained by Raschi as a kind of locust. On Sabbat 106b he says: גוין are a kind of חגב and may be eaten; on Berachoth 7b, he says: גוין and צירעין are kinds of ארבה; already the variation concerning גוין, which are counted now among חגב (§. 384), now among ארבה (§. 373), but even more the evident error of explaining צירעין too as a kind of locust, justifies the assumption that גוין also belong to the order of Hemeroptera and are related to צירעין, the hornets.

‎Bechoroth l. c. states that by honey without qualification bee honey is understood; this is thus called דבש דבורים (Machschirin 6, 4), or simply דבש for short, but the honey of the hornet is called only דבש הצירעים, and that of wasps only דבש הגוין; thus there can be no question in the remotest sense of locusts.

‎Sabbat l. c. states: Whoever catches locusts, wasps (גוין), hornets and gnats on the Sabbath is guilty of desecrating the Sabbath.

‎Another opinion bases the distinction here on the difference between insects that one is otherwise accustomed to catch for the sake of their usefulness and those that one leaves uncaught. Rashi counts among the former the locusts and the גוין, which, as mentioned, he likewise explains as a kind of locust, and among the latter the hornet and the gnats; the more correct division, however, is probably that only the locusts belong to the former insects, since they were eaten and were even the object of a regular trade (§. 384), while to the latter belong wasps, hornets and gnats, since the two former kinds of insects are left undisturbed by people on account of their painful sting alone.

‎On the reading גוי for זבי, by which the Tosephot (on Nidda 17a s.v. מקרקש) wish to denote the wasps, cf. §. 285.

### ‎§. 405.

### ‎The Hornet.

‎צירעה (Tanit 13a), pl. צירעין (Sabbat 106b); further: זיבורא, fem. זיבוריתא (A. Sara 17b), pl. זבורי (Sanhedrin 109b), and ערעיתא (Sabbat 80b).

‎For the woman's name דבורה the Talmud writes זיבורתא (Megilla 14b, and on this §. 109).

‎[p. 304] The hornet, expressly described as a winged insect (Pesachim 24b), is, as is well known, very dangerous on account of its sting when it is provoked*), and is therefore frequently mentioned alongside the scorpion (Kerithoth 3b and often). Its sting would infallibly kill a nine-year-old child unless one ground moss that grows around a palm tree in water and gave it to the child to eat (Ketuboth 50a). The Tosephoth, however, regard this not as a cure but as a preventive, and the former probably rightly, for, as is claimed, 4 hornets can kill even a horse. Once, too, a hornet killed a man by a sting on the forehead (Sabbath 80b). It is said here that the insect came out of the wall; indeed, hornets usually inhabit hollow trees, but sometimes also buildings (Voigt l. c. 4. p. 288). If someone has swallowed a hornet, he must drink very sharp (איגרא, aigre) vinegar if he still wishes to gain time to draw up a will (A, Sara 12b). As the most inhuman cruelty of the inhabitants of Sodom it is recorded that they once smeared with honey a girl who had handed bread to a poor man, and then exposed the unfortunate creature to the stings of the hornet (Sanhedrin 109b). Prayers were therefore ordained for the extermination of this insect (Tanit 14a). Crushed flies laid on the wound are said to be a remedy against its sting (Sabbat 77b), further the urine of a child 40 days old (ibid. 109b). Above all, the person stung must guard against catching cold (A. Sara 28b).

‎On the stone bumblebee cf. below §. 512.

### ‎§. 406.

### ‎The Ninevite Hornet.

‎The Ninevite hornet (צירעה שבנינוה) is so dangerous that it may be killed even when unprovoked, and indeed on the Sabbath (Sabbat 121b). Ordinarily the hornet is peaceable and simply must not be provoked (Voigt l. c. 4. p. 288); accordingly, that Talmudic hornet must be much more dangerous. According to Jalkut Schimoni (Exod. [=Exodus] fol 98, col 1) the hornet kills by poison; as is well known, however, every bee has a poison vesicle at the base of its sting (Voigt loc. cit. p. 269).

‎[p. 305]

‎In our Talmud passage צירעה שבנינוה and זבוב שבארץ מצרים (Egyptian fly, §. 427) stand side by side, and both insects are probably meant in Jes. 7, 18; there that fly is called זבוב אשר בקצה יארי מצרים "fly at the banks of the Nile of Egypt," and our hornet דבורה אשר בארץ אשור "bee in the land of Assyria"; Nineveh was, as is well known, the capital of that empire.

### ‎§. 407.

### ‎The Fig Gall Wasp.

‎The fig gall wasp, also called the fig borer (Cynips psenes), which lays its eggs in the fruits of the wild fig, is called פה דתאנה "opening of the fig" (Sabbat 90a), because the wasp lays the eggs into the small opening at the top of the fig. It is dangerous to swallow, and someone who ate the fig together with the hatched worm died as a result (ibid.). Cf. §. 440.

‎I take the liberty of adding to this article that in considering our wasp we must admire the divine omnipotence with reverence and adoration. Our insect is the means of carrying the male pollen into the female flower of the fig. The fig flower has a very small opening, so that the entry of the male pollen by the wind is made very difficult; the winged insects emerging from the pupae of our wasp now carry that dust on their wings into the female flower.

### ‎§. 408.

### ‎The Beetle.

‎The חפושית, and whatever resembles it, belongs to the unclean insects (Chulin 67b); by that addition is understood the numerous genus of beetles, of which a fair number are mentioned in the Talmud.

‎Rashi explains that word by אישקרבוטי, Escarbot.

‎If it lies in water, the water enters through the mouth and passes out through the anus, since the interior of the beetle is shaped like a simple tube and allows that passage (Para 9, 2). Thus Aristotle says (Naturg. [=Aristotle, History of Animals (Historia animalium; German title Naturgeschichte)] 4, 7, 7): Inwardly, the mouth is directly joined by the intestine, which in most insects runs straight and simple to the outlet and is coiled only in a few. Zeller (Tractatus de [p. 306] Vacca rufa, Amsterd. 1711) erroneously translates חפושין in the relevant passage in Maimuni (ch. 9, §. 11) by piscis (fish), in doing which he no doubt had חיפושא (§. 345) in mind.

‎The Aramaic name חפושית corresponds to the Arabic خُنْفَسآء [khunfasāʾ]*).

### ‎§. 409.

### ‎The Cockchafer.

‎Against earache the fat**) of the large חיפושתא is recommended (A. Sara 28b). The insect is called: גמלניתא חיפושתא.

‎Rashi explains the word חיפושתא: אשקרבוט ובלשון כנען קרוקים, i.e. Escarbot, in another language***): "Kerukim."

‎The same author explains the word גמלניתא (transposed from μέγαλον [megalon]; see in detail §. 355) by: גמה והוא הנטון, “large, namely the Hanneton”†). As is well known, there are two species of cockchafer; the common one (Melolontha [p. 307] vulgaris) is called Hanneton in French; the smaller one is the chimney-sweep (Melolontha Hippocastani). The former grows to 1 inch long, after two years it reaches 1½ inches (Voigt l. c. 4. p. 400), and it is the one meant in our passage. It is called the large beetle because the tip of its tail is longer than in the black chimney-sweep.

‎It should be noted that some derive גמלן from גמל (camel), just as in Greek βοῦς [bous] (ox) serves to denote size, e.g. βούπαις [boupais], βουγάϊος [bougaios], βουκόρυζα [boukoryza]; see p. 151, note.

### ‎§. 410.

### ‎The Tree Beetle.

‎The genus of the Xylophagi, or wood-destroying beetles, is fairly large, and it is therefore hardly possible to determine which species is meant in the Talmud. They are generally called חולעת (Middoth 2, 5) and דרני (Sabbat 102b). The cedar is most afflicted by the beetles, so that a cedar hollowed out by them is called ססמגור “moth-gnawed” (Joma 9b and Aruch on it).

‎The priests afflicted with bodily blemishes, who were not permitted to perform the sacrificial service, had the office of sorting out the worm-eaten wood, since this could not serve as fuel on the altar (Middoth 2, 5).

‎In boards they bore holes, which are filled with lead, whereupon the boards are usable for the construction of the Temple (Sabbat 102b).

‎The beetles sometimes bore through the rungs of ladders to such a degree that these break under one's step (Maccoth 7b).

‎People sought to protect the trees by exterminating the beetles (A. Sara 50b); here, according to Rashi, shaking off or picking off the beetles and caterpillars is meant; in the Mishnah (Schebiit 2, 2), on the other hand, fumigation is recommended for killing those insects harmful to the fruits, for which sulphur was probably used.

‎Buxdorf (Lex. p. 84) cites (without giving the passage) the following words (which are not found in the Babylonian Talmud), which read: כל עצים שהן נקצצים בו אינן עושין מאכולות, which he translates: “Wood felled on the 15th of July does not get worms.” Cf. Menachoth 85b and Bartenora on Schekalim 6, 2.

### ‎§. 411.

### ‎The Pea Beetle.

‎Bruchus pisi (Voigt l. c. 4. p. 361). It destroys peas and beans, in which it is found. In the Talmud (Chulin 67b) it is called [p. 308]: יחוש שבכליסים.

‎The first word also denotes elsewhere small winged insects, such as gnats (§. 432), and Rashi explains כליסים by: מין קטניה צירראש “a kind of pea, Ciceres.” This word means chickpeas; Mussaphia, who identifies כליסין with אכלסין, derives it from the Greek ʾΩχρος [Ōchros], which denotes a leguminous plant with a pale yellow pod; cf. also §. 420.

### ‎§. 412.

### ‎The Black Grain Weevil.

‎Curculio granarius (Voigt ibid. p. 348). The beetle gnaws into the husk of the grains of corn and lays its egg in it, whereupon the larva consumes all the flour of the grain, so that only the empty husks remain. These two insects, beetle and grub, are named in a proverb. To express the idea that evil produces only evil, the Midrash (Rabba Schir-ha-Schirim fol. 1, col. 3) has the proverb: מה ילדת חיפושיתה קרוצי בישי מינה “What does the beetle bear? The קרוצי, which is much worse than it.” Matnat Kehuna wrongly translates חיפושיתה as scorpion (שקרפיון); the word rather means beetle (§. 408), and קרוצי, from קרץ, means “destroyer,” and indeed the larva does much more damage than the beetle itself*).

### ‎§. 413.

### ‎The Mealworm.

‎Para 9, 2 speaks of two insects, דירה and כינה, which have fallen into the water mixed with the ashes of the red heifer. The water is still usable in that case, because those insects are (apparently) juiceless and thus cannot add any animal moisture to the water. Maimuni explains: העש והתולעת אשר יולדו בקמח “the moth and the worm that arise in flour.” These are the larvae and the beetles emerging from them (Tenebrio molitor. Voigt [p. 309] l. c. 4. p. 393), which live in flour. דירה (dwelling of the beetle) is the larva, which lives in flour, bran and old bread; from it arises the כינה, a general name for small insects.

‎Simson identifies דירה with סלמנטין, but incorrectly; see §. 422.

‎On דראון, which according to Kimchi is supposed to be identical with דירה, see §. 517.

### ‎§. 414.

‎Conclusion. In the Mishnah cited in the preceding article, Simson, following the Tosiphta, mentions some names of animals, and the issue there is the ritual ruling for the case in which the עכשוב, the כובאי and the שיבאי have fallen into the water mixed with the ashes of the red heifer. That only insects are meant here may well be inferred from their being grouped with the other insects mentioned in the previous article, and so עכשוב, according to Rashi's explanation on Ps. [=Psalms] 140, 4, is probably a kind of spider, namely the tarantula (cf. Kerem Chemed part 9, p. 124); כובאי are spiders (see §. 400), and שיבאי may correspond either to the German Schabe (cockroach/moth), or, which is more probable, to the butterfly name Asopia, namely Asopia farinalis, whose caterpillar lives chiefly in flour, hair powder and the like (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 200).

### ‎§. 415. The Vine Weevil.

‎Chulin 67b states that the worms on the trunk of the vines are to be regarded as forbidden insects; the worm is called: תולעת שבעיקרי גפנים “worm on the trunk of the vine;” this is Rhynchites Bacchus (Voigt ibid. 4. p. 358). It lays its egg in the buds and flower buds of the vine. The larva then stays in its rolled-up leaves and causes all of them to fall off. This insect thus does not touch the grape itself, and therefore stays בעיקרי גפנים, only on the trunk of the vine.

### ‎§. 416. A Related Genus.

‎Chulin 67b states that the worm on the trunk of the olive tree is regarded as a forbidden insect; it is called: תולעת [p. 310] שבעיקרי זחים “worm on the trunk of the olive trees.”

‎This insect too, like the vine weevil (§. 415), will damage not the berry itself but only the leaves or the blossoms of the olive tree. I am not able to identify it more precisely.

### ‎§. 417.

### ‎The Grape Beetle.

‎The worm that eats into the grape while it is still on the vine is called איילא דעינבא “maggot of the grape” (Sabbat 90a). Εὐλή [eulē] means maggot, and perhaps it is the grape beetle, French Staphylin.

‎Nasir 34b also speaks of grapes that have become wormy. Cf. §. 440.

### ‎§. 418.

### ‎The Date Borer.

‎Since the worm does not live twelve months, the worm found in dates that were picked 12 months earlier

‎תולעת שבתמרים

‎must have got into them only later (Chulin 58b), and this worm easily gets into them if the dates are not first properly pressed (M. Katan [=Moed Katan] 10b); cf. p. 26, note *.

### ‎§. 419

### ‎The Fig Borer.

‎The worm found in the flesh of the fig is called תולעת שבגרוגרות (Chulin 67b); B. batra 19b also speaks of figs that have become wormy. I cannot determine the insect more precisely.

‎One tenth of the figs are spoiled by the worm (B. batra 6, 2 Mishnah).

### ‎§. 420.

### ‎The Lentil Worm.

‎The Talmud (Chulin 67b) calls the worms found in lentils וזין שבערשים, which Rashi explains by כוצנש, Cossons, a word which, however, designates all kinds of grain worms.

‎Landau derives וזין from זין “motion”; he was probably thinking of the quickly moving mites *).

### ‎[p. 311] §. 421.

‎Conclusion. Chulin 13a says: One spreads produce on the (at that time flat) roof so that no כנימה gets at it; here too Rashi says: These are the כוצנס, Cossons, which are found in beans and peas; accordingly the insect would be identical with the pea beetle (§. 411).

‎At Sanhedrin 65b it is said that peas that are plucked off or pulled up (not cut off) are spared by the worms.

### ‎§. 422.

### ‎The Grain Moth.

‎In B. batra 91b the words “Ye shall eat of the old grain” (Lev. 25, 22) are explained to mean that the worm סלמנטון will not get at the old grain; this name is explained more closely by רצינתא.

‎R. Hai Gaon (on Para 9, 2) does not take סלמנטון as the name of an animal, but as the designation of grain that has become damp and rotten (similar to קריא, §. 425).

‎Simson (ibid.) identifies סלמנטון with דירה; but incorrectly, since the former contains much moisture (see below), whereas the דירה (§. 413) is said to be regarded as juiceless.

‎Sachs (Beiträge zur Sprach- und Alterthumsforschung, 2nd issue, p. 23) derives סלמנטון from Ἕλμινς [helmins]; but this designates especially the intestinal worms (helminths).

‎It is the white grain worm (Tinea granella), whose caterpillar destroys grain; it appears in heaped-up grain, whose kernels it hollows out (Voigt l. c. 5, p. 208). It is also spoken of in Menachoth 85b, where it is said that wheat is attacked by the worm. The grain worm exudes a moisture with which it binds together the clumps of hollowed-out kernels.

### ‎§. 423.

### ‎The Pomegranate Worm.

‎Sanhedrin 108b relates that Noah was at a loss, since he did not know what the chameleon (§. 275) fed on; then one day he cut open a pomegranate, out of which fell a worm (חולעתא), which that animal immediately caught and devoured. [p. 312]

‎This is the maggot that develops from the egg which the beetle lays in the fruit. The latter is the vine weevil (§. 415), which is also destructive to apple trees. Within a quarter of an hour it bores a hole in an apple, then pushes the egg into it by means of its snout, and thereupon closes the opening with a sticky substance. After a few days a little white worm with a black head emerges from the egg, which then burrows ever deeper, as far as the core. This worm is meant in our passage.

‎This worm is probably identical with הה דרימוני (Sabbat 90b), an insect that is dangerous to swallow (ibid.). We treat the worm in this place because it derives from a beetle, whereas in the following animal class we shall treat only those worms that arise either from worms or by Generatio originaria; cf. §. 468.

### ‎§. 424.

### ‎The Flour Mite.

‎Menachoth 85 b speaks of flour that has become wormy; here the reference may be either to the mealworm (§. 413) or to the flour mite (Acarus farinae); the latter often arises in countless numbers, especially in old flour (Voigt l. c. 4, p. 183). Among the decisors the mites are called מילווין, see §. 510.

### ‎§. 425.

‎Conclusion. Sota 3b says: Adultery is like the קריא לשומשמא. Rashi explains: “the worm for sesame”; Mussaphia and Bondi take קריא as caries, “rot”, an interpretation which is probably also more fitting for the image, for sesame in the course of time becomes dirty and mouldy (Bondi: Or Ester p. 266), and such a dismal end does adultery come to.

‎It may be noted, moreover, that the expression התליע is also used of seed that is in the ground and has passed into natural rot (Kilajim 2, 3).

### ‎§. 426.

### ‎The House Fly.

‎זבוב (Nidda 25a), דורבא (Berachoth 44a), pl. דירבי (Nidda 17a), and דידבתא (Gittin 86b). Of blackish colour (Rashi [p. 313] on Sabbat 76b). It arouses disgust (since it settles on dirty things and then flies onto edible ones) (Gittin 6b); yet when it has fallen into a glass, one throws it out and drinks the contents of the glass (Megilla 13b). It can hold on with its feet even to the ceilings of rooms, which is why it was described as a wonder that a man's forehead was so fat and smooth that even a fly slipped off it (Berachoth 44a). It comes flying up during meals and thereby becomes particularly annoying to man (ibid. 10b), and since, though often chased away, it nevertheless always returns, the evil inclination was aptly compared with it (ibid. 61a).

‎At times the flies constitute such a plague that public prayers were ordered for their extermination (Tanit 14a). They also have the dangerous property of settling on sick people and then flying to healthy ones, whereby they can become a means of contagion (Ketuboth 77. b).

‎It lives (as a boneless animal) for less than a year (Chulin 58b).

‎Its usefulness consists only in this, that, crushed, it is laid upon the wound of a hornet's sting (Sabbat 77b). On the catching of flies by monkeys, see §. 401.

‎On זבזין, see p. 311, note.

### ‎§. 427.

### ‎The Egyptian Fly.

‎זבוב שבארץ מצרים (Sabbat 121b), the poisonous fly living in Egypt, which is so dangerous that one may kill it even on the Sabbath (ibid., and on this p. 305). Some years ago I read in a journal that a general of the French army in Africa sent to the Paris Academy specimens of a poisonous fly, of which terrible insects 4 or 5 individuals would kill an ox. Fortunately this harmful insect is confined to a small area.

‎Meanwhile, the words of Winer (R.-W. s.v. Ungeziefer) may find a place here. They run: Rüppel, Arabic 73, mentions a small fly produced by great heat in the Nile valley, which falls in swarms upon people and animals, creeps into noses, ears and eyes, and causes acute pain. But this traveler has given no description of it.

### ‎[p. 314] §. 428.

### ‎The Blowfly.

‎It likes to frequent meat stalls, which is why it is regarded as a miracle that it was never seen in the Temple at Jerusalem (Aboth 5, 5). This, however, can be explained by the strong aromatic fragrance of the incense. According to Maimuni (More Nebuchim 3, 46), the burning of the prescribed incense with its pungent ingredients drove the foul odors and vapors away from the vicinity of the Temple, and so the blowfly could easily be kept away.

‎Interesting is the passage in Pliny (h. n. 10, 28). The Cyrenaeans prayed to the god Achoren on account of the pestilence-spreading multitude of flies that came while one was sacrificing to that god*).

‎In the Midr. (Jalk. Schim. part 2, fol. 14a) too it says: God asked the sons of Eli: “Why do you consume the remainder of the meal offering and leave the altar portion to the flies?”, in which, however, one is not to think of the blowfly, since it settles only on meat.

### ‎§. 429.

### ‎The Cellar Fly.

‎The Musca cellaris, or also vinegar fly (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 292), is mentioned in the following passage. In Chulin 67a it is called יבחושין; Raschi explains: כמין יתושים דקין הנמצאות במרתפות של יין “a kind of thin gnats which are found in wine cellars.”

‎It is found on the casks in wine cellars.

‎Raschi (Lev. 11, 11) explains יבחושין by מושיילונס, Moucherons, and Raschi mentions our fly once more on Sebachim 22a, to which we shall return again in §. 461.

‎If the fly is found in a beverage, it may be consumed together with it, since it is regarded as having originated from the beverage (Chulin ibid.).

### ‎§. 430.

### ‎A Large Species of Fly.

‎In Gittin 86b it is said that the insect קרצית, which is found between sheaves, does not render the water of purification (the water mixed with the ashes [p. 315] of the red heifer and intended for sprinkling) unfit, for when it drinks of it, it sucks the water in and does not let it fall back.

‎The said word is explained more precisely by דידבתא דביני כיפי “fly between the כיפי”; כיפי are (according to Mussaphia) rocks. Raschi explains: “The קרצית is a large fly the size of a locust and stays between sheaves.”

‎It is larger than an olive, since a second kind, of the size of the latter, is mentioned there as a smaller קרצית (§. 431).

‎Should one perhaps think here of the antlion? One may read up on the natural history of this insect; cf. the note below.

### ‎§. 431.

### ‎Related Kind.

‎In the Talmud passage cited in the preceding article, a smaller קרצית is also mentioned; it is the size of an olive and, when drinking, lets the water flow back. It is difficult to determine the species with certainty; for the largest fly known to us, the spring fly (Phryganea grandis, also Caddice, perhaps קרצית?), which grows to over an inch long, lives in brooks and waters.

‎The name קרצית carries the general sense of “to destroy” (cf. §. 412), which, however, does not substantially advance the identification of the insect; both insects probably belong chiefly to the Orient.

### ‎§. 432.

### ‎The Gnat.

‎יחוש (Sabbat 77b), פרוחי (Nidda 17a) and בקין (Succa 26a). In the last passage Raschi explains the word by צינצלא, Italian Zenzara.

‎The Talmud calls the gnat בריה קלה “light creature,” since it is said to have a mouth opening but no anal opening (Gittin 56b*).

‎[p. 316] It is the weakest creature and yet a terror to the elephant, since it flies into the opening of its trunk and stings it painfully there (Sabbat 77b). It is also dangerous to the eyes and nose of people because of its countless numbers, which is why public prayers were ordered for its extermination (Tanit 14a). In great numbers they are capable of tormenting a bound, defenseless person to death (Sanhedrin 77a). They penetrate into the room through the smallest opening, especially at night (Nidda 17a), which is why it is permitted to sleep in the sukkah in a curtained bed (Succa 26a*).

‎The gnats, although such insignificant creatures, were nevertheless created before man, so that he should not exalt himself in pride (Sanhedrin 38a); but precisely because of their insignificance, whoever eats them is an apostate from the Law, since he evidently ate the gnat not out of craving for pleasure but out of defiant wantonness (Horajoth 11a). On the fable of Titus's gnat see Gittin 56b, and on another fabulous statement of the Midrash about the gnat §. 519.

### ‎§. 433.

### ‎The Ethiopian gnat.

‎Sabbat 77b it is said that the lion is afraid of the creature מפגיע. Rashi explains: מפגיע is a small animal whose loud voice the lion fears because he believes it might be a large, strong animal.

‎But what מפגיע itself is, Rashi does not explain. Bochart explains it as the Ethiopian gnat, which stings the eyes and the part of the lion's face bare of mane so painfully that the lion abandoned the whole region on account of this troublesome insect; he therefore fears even the buzzing of this gnat (Bochart: Hieroz. 2, 4, 17, col. 567 and ibid. 5, 18, col. 792).

### ‎§. 434.

‎Conclusion. The second part of Rashi's explanation (§. 433), however, rests on a truth of natural history; for the lion does indeed fear the thos (Θῶς [Thōs]; Arist. [=Aristotle] loc. cit. 9, 2, 11 and 9, 31, 4). According to Oppian (Hunting with Dogs 3, 336) the thos is the hybrid of the wolf and the female panther; it therefore cannot be [p. 317] as large as, or even larger than, the lion; more recent naturalists, on the other hand, would recognize in the thos the ounce (Felis onzo), cf. Mellin de Grandmaison in the „Journal de Physique (December 1787, num. 5); this is the jaguar, which is indeed of the size and strength of the lion (Voigt l. c. 1. p. 271). Its ferocity and dangerousness are well known.

### ‎§. 435.

### ‎The cattle horsefly.

‎Cattle were covered with a blanket to protect them from the זבובין (Para 2, 3), and nature gave them a long tail so that they could drive off the בקי; by these the cattle in the meadows are greatly tormented (Sabbat 77b). This is the Tabanus bovinus (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 258). Even now carriage horses are protected with nets against that insect, which stings cattle and horses dreadfully.

‎בקי is Arabic and denotes any bloodsucking insect (§. 432 and below p. 319).

### ‎§. 436.

### ‎The sheep botfly.

‎The female lays its eggs inside the nose of the sheep, which become restless when this happens. At the time of the strongest development of these insects, in spring and summer, they cause the staggers in sheep (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 276). The Talmud calls these botflies דרני ראשה „worms of the head“, i.e. in the head of the sheep, for the hatched larvae crawl upward into the frontal sinuses. The rams butt one another with their horns, whereby the larvae fall out; to the ewes one tied little chips of juniper (ריהמא; according to Rosenmüller loc. cit. 4, 2. p. 120: Ginther) before the nose, whereby they sneeze and expel those larvae (Sabbat 54b).

‎דרן is a collective name for several kinds of insects; see §§. 410, 437 and 444. R. Hai Gaon (Para 9, 2) identifies דרן with דירה (§. 413); on ריהמא and חנון see also above p. 120.

### ‎§. 437.

### ‎The cattle botfly.

‎Oestrus bovis. The larva of this genus lives under the skin of cattle and other animals, especially younger ones, no doubt [p. 318] because their hide is more easily pierced and they themselves are juicier (Voigt 5. p. 277). The Talmud calls these insects: דרני דבישרא „worms of the flesh“, which Rashi explains thus: תולעים הנמצאים בין עור לבשר כשמפשיטין הבהמה ובלע"ז גרבילו"ש „worms which are found between the skin and the flesh of flayed cattle and are called גרבלי"ש in the foreign language.“

‎On דרן see §. 436; the foreign word in Rashi is not known to me; in English our botfly is called Gad-Fly, which may perhaps facilitate identifying the Old French word.

### ‎§. 438.

### ‎The mayfly.

‎The only Talmudic passage (Chulin 58b) that deals with the mayfly must, to be understood, be quoted literally and in full. It reads: אמר רב לית בקא בר יומא ולית דירבא בר שתא א"ל רב פפא לאביי והא אמרי אינשי שב שני אימרא בקחא [מבקא דאמרה לית חזיתיה לבר מחוזא דסחה במיא וסלוק ואיכרך בסדינין ואותיבה עליה ומצית מיניה ולא הודעת לי] א"ל וליטעמך הא דאמרי אינשי שיתין מני דפרזלא תלו לית לבקא בקורנסיה מי איכא איהו גופיה כמה הוי אלא במני דידהו הכי נמי בשני דידהו. According to Rashi the passage must be translated thus: „Rab said: „The Baka does not live a day, the fly not a year.““

‎Thereupon R. Papa said to Abaja: „But people relate that the female

‎Bakta

‎bore a grudge against the male

‎Baka

‎for seven years, for she reproached him: „You saw a man from Mechusa go down into the water and come out again; then you settled on him, sucked blood from him, and gave me no word of it.“

‎Abaja replied: „Have you not likewise heard the proverb: 60 pounds of iron were hung on the Baka's hammer (sting)? Is this possible? How big, then, is the Baka itself?

‎But just as the weight here is expressed figuratively, in proportion, so too is it the case with the years.““

‎Rashi misunderstood the word אימרא; he takes it in the sense of חמריא (Job 39, 10) and renders it as to resist; he says: לשון כעת חמריא נתנכרה לו ומרדה עליו אינשלבייר „similar to the biblical חמריא; the female acted as a stranger toward the male and was refractory toward him, the same as insoulever.“

‎But above all, the biblical חמריא is taken by most commentators (Ralbag [=Rabbi Levi ben Gershom (Gersonides, 1288–1344)], Ibn Ezra; see also the Aramaic paraphrase) in the sense of מרום "to [p. 319] rise up"; in that case, however, רום is construed with a following ב, and it would then have to read אימרא בבקא. Rashi takes חמריא in the sense of מרה "to be rebellious"; then it would have to be construed either with a simple accusative, אימרא בקתא בקא (after Deut. [=Deuteronomy] 1, 26; 1 B. Kön. [=Kings (German Könige)] 11, 27), or with עם, i.e. עם בקא; but מרה is never construed with מ; Aruch in fact has לבקא, which, however, is likewise linguistically incompatible with both of the meanings of חמריא mentioned.

‎What fully demonstrates the incorrectness of Rashi's explanation, however, is the form אימרא, for which אימרת or אימרית (as 3rd person fem. Itpaal) would have to stand.

‎I therefore believe I am not mistaken in supplying, before the parenthetical clause indicated by brackets, the word נפקת (she went away) or סליקת (she departed), and in taking אימרא בקתא for Ἡμέρα [Hēmera] Bacta, i.e. Musca ephemera. The Greek word is also found in the Jerusalem Talmud (A. Sara c. 1) in מילני אימירא "black day." —

‎As for בקתא, Rashi explains it by מין זבוב צינצא "a kind of fly צינצא"; similarly, on Succa 26a, he translates בקי by צינצלש.

‎Buxdorf (Lex. p. 342) takes the foreign word for the French chenille and refers to Pliny (h. n. 30, 16, 53), where it says: In culicum genere muliones non amplius, quam uno die vivere "among the gnat kind the muliones live only one day"; but apart from the fact that chenille means caterpillar and never gnat, mulio in Pliny is also that biting fly which chiefly torments mules (mulus, whence mulio; see Harduin on Plin. h. n. [=Pliny the Elder, Natural History (Naturalis historia)] 11, 18, 19). That foreign word, however, is the Italian zenzara or zanzara (§. 432), the generic name for flies; likewise בקא, Arabic البق [al-baqq], is a generic name for many blood-sucking insects, which is why the gnat (§. 432), the cattle gadfly (§. 435) and even the bedbug (§. 453) are so called.

‎If we now leave aside for the moment the clause enclosed in brackets, as the essential part of the fable*), the remaining parts of our passage have the following sense: Rab said: "The fly Baka does not live a day, and every other fly does not live a year"**). Against this Papa sets the little story of the seven-year separation [p. 320] of the female from the male of the mayfly, whereupon Abaye refers to that proverb: "60 pounds *) of iron are hung on the sting of the mayfly"; in both tales, Abaye thinks, only a hyperbolic manner of speech is to be assumed; the lifespan (years) and the intensity of the severe (pounds) wound inflicted by the fly are here conceived on a reduced scale but expressed on an enlarged scale. The seven parts of the one-day life are called years; the sixtyfold felt pain of the wound weighs by the pound; the numbers 7 and 60 are, as is well known, standing designations of indefinite numerical proportions**).

‎That little story, too, contains a natural-historical truth, for these wonderful, delicate and soft insects keep to damp places, especially along large rivers, where they come out of the water in many millions and settle on people in such quantity that one can hardly open one's mouth (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 310). At the beginning of August they appear in especially enormous numbers; this, then, is the proper bathing season, which is alluded to in our passage.

‎Incidentally, there are several species of this insect, so that the one mentioned in the Talmud cannot be determined with complete certainty.

‎On the geographical location of מחוזא cf. also article Toad.

### ‎§. 439.

### ‎The Book Louse.

‎Psocus pulsatorius, or book louse, which is found extremely often in old papers, books, natural history collections and the like, where it probably gnaws through various things (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 321). The Talmud calls it מקק דספרי "gnawer of books" (Sabbat 90a). מקק from נמק "to spoil," "to gnaw through."

‎In the Mishnah ibid. it is called מקק ספרים ומקק מטפחותיהם "gnawer of books and gnawer of their wrappers," by which one and the same insect is meant.

‎Rashi translates it by worm; but the book louse is sometimes winged and belongs to the Neuroptera.

‎To swallow it is life-threatening (Sabbat ibid.).

### ‎[p. 321] §. 440.

‎Conclusion. In the Talmud passage cited in the preceding article four insects are mentioned; they are called מקק ספרים (book louse, §. 439), חכך דשיראי (silk moth, §. 441), פח דתאנה (fig gall wasp, §. 407) and אילא דעינבא (grape beetle, §. 417); compare with this the passage in the מדרש לעולם (by R. Israel al-Nakwa; ed. Jellineck, Leipzig 1855) Abh. 20, which reads: לעולם יזהר אדם ממקק סופרים (פירוש אבק שמשימין הסופרים על כתיבתן כדי שלא תמחק) ,מסכך דשיארי (פירוש הטט שעל המלח) ומפח דתאיני (פי' עיפוש התאנים) מאילא דעינבי (פירוש כמו עכבוש שעל הענבים) מפני שרברים אלה מזיקין ואף כסם המות. According to this, instead of the insects other harmful things would be meant, namely blotting sand, gypsum clinging to salt, the mould on figs and the cobweb on the grapes; in any case these so greatly divergent variations are not without interest.

### ‎§. 441.

### ‎The Silk Moth.

‎The silk moth is called חכך דשיראי "gnawer of silk" (Sabbat 90a). Aruch explains: a worm which arises from silk kept shut away for a long time.

‎At Berachoth 56a it is called תכלא "destroyer"; in both passages silk is spoken of, for שיראי is the fine Seric silk. Cf. §. 509.

### ‎§. 442.

### ‎The Fur Moth.

‎B. mez. 78b relates that someone made the report that a moth had got into the king's מילתא. Rashi explains the word by שיראים (Seric silk, §. 441), but incorrectly, for מילתא is the Greek Μηλωτή [mēlōtē], sheepskin, which is so called because it was made from the fine wool of the Milesian sheep (§. 154). The moth is called אבזקת and is Tinea pellionella; it has grey forewings, on which there is a black spot, and a greyish head (Voigt l. c. 5, p. 209).

### ‎[p. 322] §. 443.

### ‎The Clothes Moth.

‎Tinea sarcitella. יניבא (Chulin 28a; Rashi: a worm that grows in woollen garments and is called טיניא, Teigne) and יאניבא, for which יאניבא and יניבא are also written (Chulin 85b; Rashi: a worm that destroys flax and is called טיישא (should read טינשא, Teignes).

‎This moth is driven away by the blood of animals and birds (ibid. 28a). It does not get into linen garments (B. mez. 78b), but it does get into unworked flax, which is why one pours birds' blood into the retting water in which the flax lies, whereupon the moths, which cannot bear the smell of blood, depart (Chulin 85b).

‎Linen eaten by moths is called בליתא (blatta) (Sabbat 34a).

### ‎§. 444.

### ‎The Tapestry Moth.

‎Pyralis tapezana; it is called דרנא, which gets into a tapestry (יריעה) (Sabbat 75a). Rashi explains: a worm that bites a small, round hole into tapestries.

‎This moth is found especially often in carriages lined with cloth, or in neglected wardrobe stores (Voigt l. c. 5, p. 210).

‎On דרנא see §. 436.

### ‎§. 445.

### ‎The Many-footed One.

‎נדל (Chulin 67b), on which Rashi: מאה רגלים קורין לו "It (this isopod) is called: hundred-foot"; this designation corresponds to the otherwise customary one: thousand-foot, although the female has only 34 and the male 32 feet (Voigt l. c. 5, p. 418). The glossary to Jalkut Schimoni part 1, p. 150, col. 1 calls the insect: צינטי פינדיש, Cent pieds.

‎On the other hand, Rashi on Erubin 8b explains it in general terms: שרץ שיש לו רגלים הרבה "An insect that has many feet."

### ‎§. 446.

‎(Appendix). Sachs (Beiträge zur Sprach- und Alterthumsforschung no. 2, p. 93) cites a passage from the Jerusalem Talmud which reads: שיורתא דנונא מהעביד נדל "The backbone of the [p. 323] fish becomes a נדל", where surely only the many-footed one is meant, since the fishbones running downward on both sides of the backbone bear some resemblance to the feet of the isopod, divided into two rows.

‎Sachs, however, explains the Talmudic יילי (which means hedgehog, cf. §. 134) by the Greek Ἰουλος [ioulos], millipede, and therefore cannot take נדל as the many-footed one; he considers it derived from Κνώδαλον [knōdalon] with loss of the K sound, as in the English Knight, Know, etc. But Sachs errs in both etymologies.

‎As regards יילי, the similarity of sound with Ἰουλος [ioulos] must not mislead, because otherwise the passage containing this word would become entirely senseless. B. batra 4a relates that a martyr was blinded by having a wreath of יילי bound around his eyes; it says there literally: אהדר ליה כלילא דיילי נקרינהו לעינים, i.e. the tyrant (Herod) bound on the martyr (Baba b. Buta) a wreath of hedgehog skin with the spines turned inward, which put out his eyes. Sachs, who translates יילי as millipede, finds it strange that a crown of such יילי was

‎placed on that martyr; what is strange is only the hunt for similar-sounding words, to which one then willingly sacrifices the correct content of many Talmudic passages; יילי, following the precedent of Onkelos (Lev. 11, 30), is nothing other than hedgehog.

‎Equally artificial and incorrect is the derivation of נדל; the Greek Κνώδαλον [knōdalon] denotes something monstrous, wild, troublesome, from the wild mammal down to the gnat, whereas the Talmud places all weight on the property of having many feet; it identifies נדל with מרבה רגלים (Lev. 11, 42); it therefore cannot have called just any insect, including one provided with few feet, נדל.

### ‎§. 447.

### ‎A Kind of Isopod.

‎The Talmud (Chulin 67b) says that both the many-footed one (נדל, §. 445) and the insects resembling it are forbidden to be eaten. There are several kinds of isopods; perhaps the Talmud was thinking specifically of Scolopendra gigantea; this, or a related genus, is, according to A. v. Humboldt's assurance, pulled out of the muddy ground by the children of the Indians to be eaten alive (Voigt l. c. 5, p. 420). This genus has seventeen pairs of feet and is therefore forbidden to be eaten as מרבה רגלים (Lev. 11, 42).

‎The small Aruch translates נדל as "caterpillar"; but precisely the caterpillar, which has no real feet but small warts [p. 324] for locomotion, can all the less be included under מרבה רגלים.

### ‎§. 448.

### ‎The Whale Louse.

‎The whale louse, also called whale isopod (Cyamus Ceti, Voigt l. c. 4, p. 61), is called אכלא טונא in the Talmud. The first word means "to suck", which is the basic meaning of the frequently occurring מאכולת (louse, §. 449), and ט.נא means "passion" (Sanhedrin 75a), and so אכלא טונא means the passionate louse; it bites itself very firmly into the soft parts of the whale, especially at the axils of the fins, or near the genitals, and causes the animal a painful torment (Voigt l. c. and Beumer loc. cit. p. 350). Our insect is mentioned in the following fable: We once went by ship and saw a fish which an אכלא טונא

‎nostril; thereupon the fish was killed and cast by the water onto the shore; as a result 60 cities perished, another 60 cities ate of it, etc., etc. (B. batra 73b). The glossator and Bochart identify our insect with כילבית (Sabbat 77b); but we have shown earlier that in our passage the whale (§. 179) is meant, and that כילבית (§. 357) is the spiny fish. To be sure, the fable exaggerates the size of the whale just as it also errs in having the animal killed by the whale louse; that the latter can dwell in the blowhole of the whale is natural, or at least possible, since with its foot claws it finds a soft spot here too in which to fasten itself.

‎The whale has still other, but less tormenting parasites, e.g. the whale spider (Voigt l. c. 4. p. 170).

‎On the number 60 see p. 320 note **.

### ‎§. 449.

### ‎The Head Louse.

‎כינה (Sabbat 12a) and מאכולת (ibid.). Both are general names for the head louse and the body louse.

‎The former is specifically called מאכולת של ראש (Nidda 19b) and כלמי (ibid. 20b). In contrast to the flea, both kinds of lice are called כינה רוחשת "vermin that moves by walking" (Tos. Sabbat 12a s.v. שמא).

‎This loathsome vermin is one of the most tormenting enemies of man, which is why someone said to another: "Kill the louse [p. 325] and let me hear the crackling of this enemy of mine*)" (Sabbat 12a); otherwise it is regarded as improper to kill a louse in the presence of another, since this arouses disgust (Chagiga 5a). Lice do not reproduce through copulation, but arise through generatio originaria (§. 14), namely from the dirt of the human body (Sabbat 107b; on their origin from the nits see §. 451), which more recent naturalists also concede (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 409), and which is most clearly evident in the louse disease known by the name phthiriasis (§. 473).

‎The head louse has red blood (Nidda 19b). For cleaning the head one uses the comb (ibid. 20b). This vermin is found most frequently in Media (Kiduschin 49b).

‎As a punishment, harlots got lice of every kind (משפחות משפחות של כינים) on their heads (Midr. R. Echa fol. 79, col. d).

### ‎§. 450.

### ‎The Body Louse.

‎It shares the general names with the head louse (see §. 449), but is specifically called: מאכולת של גוף "louse of the body" (Tos. Sabbat 12a. s.v. שמא).

‎It has white blood (Nidda 19b). It is multiplied by ragged garments (Berachoth 51b), and since frequent changing of the shirt is an excellent means of cleanliness, one who owns only one shirt is regarded as pitiable (Beza 32 b**). If white lice (which is the colour of the body louse, Nidda 19b) settle in a shirt that has not been washed within eight days, they promote contagious leprosy in the one who wears the shirt (Pesachim 112b***).

‎Even from this [p. 326] loathsome insect people knew how to derive a medicinal use, for which we refer to Gittin 69b.

### ‎§. 451.

‎Conclusion. As regards the reproduction of lice, they arise, as mentioned (§ 449), through generatio originaria, according to Rashi (Sabbat 12a) from the human body itself. The question now arises how the pear-shaped eggs, the so-called nits, are to be explained? Since the nits are dead or alive, the Talmud considers them a vermin of their own, although they are called ביצי כינים "eggs of the lice" (Sabbat 107b). The Talmud thus seems to assume that the nits are indeed laid by the lice, but that these eggs do not arise through copulation, but are, so to speak, the excrements of the lice.

‎An analogy for this is formed by the fish genus of the plaice, which have roe but do not copulate (see §. 355).

‎The living nit is called: אינבא חיה, on which Rashi (Nasir 39a): מביצי כינים לינטריש בלע"ז דהוא "Lentes in a foreign language, from the eggs of the lice"; the dead nit is called: אינבא מתה.

‎The former is located at the base of the hairs and, although the hair grows, always sinks downward; the latter, however, is carried along by the growing hair and thus removed ever further from the surface of the head (ibid.).

‎The nit serves to denote smallness, as the extreme opposite of greatness; thus it is said: God nourishes the unicorn (§. 174) and the nit (A. Sara 3b). Further it is said: A man on the summit of Mount Ophel appears, seen from the valley, only as large as an אינבא, a nit (Tanit 22b*).

### ‎[p. 327] §. 452.

### ‎The Flea.

‎The flea is called פרעוש (Horajoth 11a) and, in contrast to the louse (§. 449): כינה קופצוח "jumping vermin" (Tos. Sabbat 12a. s.v. שמא). Here the flea is also called כינה שחורה, but here שחור does not mean black, but reddish-brown, since only the dog flea is black.

‎It never climbs up onto the human head (ibid.); on the other hand it harasses man with bites, also jumps about on his clothes and on the ground, and since it reproduces through copulation, it may, like any other animal, not be killed on the Sabbath (Sabbat 107b*). The art of training fleas to certain feats seems to have been known already early, cf. Sch. Aruch [=Shulchan Aruch] Jore Dea §. 179, 5).

### ‎§. 453.

### ‎The Bedbug.

‎פשפש (Terumoth 8, 2). Bartenoro explains: פשפש is an insect whose smell, which arises when one rolls it with the fingers, is very disgusting; it is found in beds and in walls; in Arabic it is called البق [al-baqq] and in another language צימיצא (Italian Cimica).

‎Maimuni explains: פשפש are round, red insects, in the shape of a lentil and very disgusting; in Arabic they are called Baki.

‎Finally Rashi explains the word by: פונייז, Punaise (Nidda 58b). In this passage it is said: The bedbugs are as long as they are broad, i.e., they are round.

‎We see from this that the bedbug known to the ancients nevertheless differed somewhat from ours, since the latter is sometimes oblong; ours is also rust-brown.

‎Taste and smell are alike and disgusting (Nidda ibid.), a property which the Talmud describes as a love-gift of nature to [p. 328] this insect*); however, many another insect also enjoys this gift, e.g. the water scorpion (Nepa cinerea, Voigt l. c. 5. p. 403), which likewise stinks like the bedbug**).

### ‎§. 454. The Ant.

‎נמלה (Chulin 63a) and שומשמנא (ibid. 57b); on קמצא, which Raschi takes indeterminately for ant and locust, see §. 455.

‎The winged ants are not mentioned in the Talmud, while it directly contrasts the ant, as a wingless insect, with the wasp, as a winged insect (Pesachim 24a†). Concerning the social and constitutional way of life of these animals the Talmud gives several pieces of information in the form of a fable. Since ants, it says there, love the shade more than sunlight, someone in the middle of summer spread a cover over an anthill, so that the shade fell upon it; at once a single ant came out and, after it had convinced itself of the presence of the shade and after it had been marked by the observer in some way (perhaps by sprinkling with a white paint), returned into the [p. 329] heap, whereupon the ants immediately came out en masse. Now the cover was lifted, and the disappointed ants fell upon the supposed traitress and killed her. From this the Talmud claims to recognize the correctness of Solomon's word (Spr. Sal. [=Proverbs (German Sprüche Salomo's)] 6, 7) that the ants have no ruler, for otherwise in that incident the ants would not have killed the traitress without permission (Chulin 57b). If we strip this embellished account of its figurative part, the following natural-historical points emerge: ants love shady places; they live in an ordered constitution, but without a head, which is indeed entirely correct. Ants like to live in wooded places or places otherwise shaded by trees; they have, as Aristotle (l. c. 1, 1, 11) expressly says, no ruler over them as the bees do, but merely carry on their business in common and live in an ordered constitution.

‎In gathering provisions none robs another, which is why the ant is held up to man as a model of honesty (Erubin 100b**). Its usefulness in medical respects (Jebamoth 76a and on this §. 456; cf. also §. 392) is amply outweighed by the damage it causes. It bites off the stalks close to the ground and then carries off the grain (Menachoth 71b and Peah 2, 7); its harmfulness is so great that the destruction of anthills is permitted even on the intermediate days of the festival (M. Katan 6b). Their extermination is brought about by fetching another anthill from a distance of more than a mile or so and mixing the two heaps together, whereby a battle arises between the ants in which they kill one another. It is noted that at a distance smaller than the one mentioned the desired battle is not brought about, since in that case the ants know each other and leave one another unharmed (ibid.). That ants formally march out to battle against other anthills (of another [p. 330] species), fight battles, and after victory carry off the defeated ants or their pupae, is confirmed by Latreille (Histoire naturelle des fourmis, Paris 1812).

‎The nest or dwelling of the ants is called קינא דשומשמני (Nedarim 24b), or חור נמלים (M. Katan 6b). The sight of this wondrous insect, and in particular its wondrous way of life, drew forth the exclamation: “Thy righteousness, O God, towers on high!” (Chulin 63a).

### ‎§. 455.

‎Conclusion. The קמצי occurring in Sabbat 77b and Jebamoth 121b is rendered by Raschi indeterminately as locust or ant. The basic notion of קמץ, “to grasp,” fits both insects; yet in the latter passage the locust (cf. §. 392) is rather meant, in the former passage probably rather the ant. It says here: why are the feelers of the קמצא soft? Because it dwells among hard objects (חילפי willows, then dry objects in general, such as pine needles etc.), whereby the feelers, if they were hard, could easily break off, and the קמצא would thereby be as it were blinded, for Samuel used to say: Whoever wishes to blind the קמצא, let him tear off its feelers.

‎The feelers of the ant are, as is well known, strongly elbowed, which the Talmud describes as soft. Moreover, the sterile ones differ in form from the male and the female in having a larger triangular head but much smaller eyes; their ocelli are so small that one can hardly perceive them, and the eyes are set more to the side; for this species of ants the feelers are therefore all the more necessary, which is why I conjecture that קמצא designates the sterile ones κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν [kat᾽ exochēn]. Their dwelling among hard objects means nothing other than their nest, which consists of little chips, grass stalks, wood, pine needles and the like.

### ‎§. 456.

### ‎The Large Ant.

‎The spelling of one of the names of the ant varies: Nedarim 24b; Ketuboth 75a. has שומשמנא, whereas Jebamoth 76a has שומשנא; in other editions of the Talmud (only the Sulzbach edition is available [p. 331] to me) שומשמנא*) is written, which Sachs (Beiträge ⁊c. 2nd issue p. 22) derives from שמש, “because it preferentially seeks to stay in the sun”; this is incorrect, since the Talmud itself (see §. 454) emphasizes the ants' staying in the shade.

‎Perhaps שומשמנא designates in particular the species of workers among the ants, from שמש to serve. —

‎Whereas in Ketuboth 75a שומשמנא serves to designate some small object, as it says: A woman is glad to have a husband, even if he be only as large as a שומשמנא, Jebamoth 76a mentions a שומשנא נמלא "large ant." (On נמלא see §. 409). A surgical cure is prescribed here, according to which one lets the ant bite into the person's flesh; then one tears off the body of the ant, so that the head remains in the flesh and fills the hole that was previously at the spot.

‎Of the large red wood ant it is known that its torn-off head continues to bite (Voigt l. c. 4. p. 297); but the large black carpenter ant as well (called Formica herculeana because of its size; it grows up to eight lines long) is very given to biting and hollows out the thickest trees; the latter ant seems to be the שומשנא נמלא; it is also used (as a magical cure) against fever (Sabbat 66b).

### ‎§. 457.

### ‎The White Ant.

‎This species too, from the 5th order of insects, well known under the name termite, was perhaps*) known to the Talmud. Berachoth 54b it says in the legend: Og, king of Bashan, wanted to destroy the Israelite camp in the wilderness at one stroke; [p. 332] he therefore took a mountain three parasangs in circumference upon his head and wanted to throw it upon the camp; but God had it perforated by the קמצי, and the head of that giant thereby sank up to the neck into that loosened mass of earth.

‎Salomo b. Abderet translates the word as locust and attaches an allegorical interpretation to it; Pinner thoughtlessly copies him; Rashi, however, recognized the correct meaning of the word and translates it as ant, and we have here to think of Termes destructor, which destroys everything in a short time (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 320), since with its horny, strong jaws it hollows out everything except metals, stones and arsenic, and thus becomes the most terrible plague in the hot countries.

‎Admittedly, the last-mentioned circumstance, that termites leave stones untouched, raises a difficulty insofar as that Talmudic passage speaks of a stone (טינר) which Og wanted to throw upon the Israelites (see Sch. Aruch Or. Chajim [=Orach Chayyim (part of the Shulchan Aruch)] §. 218, 1*); one must therefore, if the natural-historical aspect of this legendary passage is not to be abandoned, think of a rocky mountain formed, in the manner of strata, of stone and earth material, so that that mountain was not hollowed out by the termites to the point of crumbling into sand, but only, as the Talmud precisely states, acquired cracks and cavities through the separation of the earthy parts, into which the head of that giant sank.

### ‎§. 458.

### ‎The Cabbage Caterpillar.

‎The well-known remarkable metamorphosis of caterpillars into pupae and of these into butterflies is not mentioned in the Talmud, but some kinds of caterpillars are indeed mentioned which are permitted or forbidden to be eaten, and it is known that the ancients roasted and ate some caterpillars, e.g. the willow caterpillar Cossus ligniperda etc. A caterpillar forbidden to be eaten is the ביניחא דבי כרבא (Maccoth 16b). The Tosephot understand by it a small fish which is found in the field and therefore, although permitted to be eaten in the water, [p. 333] is nevertheless regarded in the case mentioned as an unclean animal.

‎The only reason why the Tosephot depart from the explanation of Rashi, who explains ביניתא as caterpillar, is that in the case of a caterpillar the prohibition of eating is of course self-evident and therefore required no explicit mention, and Aruch agrees with this.

‎However, 1) it must surely appear most strange that a fish should be clean in the water and at the same time unclean on land; 2) ביניתא as a fish name can only be in the sense of בינהא or בנותא, the bonito (§. 322), but this grows to about an ell in length, and also does not go onto land of its own accord in the manner of some fish. Moreover, it is a sea fish, and how should it come onto ploughed fields, as Tosephot explain כרבא? Precisely near the sea, on shores exposed to flooding, such fields are surely found rarely, or not at all; finally 3), and this is the main objection, the mention of that prohibition was indeed needed, since the caterpillar is indeed not always forbidden to be eaten; thus the cucumber caterpillar (§. 459), as long as it is on the fruit, is permitted to be eaten.

‎Rashi's explanation is therefore to be preferred. כרבא is the Greek Κράμβη [krambē], on which see §. 394 in detail, and is the cabbage, on which many kinds of caterpillars are found*). Rashi explains ביניתא by: הולעה הנמצאה בכרוב שקורין ציילא "the worm which is found in cabbage and called ציילא"; the foreign word seems corrupted from צנילא**), chenille, and in our passage specifically the cabbage moth, Mamestra brassicae (Voigt l. c. 5. p. 164), is meant.

‎It may be noted that the leech (§. 467) is also called ביני.

### ‎§. 459.

### ‎The Cucumber Caterpillar.

‎Chulin 67b it is said that the worm which is on a cucumber still attached to the ground is forbidden to be eaten, since in this case it is called שרץ הארץ "land insect." If the cucumber has been cut off, the worm is forbidden only when it has crawled out of the fruit; as long as it remains in the cucumber, however, it is regarded as a creature arisen from its very substance, and accordingly considered clean. Thus in ritual respect one has, so to speak, [p. 334] to assume three periods. As long as the cucumber is attached to the ground, the caterpillar is forbidden to be eaten; once the cucumber is cut off, the caterpillar is permitted to be eaten; but it may again not be eaten if it crawls off the cucumber. On the application of this theory to the most varied cases, see the 84th section of the Sch. Ar. [=Shulchan Aruch] Jore Dea.

‎The fruit in our passage is called קישות, which Bartenoro (on Kilajim 1, 2) explains by קוקומברוש (Spanish Cohombros, Latin Cucumeres), cucumbers.
