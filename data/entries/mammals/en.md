# Second part, first class: Mammals (pp. 64–158)

### ‎Part Two. Special Zoology.

### ‎First Class. Mammals.

### ‎§. 111. A Species of Ape.

‎Kilajim 8, 5 states that the creature אדני שדה (Simson reads: אבני שדה) belongs to the animal kind (§. 19); R. Jose held that, like a human being, it defiles the tent after its death.

‎The older commentators make a fabulous animal of it, which is why we shall return to it once more in the last section of this work (§. 506). Here we treat the view of the more recent commentators. Already Beer Naphtali (in the מחנה כהונה on Rabba Kohelet fol. 99, col. 3) wavers between the natural-historical and the fabulous interpretation; he says: אדני שדה is either an animal that resembles man (i.e., the ape), or a man who grows out of the earth.

‎Landau (on the Aruch) asserts decidedly that אדני שדה is the chimpanzee (Pithecus Troglodytes; also called Jocko, or Barris), whereas Schwarz (תוצאות הארץ fol. 4, 2) explains the animal as the orang-utan (Pithecus Satyrus, also called Pongo, or man of the woods)*). One may waver between the two [p. 65] views, since telling considerations present themselves for each of them. In favour of the explanation as orang-utan speaks the explanation of the Jerusalem Talmud, which explains אדני שדה by בר נש דטור „man of the mountain“; indeed, the former inhabits the high, wooded mountains in the interior of Borneo, Cochin-China and Malacca, so that it is extremely difficult to catch (Voigt [=F. S. Voigt, Lehrbuch der Zoologie (Stuttgart 1835–40); the text names it (p. 9: "Voigt, Lehrb. d. Zool.")], Lehrb. der Zoologie 1, p. 243), whereas the Jocko lives in the forests of Guiana.

‎Furthermore, it is indeed the orang-utan and not the chimpanzee that is regarded by Linnaeus as, so to speak, a kind of man and is called Homo troglodytes.

‎On the other hand, the following speaks in favour of the assumption of the chimpanzee: The word ארני means either lord (from ארון, in the construct state אֲרֹנִי; it would be wrong to read אֲרֹנִי after the form of אבני, as Simson would have it), or man (as Bochart [=Samuel Bochart, Hierozoicon, sive de animalibus Sacrae Scripturae (Lewysohn used the Frankfurt a. M. 1675 edition)] thinks, holding that נ has been confused with ם; Hieroz. [=Samuel Bochart, Hierozoicon (Frankfurt a. M. 1675 edition)] P. 2, 4, 13, col. 846). The name is therefore also Gen. [=Genesis] masc., and for מטמאות (she defiles) one must read with Bartenoro: מטמא.

‎The notion of this name is

‎in any case, however, the resemblance to man, and indeed the Jocko is the most manlike, which is why it is also called the African man of the woods. It also has more intelligence than the Pongo. It builds itself huts out of brushwood against the heat of the sun; it breaks off pieces with which it beats men and elephants; it is even said to carry off Negro women and keep them in captivity for years, and it can be trained to be a skilful and obedient servant (Voigt l. c. [=loco citato: in the work cited above] 1, p. 240). Further, in favour of this view is the fact that the Talmudists very probably had more knowledge of African than of Further-Indian animals. Since, then, just as many reasons can be adduced for as against the one view and the other, the choice between the two must for the time being still remain to be determined.

‎The Midrash (l. c.) seems either not to have known of the training of this animal, and of apes in general, or at any rate not to have rated it very highly. With reference to the scriptural verse (Koh. [=Ecclesiastes (Kohelet)] 6, 11): „There are many things that increase vanity“, it says the following: For example, those who rear apes, cats, great weasels (§. 129), Adone Sadeh and seals—what enjoyment do they have of them?“ In the Jalkut Schimoni (part 2, fol. 186, 1) only apes and cats are mentioned, and in place of the other animals pigs are mentioned. In the Midrasch Jelamdenu (Pirschat Schelach [p. 66] lecha) only apes and dogs are cited.

‎The sense of all these Midrashim, however, is that all the animals mentioned are to be regarded as useless because they may not be eaten.

‎As for the second reading אבני שדה, the poet (Job. 5, 23) is said to have had our animal in mind when he says: „With the אבני שדה (stones of the field) you shall have a covenant, and with the beasts of the field peace.“ But what sense would the meaning of stones give here? Probably אבני here is meant to correspond to the Arabic ابناء [abnāʾ] (pl. of ابن [ibn], son); the א would then be regarded as an Aleph prostheticum, as in אצעדה, אזרוע etc., and the sense would be: „sons of the field“, which would mean as much as our „man of the woods“.

### ‎§. 112.

### ‎The Ape.

‎קופא (Nedarim 50b) and קוף (Bechoroth 8a). Greek Κῆβος [Kēbos] and Κεῖπος [Keipos]. Rashi on the second passage: שינייא, i.e., Singe. Maimuni (Kilajim 8, 6): *)נקרא בלשון ערב קדר „is called in Arabic: Kerdon“, and the same (Idaim 1, 5): הקוף בערב קדר והוא אשר קורין אותו במצרים אל נסנ"א בלשין ערב „The ape is in Arabic Kerdon; it is the one that in Egypt is called in Arabic al nisnun.“

‎Bartenoro (ibid.): חיה ששמה מונ"א „an animal that is called (in Spanish) Mono“.

‎Pl. קופין (B. Kama [=Bava Kamma] 80a) and קופות (probably a misprint) Midr. Rabba [=Midrash Rabbah] Kohelet fol. 99, 3, on which the מחנח כהונה: אפין, i.e. Affen.

‎The ape belongs to the genus of beasts (Kilajim 8, 6), which matters in religious-legal respects (§. 19). Outwardly it bears much resemblance to man, for which reason the benediction "Blessed be He who varies the creatures!" was ordained upon seeing it (Berachoth 58b). Its ugliness makes its appearance in a dream an evil omen (ibid. 57b). It is trained for various services, such as arranging and tidying utensils (Joma 29b) and pouring water over people's hands (Idaim 1, 5), and it was gladly kept for pastime and amusement (Tos. [=Tosafot] B. batra [=Bava Batra] 20a. s.v. כיון). All those performances, however, are not expressions of intelligence, [p. 67] but a purely mechanical activity, hence the expression מעשה קוף בעלמא הוא "a mere aping" (frequently). It is questionable whether it is mentioned in the following Talmud passage (B. Kama 80a); there it says: One raises spitz dogs, cats, קופין and large weasels to clear the house of rats and mice; that the ape is not a carnivorous animal is well known, and indeed Bochart (l. c. 1, 3, 36, col. 1036) had in the Talmud edition before him קופדים (see article Hedgehog) for קופין; however, perhaps in our passage one may think of cleaning the dwelling (לנקר את הבית) of flies (§. 426) and spiders (§. 401), since the ape eagerly catches and swallows these insects (Voigt, l. c. 1. p. 238).

‎It readily seeks the opportunity to slip away, whereupon it hides in caves (Nedarim 50b). Its gestation period is (though erroneously, cf. §. 17) three years (Bechoroth 8a).

### ‎§. 113.

### ‎The Guenon.

‎קיפוף (Bechoroth 8a and Berachoth 58b) and קיפוד (ibid. 57b). In the latter passage, however, Aruch and Tosephot (on Chulin 63a s.v. באות) already read, probably more correctly, קיפוף; the Greek Κερκοπίθηκος [kerkopithēkos] does not suffice to justify the reading קיפוד, although Rashi (Berachoth ibid.) did indeed have it. The name קיפוף is either a reduplication of קוף, to distinguish the guenon, which belongs to the ape genus, from the ape, or a designation of the long, curling tail of the guenon; cf. the Arabic قَفَّ [qaffa], nectere, to entwine. In any case the word in the three first-mentioned Talmud passages, where it is mentioned alongside the ape, means the guenon. On the first passage Rashi says: כעין קוף ויש לה זנב ובלשון אשכנז מרקצא "an ape-like animal with a tail, which in German is called Meerkatze."

‎On the third passage the same says: קפוד חיה שדומה לקוף ויש לה זנב ארוך ושמה מרטינ"ה "The קפוד is an ape-like animal with a long tail, which is called Marmot."

‎On the other hand, Rashi says on the second passage: קפוף עוף שקורין וולטיר ויש לו לסתות ולחיים כאדם "The קפוף is a bird which is called Vulture, and has jaws and cheeks like a man."

‎According to this explanation one would have to think of the bearded vulture in the latter passage, but without any reason. The passage reads in full: "Whoever sees an elephant (on account of its nose-like trunk, see article Ele68 [p. 68] The Lion.

‎phant), an ape and a קפוף says: ""Blessed be He who varies the creatures!"""

‎Reference is thus made here to the distortion of the human form, or of one of its body parts, produced by nature, which therefore fits the guenon quite well. It should not be overlooked that in all the cited passages the three animals mentioned are named side by side, which makes it all the more probable that קפוף everywhere denotes one and the same animal. (Cf. on this Luzzatto on Jes. [=Isaiah] 14, 23). It may be noted that in the relevant passage in the Tur and in the Shulchan Aruch Orach Chajim (§. 225, 8) קפוף goes unmentioned, probably as a result of the uncertainty owing to Rashi's double explanation.

‎Its gestation period is three years (Bechoroth 8a; but see §. 17), and its ugly appearance in a dream is an evil omen (Berachoth 57b).

‎§. 114.

‎The Lion.

‎Generic name: ארי (Aboth 5, 20), אריא (B. Kama 40a) and אריה (Jebamoth 122b). The male lion is called ארי זכר (Sabbat 150a), the female: לביה (B. Kama 16b) and גורייתא (Sabbat 67a).

‎Of אריא the pl. is אריות (Chulin 3b) and אריותא (Sanhedrin 59b*).

‎The young lion or whelp is called גוריא (ibid. 64a).

‎ארי is derived from ראה (the seeing one, Bochart), or ארה (to tear off, tear apart, Gesenius [=Wilhelm Gesenius, Hebrew lexicon (probably Thesaurus linguae Hebraeae et Chaldaeae Veteris Testamenti, 1829–58)]).

‎לביה from لَبَأَ [labaʾa] (to suckle, Rosenmüller [=E. F. K. Rosenmüller, Handbuch der biblischen Alterthumskunde, vol. 4, part 2 (Leipzig 1831)]), or from לבי (to roar, Fürst [=Julius Fürst, Hebräisches und chaldäisches Handwörterbuch über das Alte Testament (Leipzig 1857–61)]).

‎גוריא from גור (to suck, Fürst).

‎The lioness was called כלבא by some (Jalk. Schim. [=Yalkut Shimoni] part 2, §. 721). The lion is the king of beasts (Chagiga 13b). It is the symbol of strength (Pesachim 112a) and therefore serves as a title of honor for distinguished scholars (Sabbat 111b). The call of God, too, is poetically compared to the roar of the lion (Berachoth 3a**), which, as travelers in Africa relate, so terrifies herds and other animals that they then crowd together and, trembling, fear the still distant king of the forests.

‎The mighty spirit of seduction to idolatry also appears in the [p. 69] form of a young lion, whose roaring is heard 400 miles away (Sanhedrin 64a); at the basis of this hypersymbolism there certainly lies the truth that the lion's roar is very loud and far-reaching.

‎Other passages which denote the majesty and fearsomeness of the lion are: "Be a tail among lions and not a head among foxes!", for there strength rules, here cunning (Aboth 4, 15), "Do not fight the dead lion!" (Gittin 83b), i.e., have respect for greatness that once was.

‎If one was afraid to approach an object, one said: The lion crouches upon the object (Schebuoth 22b*). Its voracity was expressed by the proverb: “A handful does not satisfy the lion” (Berachoth 3b), and its satisfied lust for prey, which it makes known by joyful roaring while devouring its quarry, was alluded to by the proverb: “The lion does not roar over a heap of straw, but over a heap of meat” (ibid. 32a**). It was kept in menageries and fed with the flesh of wild asses (Menachoth 103b).

‎The Talmud seems to indicate the nature of the lion's voice through the various expressions for its modulation. When the lion is hungry and goes out in search of prey, its roaring at this time is called שאג, which is why God cries out over the loss of the Temple like the lion (Berachoth 3a); when the lion catches sight of the prey, or is already near it, the voice is modulated and is called עוא (Chulin 53a); while devouring it, it growls (Berachoth 32a), which is called נהם.

‎The fore parts of its body are strikingly more developed than the hind parts, so that it appears narrow behind and broad in front (Middoth 4, 7). Its gestation period is three years (Bechoroth 8a); but this is erroneous, as was discussed in §. 17; it is 180 days, see Voigt, l. c. 1, p. 268***).

‎If it seizes a [p. 70] 70 The Tiger.

‎prey, it devours it at once, without waiting for its death (Pesachim 49b), which agrees entirely with the statement of more recent naturalists, for the lion, like all beasts of prey that feed on living animals in general, thrusts its head into the prey and burrows in it, in order to enjoy the hot food with relish.

‎Sometimes, however, the lion lingers among herds without touching them (Chulin 53a), or its greed abates once it has torn one of them to pieces (ibid.). From the wound that it has made with its teeth or with its claws a yellow fluid flows (Arist. [=Aristotle] l. c. 9, 31, 3), which is called ויהורא (Chulin ibid., and on this §. 86).

‎It attacks man only when it is very hungry (Jebamoth 121b), but never attacks two people who are together (Sabbat 151b).

‎It sometimes drags part of its prey into its den in order to have a store (Sanhedrin 90b), or as food for the lioness and the cubs (B. Kama 16b). Whoever has passed safely by a lions' den (גוב של אריות) must recite a blessing of thanksgiving (Berachoth 57b).

‎It can be tamed (Sanhedrin 15b), but because of its dangerousness it is kept in a cage (Sabbat 106b), and trading in it is forbidden (A. Sara [=Avodah Zarah] 16a). It is especially dangerous in the mating season, hence the proverb: “Who would spread a garment between lion and lioness when they are mating?” (Sanhedrin 106a).

‎For all its strength, however, it fears the Ethiopian gnat (Sabbat 77b), which will be discussed in detail in §. 433.

‎Lion's milk is used in medicine (Midr. Jalk. Schim. [=Midrash Yalkut Shimoni] part 2, §. 721).

‎§. 115. The Tiger.

‎טגריס (Τίγρις [Tigris]) and, by circumlocution: אריא דבי עילאי “lion of the forest Ilai” (Chulin 59b). Equal to the lion in size is the tiger, an animal little known to the ancients. Aristotle is the first to mention the tiger. He says: The Indian dogs are said to be born of the tiger and the dog (Naturg. [=Aristotle, History of Animals (Historia animalium; German title Naturgeschichte)] 8, 27, 8), from which passage one sees at the same time the inexact knowledge of the animal, since Aristotle attributes to the tiger the size of a dog. According to Aelian (Thierg. [=Aelian, On the Nature of Animals (De natura animalium; German title Thiergeschichte)] 8, 1) the tiger is borne by the Indian bitch, which likewise attests an incorrect [p. 71] knowledge of the animal. Later naturalists, on the other hand, describe it as an animal most similar to the lion, thus Oppian (Κυνηγετικά [Kynēgetika] book 3, cited in Bochart l. c. P. 3. lib. 1. col. 721 and ibid. lib. 8 col. 792): “After all this you will regard it as a mountain lioness, except for the hide, which surrounds it in variegated spots.”

‎Thus Varo (De ling. lat. [=Varro, On the Latin Language (De lingua Latina)] lib. 4) also places the two animals side by side: “Tigris est ut leo varius.”

‎Likewise, as we have seen, the Talmud too calls it, besides טגריס, also אריא דבי עילאי.

‎Rashi takes the word עילאי as the proper name of a certain forest; but perhaps the two words בי עילאי should be read as one word בעילאי, which, like באלא (Chulin 80a), designates either the forest of Lebanon or simply a forest. Or might עילאי perhaps be related to Ὕλη [Hylē], Silva? Compare article Ferret.

‎Münster [=Sebastian Münster (1488–1552), Hebraist; probably his Dictionarium Chaldaicum (Basel 1527)] translates עילאי by major.

‎Of natural history concerning this animal the Talmud has communicated only little, and moreover only in fabulous garb. Chulin 59b it is said that the tiger is so large that the distance between the individual lungs is nine fists wide, which the Tosephot even want to understand of the lungs lying next to each other! This much can be gathered from the passage: that the Talmud ascribes to the tiger greater strength and fearsomeness than to the lion, of which at least the latter is correct, for the tiger is in fact the cruelest and most fearsome beast of prey (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 269*).

### ‎§. 116.

### ‎The Leopard.

‎In antiquity there were special names for the different sexes of the panther (§. 117). The Greeks called the male Πάρδαλις [Pardalis], the female Πάνθηρ [Panthēr]; the Romans called the former Pardus, the latter Pantera. Later both sexes were given the common name Variae**), because of the variegated [p. 72] hide.

‎The Arabs had a common name for panther, leopard, tiger and ounce: نمر [namir] (namiron), because with all these animals regard was paid to the spotted variegation of the pelt. Thus the Hebrew נמר also seems to be a generic name for the various spotted animals. The dictionaries translate the word as Pardus and panther; Elias (in the ספר הברית p. 68; see §. 193 note) as: tiger; Rashi, on the other hand (on Jerem. [=Jeremiah] 13, 23), as: ליאפרט "leopard", the sense in which we would be inclined to believe it must be understood in the Talmud, since the latter has טגריס (§. 115) for tiger and ברדלס (§. 117) for panther. The leopard is regarded by the ancients as a bastard of the panther and the lioness (see the passages in Bochart l. c. 3, 9, col. 804); the rabbis too consider the נמר a bastard, but as that of the boar and the lioness. Thus Bartenoro says (following Simeon b. Zemach Duran on Aboth 5, 20): "The Namar is borne by the wild boar and the lioness; for the latter, in the rutting season, puts her head into the forest thicket and with roaring calls the male; but as soon as the boar hears the roaring, he comes and covers the lioness; the Namar, accordingly, being a bastard, is less strong than bold and rapacious."

‎For in that Mishnah it says: Be firm and unshakable as the Namar (הוי עז), which, however, Rashi explains by: אנגוייש, angoisse. This expression denotes the anxious, fearful expectancy that was ascribed to the leopard when it awaits its prey, since, as mentioned, it is not so much strong as rapacious and therefore lies in wait for its prey with a certain agitation.

‎To this also belongs the statement of the Talmud that the נמר lives in polygamy and, probably as a despised bastard, is presented as the counterpart of morality (Kiduschin 70a).

‎Its danger to humans and herds is often mentioned (Sanhedrin 2a; B. Kama 15b); yet it is tameable (ibid.). Its gestation period is three years (Bechoroth 8a; but see §. 17).

### ‎§. 117.

### ‎The Panther.

‎The name ברדלס occurs nine times in the Talmud, but has three different meanings, for which reason, for an easier overview, the Talmud passages belonging together in each case will be grouped.

‎[p. 73] α) A. Sara 42a; Nidda 15b and Pesachim 9b are parallel passages in which ברדלס is mentioned alongside חולדה (weasel), and in which the word therefore denotes a small beast of prey, namely the polecat. In the article Polecat we shall give in more detail Rashi's divergent explanations in the above passages.

‎β) In B. Kama 16b, where the metamorphosis of the hyena believed in antiquity is discussed, ברדלס denotes the hyena, which will be discussed in detail in the relevant article.

‎γ) In the passages B Kama 15b; B. mez. [=Bava Metzia] 24a; ibid. 93b; Sanhedrin 2a; ibid. 15b and Bechoroth 8a, ברדלס denotes the panther. In B. Kama 15b and in both passages of Sanhedrin, ברדלס stands alongside the lion, wolf, bear, leopard and the serpent.

‎In B. mez. 24a alongside the lion, leopard and the bear.

‎Ibid. 93b alongside the lion, bear, leopard and the serpent.

‎Finally, at Bechoroth 8a alongside the wolf, lion, bear, leopard, elephant, ape, and the guenon.

‎From this it follows that ברדלס must be a large animal, and indeed, in most of the cited passages, a large beast of prey. Nevertheless, Rashi and the Tosephot show the greatest uncertainty in identifying the animal. At B. mez. 24a, where ברדלס is named as a beast of prey alongside the large beasts of prey mentioned, Rashi explains it as hyena, or polecat, which kills geese and hens, and at Sanhedrin 15b, where ברדלס is even presented as a beast of prey dangerous to humans, he likewise explains it as polecat (פוטיש, Putois), which, however, the Tosephot (ibid.) already rightly reject, since the polecat is dangerous neither to humans nor to herds of cattle. The Tosephot, however, waver between polecat and hyena and even at Bechoroth 8a, where the gestation period of the animals is dealt with, take ברדלס as polecat. It is, however, beyond doubt that the word, in its juxtaposition with the names of other large beasts of prey, can only mean the panther*).

‎The word corresponds to the Greek Πάρδαλις [Pardalis], by which in antiquity, to be sure, only the female of the panther was designated (see §. 116), yet the Talmudic word denotes both sexes. It always stands alongside נמר, with which our animal has much [p. 74] similarity and kinship, both because of the spotted variegation of the pelt and in general, because of the cat family. Its danger to humans and herds is well known, and the Talmud departs from Aristotle only in that it considers it tameable (B. Kama 15b), whereas Arist. (l. c. 1, 1, 12) declares the wolf and the pard to be the animals living in a perpetual state of wildness.

‎The female carries for three years (Bechoroth 8a; but see §. 17).

### ‎§. 118.

### ‎The Cat.

‎חתול (B. Kama 80a), pl. חתולים (ibid.), שונרא and שינרא (Berachoth 56b), fem. שונרתא (ibid. 6a). The cat was early on admitted into human dwellings on account of its usefulness. It exterminates mice (B. Kama 80a), weasels (Sanhedrin 105a) and even eats snakes (Pesachim 112b), whose venom is harmless to it (Sabbat 128b), the only danger for the inhabitants of the house being that the cat leaves behind some snake bones, which then penetrate people's heels and could have fatal consequences (Pesachim ibid.). The cat also provides a benefit with its soft, beautiful pelt (B. Kama 80b).

‎On the other hand, through her voracity she causes much damage, and through her tendency to bite even danger. She kills young fowl, young lambs and kids (Chulin 52b and 53a), and even ventures to attack large fowl (Ketuboth 41b). She has five claws, which is why a hen whose face had been struck by the paw of a pursuing cat was once examined, and five spots of blood were found at the wounded place (Chulin 52b). She can also become a mortal danger to infants if she is left unwatched (B. Kama 80b), for which reason it was customary to tie a protective leather over the chest of children (Kelim 26, 5; Maimuni there, however, explains differently).

‎Her rage for catching is all the greater when an escape route offers itself to the prey, in which case the wounds she inflicts are always suffused with red, whereas otherwise they do not show the inflamed redness (Chulin ibid.).

‎White cats bite more than black ones (B. Kama 80b), and because of their dangerousness, one who kills a vicious cat may regard it as ownerless property and accordingly appropriate its skin (Choschen Mispat §. 266, 4). Here the cat is called רע חתול, in which connection one should [p. 75] perhaps think of the well-known madness of the cat, in which state she is indeed dangerous. For cats have such an idiosyncrasy toward certain camphor-like, aromatic plants, such as Teucrium officinalis, that in their vicinity they fall into a kind of madness (Voigt, l. c. 1, p. 277 ff.).

‎Her food consists, as mentioned, of small predatory animals such as mice, rats and weasels *). Her enmity toward the last of these gave rise to the proverb for the false friendship of two enemies: “The cat and the weasel celebrate a wedding together” (Sanhedrin 105a). Sometimes the cat overeats on mice and perishes from it (B. mez. 97a).

‎She buries her excrement, which is why she is presented as an image of modesty (Erubin 100b); Pliny (h. n. [=Pliny the Elder, Natural History (Naturalis historia)] 10, 73, 94), however, explains this by saying that the cat fears the mice might be driven away by the smell of her excrement.

‎Cat excrement was also used for the same purpose (see article Mouse).

‎The cat, in contrast to the dog, has a poor memory for everything, which is explained by her eating mice (Horajoth 13a). This passage reads: מפני מה כלב מכיר את קונו וחתול אין מכיר את קונו אמר להם ומה האוכל ממה שהעכבר אוכל משכח תלמידו האוכל עכבר עצמו לא כל שכן; but apart from the fact that only the second part of the question is answered here, even in this answer the assertion that eating something previously touched by a mouse causes forgetfulness is surely something quod est demonstrandum; and yet, if only we do not hold to the strict wording, a truth underlies our passage; for eating mice is indeed the cause of her forgetfulness, not in itself, but because the cat is bound to it, for her thoughts and strivings are directed only at dainties and at catching mice, while everything else leaves no lasting impressions in her memory.

‎Nevertheless, she does not leave a comfortable dwelling once she has chosen it, which is why one need watch her but little (Sabbat 51b).

‎She belongs to the genus of wild beasts (Heller on Kilajim 8, 6). Her gestation period is 52 days (Bechoroth 8a), which is fairly accurate, since according to Voigt too (l. c. 1, p. 277) [p. 76] it lasts 55 days.

‎The womb of a black cat served mystical purposes (Berachoth 6a). In a fight with the rat (see that article) she must sometimes succumb (B. mez. 97a).

### ‎§. 119.

### ‎The Hyena.

‎In the Mishna (B. Kama 1, 4) it says: The wolf, lion, bear, leopard, the snake and the ברדלס are regarded as dangerous animals, and the owner must, if they have caused damage, pay full compensation. Bartenoro explains the word: חיה שקורין לה בערבי אלצבע “An animal that in Arabic is called اَلْضَبْع [al-ḍabʿ].” This is the same as the Hebrew צבוע, which Rosenmüller (Bibl. Alterth. [=E. F. K. Rosenmüller, Handbuch der biblischen Alterthumskunde (Leipzig 1823–31)] 4, p. 147), following Bochart (l. c. P. 1. lib. 3. cap. 11, col. 829 ff.), translates as hyena.

‎The Talmud gives her the various names: ברדלס, נפרוא, אפא and צבוע (B. Kama 16b). On Joma 84a Rashi explains the אפא דיכרא (male hyena) mentioned there by: הוא הצבוע ובלע"ז פוטייש “The אפא is the צבוע, in French Putois” (polecat); but the incorrectness of this explanation is already evident from the fact that Rashi himself, on B. mez. 24a, designates צבוע and פוטיאש as different animals (see article Polecat*).

‎Rashi explains the diversity of those four names as designations of the different states through which the hyena successively passes in its metamorphosis. As is well known, the belief prevailed in antiquity that the hyena changes its sex. Pliny says about this: “The common people believe that hyenas have both natures, alternating yearly and being now male, now female, and that they therefore give birth without a male; Aristotle, however, denies this” (h. n. 8, 30, 44). This belief, however, was also held by Aelian (Thierg. 1, 25), Oppian (Κυνηγετικά [Kynēgetika] 3, v. 289) and others.

‎Aristotle describes this belief as fabulous and explains its origin by the fact that the hyena has, directly beneath the tail, a blind pouch which has the shape of the female genitals [p. 77] and is mistakenly taken for them. In the male the anus lies beneath this blind pouch, and in the female the actual genital opening lies only beneath these two (N. G. [=Aristotle, History of Animals (Historia animalium; German title Naturgeschichte)] 6, 28, 2). Thus that belief arose, and Aristotle in another place (De gener. [=Aristotle, On the Generation of Animals (De generatione animalium)] 3, 6) names Herodorus of Heraclea as the authority for that legend. We should therefore not be surprised if the Talmud embellishes that fable of the hyena's metamorphosis even more prolifically. The mystical number seven plays a principal role in it. The male hyena, it says there, takes on the form of a bat seven years after its birth, again after seven years the form of that bat which is called ערפד, again after seven years that of the stinging nettle, then after an equally long time that of the thornbush, and finally it becomes the evil spirit.

‎If we strip this strange legend of its embellishment, its ethical teaching is that evil sinks ever deeper and finally reaches the highest degree of depravity. We see from this that the malice and rapacity of the hyena were well known, which we at least wish to note as being correct in terms of natural history. The Talmud is also right in holding it to be tameable, on which one may compare Voigt l. c. 1, p. 279.

‎The names צבוע and ברדלס are taken from the variegated spotting of the pelt, since the hyena is either striped or spotted.

‎אפא is probably derived from אף "anger", "malice", since the malice of the hyena is well known; likewise גפרוא seems to be derived from פרן "insolent"; cf. article Parrot on פירוז.

### ‎§. 120.

### ‎The Fox.

‎שועל (Aboth 2, 10), תעלא (Joma 43b), pl. שועלים (Sanhedrin 38b). The fox, as is well known, lives at night in burrows it has dug itself or taken from badgers, and by day in the bushes (Voigt l. c. 1. p. 281). The Talmud therefore has a proverb: "The fox does not die in the dust of its burrow", since it mostly stays there and thus remains unharmed by the dust to which it has become accustomed (Nedarim 81b). It also happened that a fox hollowed out a very large gourd and made its nest in it (Ketuboth 111b). That in rich, loose soil the gourd not rarely grows so large that it can reach 2 feet in diameter and weigh about 70 pounds is well known.

‎Its bite is dangerous and, because of its [p. 78] thin and curved teeth, hardly curable (Aboth 2, 10). It kills young goats and lambs, as well as all kinds of poultry (Chulin 53a and on this Schul. Ar, Jore Dea §. 57, 1), and is especially harmful to vineyards, since grapes are its favourite food (B. Kama 92a*).

‎Its usefulness consists, besides its pelt, also in this, that the tooth of a living fox prevents a person's sleep (if he keeps it on him), whereas the tooth of a dead one promotes it, for which purposes it was used (Sabbat 67a). Superstition hung a fox's tail between the eyes of a horse, which was supposed to contribute to its well-being (ibid. 53a).

‎It is not harmful to sown fields, which is why, to describe a futile endeavour, the proverb "He has gained as much as a fox from a ploughed field" is often used in the Talmud (Joma 43b; Nidda 65b).

‎Its gestation period is 6 months (Bechoroth 8a). It belongs to the small animals (חיה דקה, B. Kama 80a). The fox, since it lacks strength and courage, seeks to obtain its food through cunning, which is why it is said: "Be a tail among lions and not a head among foxes!" (Aboth 4, 15). Others explain this saying to mean that the lion raises its tail vigorously and lashes about with it, while the fox, when caught in its roguery, puts its tail between its legs and runs off, as it were ashamed, as a cowardly thief (see Buxdorf [=Johannes Buxtorf, Lexicon Chaldaicum, Talmudicum et Rabbinicum (Basel 1640)] Lex. tal. p. 2619).

‎When fleeing, in order not to be caught, it does not run straight ahead but makes all possible zigzag leaps (Sota 10a).

‎Hence of someone whose promising reputation had come to nothing, people exclaimed: "This lion has become a fox!" (B. Kama 117a).

‎The crafty man sometimes gets further through cunning than the simple man through honesty, but then prudence advises not to have him as an enemy, and hence the proverb: תעלא בעידניה סגיד ליה "When the fox is at the helm, call him politely 'Brother!'" (Megilla 16b, and on this my contribution in the Jüdisches Volksblatt vol. 1, p. 136). Its appearance is also a bad omen to superstition, in which people presumably held the opinion that an undertaking could not succeed if cunning and dishonesty stand to the right, i.e. at its side (Sanhedrin [p. 79] 65b). When running it often turns its head back to look behind it (Midr. Rabba Schemoth fol. 139b).

‎The fox plays a principal role in the world of fables. Rabbi Meir knew 300 fables about it (ibid. 38b); Rabbi Jochanan b. Sakai is also praised for possessing knowledge of the fox fables (Succa 28a, where Rashi gives information on the origin of the fables); however, only a few of them have been preserved in the Talmud and Midrash, of which we shall give a selection here. I refer here incidentally to a collection

‎twenty in number

‎of fables and tales from the animal world of the ancients, which I published in the Jüdisches Volksblatt (vol. 3), from which I here communicate some fables about the fox.

‎One day Reynard advised his oafish friend, the wolf, to present himself on the eve of the Sabbath in the kitchen of a well-to-do and charitable Israelite, in order to offer, very politely, his assistance in preparing the food. When, however, Master Isegrim received a portion of blows instead of alms, he called his friend to account, accusing him of treachery; the fox, however, answered good-naturedly that this painful misfortune of his bosom friend is explained by the fact that his father, on a similar visit, instead of helping the cook, had rather stolen the best pieces from him and had brought a like misfortune upon himself.

‎Little consoled by this, the wolf asked: “What, and I am to atone for the sin of my father?” “Certainly,” said the pious friend, “do you not know what Scripture says: ‘The parents eat unripe grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge’” (Jerem. 31, 29; Sanhedrin 38b).

‎Another time the fox was tormented by a burning thirst, and he invited the wolf to accompany him to a well. It was a bright moonlit night, and the disc of the moon was reflected clearly and whole on the surface of the water in the well. “Look down,” said the sly one to the oaf, “see what a splendid cheese is down there in the well!” The wolf grew greedy; yet the fox did not entirely trust him, and to attain his aim of quenching his thirst he made use of a double ruse. Above the well there was a beam, to which a chain with two buckets was fastened. In one bucket the fox now placed a stone that was lighter than himself, and he climbed into the second bucket, in which he had likewise first placed a stone, which, however, was lighter than the [p. 80] first and which could counterbalance it only together with the fox. This precaution was necessary in case the wolf should not let himself be lured, since the fox would then merely have to throw the stone he had kept with him out of the bucket in order to be jerked upward by the upper, heavier stone. But in the end this precaution was superfluous; the foolish wolf climbed into the upper bucket and sank abruptly into the depths, while the fox in the same manner rose to the top, so that the latter barely had time, in the brief moment of their meeting, to wish the wolf a good appetite (Sanhedrin ibid. and on it Edels in the Ain Jacob).

‎Yet the fox's cunning did not always succeed. One day he saw fish which, for fear of being caught, were seeking deeper places in the water. “Come up, my friends,” he called to them, “let us dwell together in peace after the custom of our fathers!”

‎“Fool,” replied the fish, “is this how you prove the cunning for which you are praised? We are not safe in the place of our life; how could we be so in the place of our certain death?”

‎(Berachoth 61b). As is well known, fish are the favourite food of the fox, and herrings are positively irresistible to him (Voigt l. c. 1. p. 281).

‎Finally, let one more fable be related, which I gave in the following adaptation in the place mentioned. The lion once invited all the animals to a festive banquet. A wide plain was chosen as the festival ground, and to counter the heat of the sun the king of beasts had large tents erected, whose covering consisted of the hides of such animals as the lion had killed, and among which there were even lion and wolf skins.

‎The merry company also wanted the spice of song, and Reynard, nolens volens, had to interrupt his eating and sing the gentlemen a little song. But they were to receive a bitter pill. “Will you also join in my song?” asked the fox. “Yes!” resounded from all sides, and the singer began:

‎Three cheers for our noble host, He bade us to the feast, Full of love and kindliness He gives us of the best: Ham of bear and haunch of boar, Panther's tongue and breast of ox, And wolf's tripe on top of all

‎O friends, what earthly bliss! [p. 81] But why do you grow so sullen now? Just look up overhead —; Happy he who has escaped from here, And may praise the host elsewhere!

‎Everyone fled, and Master Reynard enjoyed the dessert in perfect peace. After Midrasch Rabba Ester fol. 125, col. 4; which, however, sees the point of the fable in the cue that the fox gives the lion, namely that the lion too will meet his death.

### ‎§. 121.

### ‎The Wolf.

‎זאב (Bechoroth 8a), דובא (Chulin 75b) and לוקוס (Λύκος [Lykos], Succa 56b*). The voracity of the wolf has become proverbial, which is why a proselyte woman who had gone over from Judaism to Hellenism kicked the altar with her foot and cried out in Greek: “Wolf, wolf, how long will you devour the wealth of the Jews!” (Succa 56b). The same image of the wolf and the altar, but in a favourable sense, is used in Midrasch Rabba Bereschit fol. 112, col. 3. His voracity is the reason why he is not, like other animals, sufficiently careful of his young; he prefers to devour the prey himself (Rabba Bereschit fol. 112, c and on it Ide Mosche). He quenches his thirst at a spring and is sometimes caught there in nets (Rabba Ester fol. 126a).

‎It is dangerous to herds of cattle (B. Kama 15b); in flocks of sheep and goats it attacks the he-goats in particular, because these frequently raise their heads and so, as it were, provoke it (Sabbat 53b). When hungry it even attacks people, and thus it once killed two children (Tanit 19a); otherwise, however, it is cowardly, and the shepherd has only himself to blame if he could not drive off a single wolf. Even against two shepherd dogs a wolf can accomplish little; on the other hand, it is dangerous when the wolves hunt together in packs (B. mez. 93b**). In its wild state anyone may kill it and regard the pelt as ownerless [p. 82] property; but if it is tamed, it is regarded as private property (Sanhedrin 15b). The Talmud's statement about the tameability of the wolf is more correct than that of Aristotle (l. c. 1, 1, 12; §. 117), according to whom the wolf and the pard live in a perpetual state of wildness. Voigt (l. c. 1. p. 285) relates that an officer in Potsdam in the time of Frederick the Great had a wolf that accompanied him daily to the guard parade.

‎The wounds which the wolf inflicts on cattle with its teeth are not round but elongated and torn (Sebachim 74b), yet only in small cattle does the wound it inflicts become inflamed (Chulin 42a). Outwardly it bears much resemblance to the dog and, especially at twilight, can hardly be distinguished from it (Berachoth 9b); it also sometimes mates with the dog (Rabba Bereschit fol. 31b). Its gestation lasts three years (Bechoroth 8a; cf. however §. 17). On the fables of the wolf with the fox see §. 120, and on those of the wolf with the dogs §. 36.

‎Hypocritical meekness is called: ענוה זאבית, cf. Buxdorf p. 1633.

### ‎§. 122.

### ‎The Dog.

‎כלב (Bechoroth 8a), כלבא (Gittin 69b); fem. כלבתא (M. Katan [=Moed Katan] 10a) and גורייתא (Erubin 86a).

‎Further לםם (Sabbat 63b) and נבחן (Sanhedrin 63b).

‎Young dogs are called: גורייתא זוטרתא (Sabbat 155b*).

‎On נאני as a name for the dog see §. 511, on שגל see §. 123, and on שואבות p. 86.

‎The name נבחן is derived from נבח "to bark"; the ן is epenthetic, as in גרזן. The Talmud traces this name back to 2. Kön. [=Kings (German Könige)] 17, 31; there, however, the text reads נבחו**); cf. Nork: Die Götter Syriens [p. 83] p. 56.

‎The word לםב, which (Job 6, 4) is composed of בם, from בבם, and the ל-praefixum, is taken by the Talmud as a root word and explained as "dog", which is supposed to be called לםב in Greek. Without repeating here the conjectures already offered about this supposedly Greek word, I may be permitted to add the following surmise. Wolves, dogs and all those animals which drink by lapping with their broad, hollow tongue are described in Homer (Il. [=Homer, Iliad] 16, 161) as Λάψοντες γλώσσησιν ὕδωρ [lapsontes glōssēsin hydōr]; likewise the lapping of the dog when drinking is called Λάψις [lapsis].

‎For Λάψων [lapsōn], Λάμψων [lampsōn] may originally have been written, to which the Latin Lambo still points; a similar example is Λαψάνη [lapsanē], which, like Λαμψάνη [lampsanē], denotes one and the same kind of herb. But whereas in Λάψων [lapsōn] the μ [m] was lost, לםב dropped the π [p], and one said Λάμσων [lamsōn] for Λάμψων [lampsōn], thinking here of the lapping of the dog, which (Sanhedrin 68a) is expressly mentioned as a natural-historical characteristic of the dog; and that the dog was named after one of its functions is evident from the name נבחן mentioned above and from שואבות.

‎The dog belongs to the beasts (Kilajim 8, 6; cf. on this §. 19), yet it may not be yoked together or crossbred with the wolf, to which it bears such an outward resemblance that, especially at dusk, it can hardly be distinguished from it (Berachoth 9b), since it is much weaker than the wolf (Kilajim 1, 6).

‎It is very hardened against hunger and maltreatment (Beza 25b. Raschi: אנדריש, endurci), in that it retains the food it has taken in its intestines for three days (B. batra 8a), and in extreme hunger even devours its own filth (B. Kama 92b), just as it is altogether its nature always to return to its excrement and to sniff at it (Joma 53b). As a rule it urinates against the wall (B. batra 19b).

‎It is called the poorest of all animals, since in the towns people used to throw it no food at all, and in the country only a meagre bit [p. 84] (Sabbat 155b). Only to the shepherd's dog was food given, consisting of bread prepared from a little flour and mostly from bran (Chalah 1, 8). The dog is not a beast of prey (Chulin 53a), yet in need it tears young lambs (B. Kama 15b) and laps up oil (ibid. 19b).

‎If it happens to find food and water, it eats first, since it could easily find drink elsewhere even if it were disturbed (Teharoth 3, 8, and on this §. 36). It sometimes bites people, without, however, injuring a bone in doing so (Pesachim 49b). It is skilful at catching objects thrown to it in the air (Sabbat 11b). Its gestation lasts 50 days (Bechoroth 8a, and on this §. 17). In copulation both partners remain attached to each other for a long time (Sanhedrin 108b), which, incidentally, is also the case with the wolf and the seal (Plin. h. n. [=Pliny the Elder, Natural History (Naturalis historia)] 10, 63, 83).

‎Its sense of smell is extraordinarily keen and scents bread buried three fists deep (Pesachim 31b).

‎Its usefulness is very great. People used to say: "Do not live in a town where no barking of dogs is heard (ibid. 113a), for it is not only the faithful guardian of the flocks (Chalah 1, 8), but also of people (Beza 15a). If there are two dogs with the flock, they defend it against the wolf (B. mez. 93b).

‎Dogs' excrement is used by tanners (Berachoth 25a), namely for tanning morocco leather (Chagiga 4a: קירדון, cordovan). Of interest here is Rashi on Ketuboth 77a. He says: “I do not know what dog excrement is used for, but I saw in Germany how the laundry is left to soak in dog excrement for one or two days before it is fit for washing.”

‎The dung of a white dog also possesses healing power (Gittin 69b), as do the hairs from the beard of an old dog (Sabbat 67a).

‎Yet because of its rabies it is extremely dangerous, and in this condition it may be killed even on the Sabbath (ibid. 121b). Even a head of cattle bitten by a mad dog is dangerous to eat (Chulin 58b).

‎Madness is recognized by the following signs. The dog holds its mouth open (because its mouth muscles swell), saliva flows from it copiously, its ears hang down, its tail lies on its thighs, and the dog then walks along the sides of the street; nor is it able to bark (Joma 83b). Against its bite, eating its diaphragm (חצר הכבד see on this §. 67) is recommended; [p. 85] but the success of this cure is very much doubted, see §. 101.

‎There are also otherwise vicious, biting dogs, the keeping of which is described as a sin (B. Kama 15b); in general one should keep a dog only on a chain (ibid. 79b), and only in harbor towns may one let it off the chain because of the insecurity (ibid. 83a). The bitch is more given to biting than the dog, which Aristotle (l. c. 9, 1, 3) also states, and hence the proverb ran: “If a dog barks at you, just go in; if a bitch barks at you, go away” (Erubin 86a). A pregnant woman miscarried when she was frightened merely by the barking of a dog (Sabbat 63b). Among themselves dogs are almost always quarrelsome and biting (Pesachim 113b); cf. §. 35*).

‎The four canine teeth of the dog are called ניבי (Sabbat 63b); the other teeth are saw-shaped and uneven (M. Katan 11a). Pliny (h. n. 11, 37, 61) also reports this and calls these teeth Dentes serratos, which when the mouth is closed come to rest not on top of one another but interlocking.

‎In the Orient the dog is regarded as the most contemptible animal, on which Rosenmüller (Bibl. Alterth. 4, p. 95 ff.) reports in detail, and so it becomes understandable that people were accustomed to give dogs the names of hated persons (A. Sara 54b; see also Rabba Wajikra fol. 174b). The slanderer and the bribed witness were considered worthy of being thrown to the dogs (Pesachim 118a). Disgusting and otherwise forbidden food was given to the dog (Beza 21a and often), and the spoilage of food must have reached the highest degree if even the dog leaves it untouched (see Heller on Teharoth 8b).

‎Its barking is called נבח (B. Kama 83a), its howling צעק (Berachoth 3a), where it is mentioned that the dog usually howls around the middle and the end of the second night watch, i.e., around 12 and 2 o'clock at night (ibid.**). Of the faithfulness of the dog there is no explicit mention in the Talmud, which is explained by the fact that in the Orient the dog has not become as accustomed to humans as in the West***); it was not tolerated in any [p. 86] human dwelling and was otherwise treated very unkindly too; yet it is said of it that, in contrast to the cat, it knows its master. The latter, as is well known, has attachment and faithfulness not to its master but to the dwelling once made its own, where it finds care and comfort, whereas the dog, even on meager fare, keeps faith with its owner (Horajoth 13a). On the fable of the two dogs and the wolf see §. 36.

‎In magic and mysticism the dog plays an important role. When dogs howl, they behold the Angel of Death; when they bark, they see the prophet Elijah (B. Kama 60b); compare with this Nork (Mytholog. Wörterb. [=F. Nork, Etymologisch-symbolisch-mythologisches Real-Wörterbuch (Stuttgart 1843–45)] article Hund). For one who goes out to seek a wife, the barking of dogs is a significant omen (Rabba Bereschit fol. 66a). In this passage it is at the same time noteworthy how the author of the Ide Mosche identifies שואבות (drinking by lapping) with כלבים, which supports our explanation of למס. Finally, according to some, the expression: “In the time of the Messiah the insolence of men will be doglike” (דור שפניו דומין לכלב) is to be taken literally (!) (see Matnat Kehuna on Rabba Schir ha-Schirim fol. 17, c).

### ‎§. 123.

### ‎The Lapdog.

‎Among the small, dainty dogs that the wealthy are accustomed to keep in their rooms and that ladies let lie in their laps, for which reason they are generally called lapdogs, belong the Bolognese, the Maltese, the Angora dog, the little lion dog and the silk poodle. One of these dogs is mentioned in the Talmud. It says (Ketuboth 61b): “If someone forbids his wife to be active in the [p. 87] household, he must divorce her, because idleness leads to all manner of vice.” The Talmud extends this Mishnah saying to the effect that divorce must take place even if the husband himself permits the wife to pass the time with chess (אישקקיש, Échecs; in Ibn Ezra, in the well-known poem on chess: שקאקי, Italian: Scacco*) and with lapdogs. Here the dogs are called: גורייתא קיסנייתא, which Rashi explains by כלבים דקים. Regarding גורייתא there is no doubt that the word means dogs, see above p. 82; the second word קיסנייתא, on the other hand, presents some difficulty. Bochart (Hiero. [=Samuel Bochart, Hierozoicon (Frankfurt a. M. 1675 edition)] P. 1, l. 2, c. 55, col. 664) would compare it with Casina, the word with which Plautus titles one of his comedies, and of which he says in the prologue that the comedy bears a dog's name (cum latranti nomine). But apart from the fact that the word “Casina” is a comic invention of Plautus, others would even read Canissa for it, but are no more fortunate in this, since this formation of the word from Canis does not otherwise exist at all, and the word is in any case merely invented by Plautus.

‎It is simpler and more correct to read קיטנייתא for קיסנייתא; this is also what the Sulzbach edition of the Talmud, which is available to me, has, and Naphtali also read it so in his מתנת כהונה on Wajikra Rabba fol. 186, col. 4. His words are: גור כלב קטן כדאמרינן בפרק אעפ"י דמטלא בגורייתא קיטנייתא פירש"י ז"ל בכלבים קטנים. Münster (in his Aruch s.v. קסם) translates correctly according to the sense: Catuli, Catelli.

‎In any case it follows from this passage that in the Talmudic period people had already begun to lay aside the Oriental aversion to the dog and were already acquainted with the Occidental hospitality toward this quadruped.

### ‎§. 124.

### ‎The Fox Spitz.

‎In B. Kama 80a it is said: One may raise cats, monkeys (or hedgehogs, see §. 112), polecats and כלבים כופרין so that they keep the house clean (according to Rashi: of mice). In explaining כלבים כופרין Rashi wavers between small, dwarfish dogs and large hunting dogs, which one may raise because of their harmlessness. The latter explanation, however, is incorrect already for the reason that large hunting dogs (see the following §.) do not serve for catching mice. A guiding source for determining that name, however, is given by the Mishnah (Kilajim 1, 6). Here it says: “The כלב הכופרי and the fox, although they resemble each other, may nevertheless not be crossed with one another.” Maimuni and Simson explain it as the hunting dog kept by villagers (כפר, village); the latter adds: the כלב הכופרי is, like the fox, small.

‎If, however, as also appears, the qualifier כופרי is meant to indicate only the local reference of habitat, this assumption nevertheless seems to us untenable for a twofold reason. First, in that first Talmud passage the raising of the dogs is permitted generally for towns no less than for villages, which explains why Rashi leaves that qualifier entirely out of consideration. Second, Simson cites the Jerusalem Talmud, which counts the כלב הכופרי among the wild animals, which even R. Meir concedes, although the dog is counted among the domestic animals (see Kilajim 8, 6). R. Meir, therefore, who counts the dog among the domestic animals and the כלב הכופרי among the wild animals, cannot possibly have thought merely of the local factor in distinguishing the two kinds.

‎We therefore believe we are not mistaken in seeing in the כלב הכופרי the hybrid of the dog with the fox. Aristotle too (l. c. 8, 27, 8) reports that the Laconian dogs are hybrids of the fox and the dog; these dogs are called fox spitzes among us (see Voigt, l. c. 1, p. 301). Now since foxes, owing to the proximity of the forests, do indeed come closer to villages than to towns, those hybrids too may be met with sooner in the countryside than in more populous towns, which explains the name of the dog [p. 89]. The fox spitz is small, even smaller than the English spitz. In the Mishnah (Kilajim 1, 6) the הכופרי therefore stands in the same relation to the dog and the fox as the פרד mentioned there to the horse and the ass: the one as well as the other are hybrids (see article Mule); that the fox spitz can be trained to catch mice, or is perhaps already disposed to it by nature, will not be surprising*).

### ‎§. 125.

### ‎The Hunting Dog.

‎The hunting dog, Canis sagax, is not expressly mentioned in the Talmud, but it is by Rashi (B. Kama 80a). R. erroneously explains כלבים הכופרין as hunting dogs which were supposed to serve to clear the dwelling of mice; but apart from the fact that its breeding is rare, on which weight is laid precisely in the said passage, it also serves only for tracking hares and foxes, and occasionally also for big-game hunting (see Voigt l. c. 1, p. 295). Cf. the preceding article; cf. also §. 122, note*, where hunting with dogs is called קינוגיא.

‎The greyhound (whippet) is, according to some, supposed to be the Hebrew זרזיר (Prov. [=Proverbs] 30, 31).

### ‎§. 126.

### ‎The Marten.

‎נמיה (Chulin 52b; Rashi: מרטרינא, Martre). A harmful beast of prey. It strangles lambs and young goats (ibid.) and is especially very harmful to poultry, which is why the air holes of henhouses are made very small, so that the marten and the weasel could not get into them (Sabbat 146b). The ladder was placed four cubits away from the dovecote, because at a lesser distance the marten can leap into the dovecote (B. batra 22b). It was once so bold as to snatch a piece from a man who was bringing game to the market (Schekalim 12, [p. 90] col. b*).

‎Incidentally, Rashi does not seem to be of a firm opinion in identifying the animal. At B. batra 22b he explains נמיה by חיה קטנה "small animal", and at Chulin 52b he renders it in his own name by חיה and only in the name of others by "marten", while he at Jes. 34, 14 and Kimchi at Jerem. 50, 39 designate as marten the ציים mentioned there, which Jonathan [=Targum (Pseudo-)Jonathan] explains by חמוץ or חמון; it is, however, beyond doubt that the talmudic נמיה means the marten, see Choschen Mispat §. 155, 17, and the one mentioned in the Talmud would probably be the pine marten or, as it is also otherwise called, the beech marten. This animal makes considerable leaps and is especially harmful to feathered game. See Voigt l. c. 1, p. 308 ff.

‎According to the Midrash (Rabba Bereschit fol. 23, col. 1) its gestation period lasts 70 days.

‎The excrement of the marten is very foul-smelling, which is why one may not pray within four cubits of it (Jerusalem Talmud Berachoth C. 3, fol. 6, d).

### ‎§. 127.

### ‎The Polecat.

‎In the three parallel passages Pesachim 9b; A. Sara 42a and Nidda 15b, ברדלס is mentioned beside the weasel and means: polecat. But while Rashi leaves the word unexplained at the last passage, he translates it wrongly at the second as hyena (see article Panther) and only at the first passage does he translate it correctly by אלמנה פוטיישי, Olans putois, i.e., stinking polecat, since, as is well known, this animal spreads a stench through a strong anal secretion, so that even the pelt still always retains some of its smell (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 310). We have already shown earlier (§. 117) how ברדלס, which occurs frequently in the Talmud, has three meanings, and it is therefore no wonder that the decisors, induced by Rashi's uncertain explanations, waver in identifying the animal, see Isserls on Choschen Mispat §. 303, 6 and, on the other hand, Moses Ribkas (in the באר הגולה ibid. §. 389, 8). There is, however, no doubt that in the said parallel passages only the polecat is meant, since it is reported there that the polecat and the weasel drag away meat thrown into [p. 91] pits, namely, according to Rashi's explanation at Pesachim ibid., through the holes which these small beasts of prey dig. Such a hole must be a fist wide, for once a slave woman threw an aborted fetus into a pit, which was not found again because, as was supposed, one of the said animals had dragged it into its hole; the hole therefore cannot have been too narrow if the fetus could be dragged through it. It is, moreover, also known that the polecat digs underground passages into henhouses or as far as the pond.

‎Therefore Rashi's translation at B. mez. 24a is erroneous when he renders ברדלס, which is mentioned there beside large beasts of prey, as polecat, since the word there means panther; his statement, however, that the polecat is wont to kill geese and hens is correct.

‎Since this animal is pitch-black or brown, yellowish on the sides and generally of a yellow sheen, it is called ברדלס after its variegated coloring, see §. 117.

### ‎§. 128.

### ‎The Common Weasel.

‎חולדה (Pesachim 9a), כרכושתא (Sanhedrin 105a). The young are called בני כרכושתא (B. mez. 85a). On גלא (Γαλῆ [galē]) see §. 521.

‎Of all animals there is an equivalent in the sea, except of the weasel (Chulin 127a), about which the following legend exists among the rabbis. At the arranging of the universe the ruler of the sea also sat in council and demanded more inhabitants and subjects, for which he undertook to feed them properly. His demand was granted, whereupon he threw all the land animals into the sea in pairs, male and female, so that all of them are found again in the sea, only with a more or less altered form. The weasel, however, was standing close to the shore at the time, so that its form was reflected on the surface of the water, and when the prince of the sea wanted to throw it into the water as well, it pointed to its reflected image, pretending that it had already been thrown into the sea, by which ruse it escaped the common fate of the land animals.

‎As is well known, there is an extraordinary number of sea creatures which, although belonging to the realm of fish, nevertheless bear the names of land animals because of a partial resemblance to the latter. [p. 92] Every zoological handbook lists hundreds of them; the Talmud too names the sea dog (Kelim 17, 13), the sea goat (B. batra 74a), the sea ass (Chulin 127a), the sea ox (A. Sara 39a), etc. However, the ancient naturalists depart from that Talmudic legend in that they also call a fish the sea weasel. The liver of the Mustela marina was used as a remedy (Plin. h. n. 32, 9, 37); Ennius too (cited in Bochart l. c. P. 2, lib. 1, cap. 42, p. 751) has a verse: „Omnibus in Clupea praestat mustela marina“. This fish, whose liver is said to be a delicacy, lives according to Pliny (h. n. 9, 17, 29) in the Lacu Brigantino, i.e., in Lake Constance near Konstanz (see Harduinus on the cited passage). Without extending here the inquiry into the identification of the said fish, it suffices to have shown that the view of the Talmud is not a universal one. We wish first to add two other legends to the one above. Well known is the beautiful legend of the well and the weasel, which avenged the perjury of a young man and his breach of faith toward a virtuous maiden, in that through the two of them his two children met their death. Pseudo-Rashi on Tanit 8a relates it in full, from which, as far as natural history is concerned, this much can be gathered: that the weasel can become dangerous to sleeping children.

‎Less well known is the legend which Bochart relates from Abdollatif. It runs: „Someone caught a young weasel and shut it in a cage, but in such a way that the mother could see it. At once she ran off and brought a coin from her hiding hole; she laid it down as though she wished to ransom her young. When, however, the young one was still not set free, the mother repeated the trip several times until she had brought five coins; but when, despite this, the young one was not released, the old one brought an empty rag, as though she wished to indicate to the jailer that her store of money was exhausted, and when the young one still remained captive, the old one made as if to take the denarii back, whereupon the man released the young one“ (Hieroz. P. 1, lib. 3, cap. 35, col. 1029). Without going further here into the fabulous embellishment of this legend, we need only note the truth of natural history that small predatory animals living in holes carry off shiny objects, as is indeed the case with the weasel, the mouse (see that article) and others, in connection with which the following Midrash passage (Wajikra Rabba fol. 171, col. 4) deserves to be [p. 93] mentioned; it runs: „A ruler once had all the receivers of stolen goods put to death and all the thieves set free. When he was reproached for this, he summoned all the people and before their eyes scattered coins, which the weasels, which he had had brought, carried off into their holes. The next day he again scattered coins in the presence of the people, but in the meantime had the weasel holes stopped up, so that these animals brought the coins back when they found their dwellings closed. The ruler now showed how, through the removal of the thieves' den, the thefts too are removed."

‎A similar saying is found in the Talmud, see article Mouse.

‎We now give the natural-historical material which the Talmud contains about the weasel. It lives under dung heaps (Pesachim 8b), in holes and crevices in walls (ibid. 118b), and finally also in pits (Nidda 15b). It also burrows into the earth (see Rashi on Succa 20b).

‎It kills other predatory animals that are larger than itself (such as rats; Voigt l. c. 1, p. 312) and carries them off in its mouth (Teharoth 4, 3); it is especially dangerous to domestic fowl (Chulin 52b), for which reason the air holes in henhouses were made very small, so that the weasel could not get in (Sabbat 146b); on the other hand, it does not attack the hens when they scratch on the dung heaps (Pesachim 8b). Its teeth are pointed and curved, so that its bite on the heads of hens perforates the skull and the meninges (Chulin 56a*). It gnaws at human corpses, which must therefore be guarded against weasels (Sabbat 151b). It is therefore no wonder that it was hunted with traps (Kelim 15b), and it also has a fearsome enemy in the cat, for which reason, of the feigned friendship of two perpetual enemies, people used to say: „Cat and weasel are celebrating a wedding together" (Sanhedrin 105a). It is very voracious and leaves nothing of the meat that it finds, which it is indeed wont to do with bread, since of this it merely eats its fill (Pesachim 9a). It drinks by lapping (Para 9, 3). It gave superstition occasion for erroneous notions (Sanhedrin 66a).

### ‎[p. 94] §. 129.

### ‎The Great Weasel.

‎For better orientation in this article we must quote the relevant Talmud passage in full. It reads, B. Kama 80a: מגדלין כלבים כופרין וחתולים, וקופין וחולדות סנאים מפני שעשויין לנקר את הבית. מאי חולדות סנאים? אמר ר"י שרצא חרצא ואיכא דאמרו חרוא דקטיני שקי ורעיא ביני וורדיני ומאי שרצא דמחתאי שקירה. Note above all that for קופין a more correct reading is קופדים (hedgehogs), see §. 112, since the hedgehog, but surely not the ape, serves to catch mice. Further, the gloss reads חרצא for חרוא, and probably rightly so, since the second opinion (איכא דאמרי) differs from the first only in that it explains שרצא חרצא as designations of two different animals, namely חרצא as an animal with long and thin legs and שרצא as an animal with short legs, whereas Rabbi Jehuda takes both names for one and the same animal, namely in the sense of “boring creeping animal”. חרצא is the noun of חרץ “to make cracks”, since the animal lives in cracks or bored holes. Here it is undoubtedly the great weasel or ermine, which indeed has high hind legs that, through their thinness, form an empty space between them (cf. also the Tos. ibid. s.v. דקטיני). But before we substantiate our view further, we shall first examine some other views. Sachs, who not infrequently lets himself be guided by the mere similarity of sound of Aramaic and Greek words and thus makes the most striking conjectures (see especially article “Many-Foot”), explains (Beiträge zur Sprach- und Alterthumsforschung no. 2, p. 94) חולדות סנאים as the land hedgehog (Ἐχῖνος χερσαῖος [Echinos chersaios]); the latter Greek word is supposed to be the Aramaic חרצא. or חרוא. But apart from the fact that this animal has already been mentioned immediately before in the more correct reading קופדים, it is precisely the land hedgehog that has short and fairly strong feet; in general the hedgehog can hardly be called חולדה; the Hebrew אנקה seems rather to be a transposed form of Ἐχῖνος [Echinos], and besides this name the Talmud has still other designations for the hedgehog, cf. that article.

‎Less mistaken, though imprecise, is Maimuni's explanation of the animal (on Kilajim 8, 5, where סניים is written for סנאים), when he says: “It is similar to the weasel and belongs to the fox genus.”

‎The ermine, however, shares only certain characteristics with the fox [p. 95]: thus during the summer it is reddish-brown above and always has a white throat, which is sometimes also the case with the fox. Both animals have a valuable fur, both are shy and not easily tamed, both spread an unpleasant smell. Like the brant fox, the ermine too has a black, bushy tail tip; there are, moreover, also entirely white foxes, a colour which the ermine assumes in winter. Nevertheless, for all that, the ermine cannot be counted among the fox genus, since the weasel genus is distinguished by its slender, sinuous body, and especially by its short tail, and it is precisely in this that it differs from the fox. Sachs (loc. cit.) says: The explanation wavers between “land hedgehog and squirrel”; I do not know, however, who gave the one or the other explanation; but that the latter explanation is false is evident from the simple circumstance that the squirrel, on account of its bushy tail, could least of all be called חולדה; and does that little animal serve for catching mice?

‎The simplest view, however, is that חולדות סנאים are ermines. Pliny (h. n. 29, 4, 16) says: There are two kinds of weasel; the one, which is called the forest weasel*), differs from the common weasel in size. It is rightly called חרצא, since it mostly inhabits holes in the ground and cracks in walls (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 312); yet it also stays among shrubs and trees (in Pliny: Silva, for which סנאים is, however, an improper equivalent), and its bodily form is entirely that given by the Talmud. The Tosephot already cited very correctly distinguish the great weasel from the small one, and the only difficulty is presented by the expression ורעיא בינ וורדיני, since here the rose bush alone is mentioned; but this does not constitute a decisive factor, since the ermine can, in any case, also stay among rose thorns.

### ‎§. 130.

### ‎The Ferret.

‎Sabbat 28a it is said that the skin of the Tachasch animal (§. 176), which in the desert served to cover the Tabernacle, resembled the pelt of the אילן חלא in its variegated colouring. Rashi's explanation [p. 96] is very inadequate; he says merely that the חלא אילן is an unclean animal, i.e., one forbidden to be eaten, and spotted with colours. More detailed is the explanation of the Aruch, which we quote verbatim: חיה קטנה כדמות חתול ומרוקם הוא ויש בו גוונין הרבה וטמא הוא ושמענו שמצויין הן במקומות שצדין בהם שפנים ובלשון ארמי שפקא ובלשון ערבי זבזיב ובלשון יון חלא אילן, i.e., “a small, spotted, cat-like, many-coloured, unclean animal. We have heard that it is common in places where rabbits are caught. In Aramaic it is called שפקא, in Arabic زَبْزَب [zabzab], in Greek חלא אילן."

‎As regards first of all the foreign names, the Arabic one does indeed designate an animal that is cat-like and dangerous to humans; Bochart treats this in detail (Hieroz. P. 1, cap. 30, col. 987); on the other hand, according to Bochart, the Aramaic word is to be read not שפקא but כשקא, since the Syriac ܣܡܘܐ [סמוא] is used of a variegated pelt; as for the word חלא אילן, however, Bochart does not consider it Greek at all but Hebrew, to which he attaches a number of very daring and improbable conjectures. We shall, however, attempt further below to demonstrate the Greek origin of that name.

‎If for now we summarize the natural-historical data given according to the explanation of the Aruch, they are 1) the smallness, 2) the resemblance to the cat, 3) the uncleanness, 4) the training for catching rabbits and 5) the variegated colouring of the animal.

‎However clearly the first four characteristics of the animal point to the ferret, or the wild weasel, it is just as difficult to account for the last characteristic, for it is precisely on the variegated coloring, in which the Tachasch and the Tala Ilen resemble each other, that the Talmud lays the greatest weight; and yet the wild weasel, which originates from Africa, is yellowish, or according to others pale yellow, and only the tail is black, while its eyes are rose-red; the pelt thus has at most two different colors. Nevertheless, the other characteristics weigh so heavily in favor of the assumption of the ferret that one might feel justified in conjecturing either that the Talmud mistakenly held it to be many-colored, or that perhaps such a variety of the ferret actually existed, or finally, that the principal colors yellow and black gave occasion for that comparison with the ferret. Indeed, the ferret is smaller than the polecat, is described by the ancients as [p. 97] an animal resembling the cat (see on this the sources in Bochart l. c.), and its training for catching rabbits is universally known. The question now arises what meaning the words אילן חלא, designated as Greek, are supposed to have. We showed earlier (§. 122) how the Talmud named the dog in Greek after one of its functions; thus the words in question may likewise designate a natural characteristic of the ferret. Perhaps the following conjecture may not be unacceptable. The ferret originally lives in forests and in the wild state lives on small predatory animals, especially on wild rabbits. Even after taming it remains behind in the burrow as soon as it has caught a rabbit and sated itself on its blood. It must then be laboriously lured out, the hunter fastening a rabbit to a flexible pole and pushing it into the burrow; the ferret bites fast onto it and is pulled out together with it. This shows the ferret's inclination to withdraw from its master and to return to its former state; it manifests a striving toward its former abode, the forest, and may therefore have been called Ζηλοῦν ὕλην [Zēloun hylēn] (longing for the forest).

‎Landau (in the Aruch) derives it from Θάλλω [Thallō] and ὅλον [holon], and it is supposed to point to the variegated spotting, though here too no more than a mere conjecture was offered. The name still awaits a happier solution.

### ‎§. 131.

### ‎The Seal.

‎In the Mishnah Kelim 17, 13 it says: “Vessels made from the skin of all sea creatures are not susceptible to impurity; only those made from the skin of the seal (כלב המים) are capable of becoming impure,” because this animal, according to the explanation of the commentaries, comes onto land when in danger of being caught, and is thus regarded in ritual respects as a land animal. This statement is made by no naturalist; rather, the seal quickly dives under at approaching danger. But that explanation of the Mishnah is also superfluous, because the seal very often comes onto land and stays on it. Pliny reports that the seal gives birth to its young on land and does not lead them into the water before 12 days (h. n. 9, 13, 15); further, that it breathes [p. 98] and sleeps on land (ibid. 9, 7, 6). It is easily kept in gardens in a tub filled with water that is sunk into the ground up to the rim. Here the seals often sun themselves on dry ground, but at the slightest noise slip quickly into the water (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 316). From all this the ruling of the Mishnah is easily explicable. On its mention in the Midrash see above p. 65.

### ‎§. 132.

### ‎The Beaver.

‎It is very doubtful whether this animal is meant in the Talmud. Chulin 127a says: ביברי דנרש אינן מן הישוב “the ביברי in Naresch are not land animals.” Rashi says: חיות המציות באותו מקום בברנ"ש בלע"ז “animals in the place Naresch, and called בברנש.” Mussaphia and Bondi recognize here the beaver, French Bièvre. The Tosephot, however, are of another opinion and take ביברי for a town

‎name, since at Erubin 56a בי בירי and at Sota 10a בי בארי is mentioned as a town alongside נרש. It is indeed strange that precisely the beavers of the town and surroundings of Naresch alone should be regarded as water animals; the Tosephot therefore understand those Talmudic words in an ethical sense: “The Biberians and Nareschians do not have the proper customs of other people.” Buxdorf explains the word ביברי yet differently, as Vivaria, in which animals were kept and perhaps also bred in Naresch; but the explanation of the Tosephot fits the whole context of the Talmud passage, since shortly afterward the wickedness of the inhabitants of Naresch is related; moreover, Rashi would not have needed to explain the word ביברי with a foreign word if it were supposed to mean beaver.*)

### ‎[p. 99] §. 133.

### ‎The Bear.

‎דוב (Bechoroth 8a) and דובא (B. mez. 85b*). The Talmud compares the Persians to the bear in the following four respects. Like it, they eat much, are heavily covered with flesh, very hairy and restless (Kiduschin 72a). Here Raschi explains that the bear, bound to a chain, walks back and forth. Raschi explains more simply on A. Sara 2, 6: the bear is wont to walk constantly back and forth, which is why it is called דובא ניידא "restless bear" (Kiduschin ibid.). These four qualities do indeed belong to the bear. It is voracious, so that it swallows its food almost without chewing, and its voracity has become proverbial. Its fatness helps it to pass the winter almost without nourishment. Its fur is woolly and thick-haired, becoming somewhat smoother only with age. Finally, it is wont to be in constant motion, as can be observed when it is in an enclosure. It stays near the forest so that it can flee quickly when danger approaches (Sota 47a). It is considered more dangerous than the lion, although it is inferior to it in strength (Sanhedrin 98b), which the Tosephot (A. Sara 2b. s.v. ואריא) explain by saying that the bear is more inventive. Probably what is meant here are its keenly developed senses, which, except for sight, are extraordinarily sharp in the bear.

‎Because of its dangerousness, trade in it is forbidden (A. Sara 16a); yet it is tamable (B. Kama 15b), which is especially the case when it is caught young. Into old captured bears one inserts an iron ring through the nose, to which a chain is fastened, which Raschi had in mind in his explanation mentioned at the beginning.**) Its gestation lasts three years (Bechoroth 8a, and on this §. 17).

### ‎[p. 100] §. 134.

### ‎The Hedgehog.

‎אנקה (already biblical, related by transposition to Ἐχῖνος [Echinos]; Raschi: הריצון, Hérisson, Chulin 122a), קפוד, or קפוד ((Bartenoro: ארצו, Spanish Erizo, Kilajim 8, 5) and יילי (B. batra 4a, which Sachs erroneously takes for Ἴουλος [Ioulos], see on this in detail §. 446). Bartenoro on the cited passage says: "קפוד is an animal whose body is entirely covered with spines and which, when touched, draws in its feet and rolls itself up like a ball."

‎This happens because the skin of the back is developed on the inside into a powerful muscle, with which the animal can enclose its head, belly and paws and so make itself unassailable.

‎Raschi (B. batra ibid.) says: שערו קשה כמחטין "Its hair hard as needles", more precisely the same on Sabbat 54b: נימין חדים כמחט "Its spines sharp (i.e., pointed) as needles"; for the hedgehog has no guard hairs, but spines.

‎One used to tie the hedgehog skin (which in itself is soft, Chulin 122a) around the udders of cows so that the יאלי would not suck the milk from them (Sabbat 54b). The Talmudic word יאלי is explained in various ways. Raschi translates it as leeches, against which the Tosephot already rightly remark that leeches are never called יאלי and in any case suck only blood, not milk; they therefore take it in the sense of יילי, by which, as mentioned at the beginning, the hedgehog is designated. This explanation, however, is no happier, for firstly it is unknown that the hedgehog sucks milk, and then קפוד rather than יאלי should have been used, since the former word is mentioned immediately before.

‎Maimuni (Sabbat 5, 4) takes the word for snakes, which are wont to suck the milk from sleeping cows, but which are scared away by the rattling of the hedgehog spines. Snakes do indeed love milk (§. 105) and have keen hearing, so that they are scared away by the slightest noise (§. 33); but snakes are no more called יאלי than leeches are, which is why I may be permitted to add a conjecture. The Hebrew radix יאל has the meaning "to begin, to commence" and could thus serve as a designation for a "young one", which began its existence a short while before. One therefore tied a hedgehog skin around the udders of the cows in order to prevent the calves from sucking, and the word would be read יְאֳלִי; [p. 101] the Jerusalem Talmud (Sabbat 7, c) explains that practice entirely in the sense suggested.

‎With the hedgehog skin the eyes of a martyr were put out (B. batra 4a). In the passage B. Kama 80a, where it says that one raises cats, fox-spitzes, large weasels and קופין for catching mice, Bochart (Hieroz. P. I. lib. 3, cap. 36, col. 1036) more correctly reads קופרים "hedgehogs" for קופין, since apes are by no means trained to catch mice (see the articles Ape and Spider). The hedgehog, as is well known, thoroughly exterminates mice and cockroaches.

‎On the other hand, Bochart wrongly translates in the Talmudic passage (Berachoth 57b): "The elephant and the קפוד are in a dream apparitions announcing misfortune", by "hedgehog", since here the word means guenon (see this article), as reference is being made here to the partial resemblance of the animal to man.

### ‎§. 135.

### ‎The Mole.

‎תנשמת (Chulin 63a), באות (ibid. *), קרפראי (Berachoth 57b), קורפדאי (Raschi: טלפא, Talpa, Chulin 63a) and אישות (Kelim 21, 3). The Jerusalem Talmud takes אשת (see Tos. on Moed Katan 6b. s.v. צדין) for חולדה "weasel", which misleads Bochart into translating the biblical חולד as "mole" (Hieroz. P. I, lib. 3, cap. 35, col. 1024); but it is beyond doubt that the Babylonian Talmud does not understand אישות as weasel, since Kelim 21, 3 speaks of the catching of the אישות "moles" and ibid. 15, 6 of the catching of the חולדה "weasel", so that the two names absolutely cannot be identical. The view of the Jerusalem Talmud can perhaps be explained by the fact that both animals share several qualities and were considered related genera. Both animals are carnivorous, burrow under the earth, are short-footed, short-tailed and have a delicate, soft pelt.

‎The mole has no eyes (Moed Katan 6b). Aristotle, too, says of it: “Externally it has no noticeable eye; if, however, one removes the rather thick skin of the head, one finds a pair of imperfect ones, which nevertheless have all the parts of real eyes” (Thierg. 4, 8, 2). Pliny (h. n. 11, [p. 102] 37, 52) also repeats this. These are the Syrian moles, which indeed have no noticeable eye externally, and they are not to be confused with our moles. Schwarz (תוצאות הארץ, Jerusalem 5605, p. 4) relates that he sent a specimen of this animal to London; he calls it, however, “field mouse”, and it is said not to look like our mole. It has a thick, round head and is two spans long.

‎Since the weasel also has such a formation of the head, this is likewise an explanatory reason for the view of the Jerusalem Talmud mentioned above.

‎It burrows into the earth (Raschi, Succa 20b) and, both by this and by gnawing through the roots, does much damage to grain fields and orchards, for which reason it may be killed even on the intermediate days of the festival (M. Katan ib.)

‎In the passage Berachoth 57b, where it is said: “All birds are auspicious appearances in a dream, except the owl, the night owl and the קרפדאי (or according to the Aruch: קרפדאי), Raschi translates the word by טלפא, moles; yet here the word probably means “bat”, whose

‎truly hideous shape motivates that saying. Raschi’s translation can be explained by the fact that the bat belongs to a side family of the mole and the shrew, as Raschi himself (Chulin 63a) correctly indicates this relationship when he says: קלבא שוריץ דומה לטלפא שבשרצים, “The chauve-souris (bat) is among the birds what the talpa (mole) is among the small beasts of prey”, which is also repeated by the most recent naturalists (Voigt 1, p. 336).

### ‎§. 136.

### ‎The Bat.

‎עטלף (Bechoroth 7b. Raschi: קלבה שוריץ דומה לעכבר ויש לו כנפים “The chauve-souris [bat] resembles the mouse and possesses wings”, i.e., a flying membrane serving for flight *).

‎Further באות (Chulin 63a**), תנשמת (ibid.), קיפוף (ibid.), ערפדא (B. Kama 16a) and כרושתינא (Sabbat 78a). Here Raschi says: עטלף שקורין [p. 103] טלפא, i.e., כרושתינא is the עטלף which is called talpa (mole); but this is evidently incorrect, for עטלף in the Talmud means only bat, as indeed Raschi himself explains in the first-cited passage. Still more striking is Raschi's contradiction, who on Isaiah 2, 20 directly sets talpa opposite עטלף. The Raschi passage in Sabbat ibid. must therefore be emended to שורייץ קלבא שקורין עטלף שקורין, or to חנשמת טלפא; that the former emendation is the correct one, however, we shall demonstrate further below by parallel passages from Pliny, agreeing with the Talmud passage there.

‎As regards the other names, the biblical תנשמת occurs both among the birds (3. B. M. [=Buch Mose: book of Moses (5. B. M. = Deuteronomy)] 11, 18) and among the lower four-footed animals (ibid. 11, 30); for this ambiguous word in the Bible the Talmud has the common word באות and says: באות among the four-footed small beasts of prey is the mole, באות among the birds is the bat; but since עטלף also occurs among the birds (3. B. M. 11, 19), the different names עטלף, תנשמת and ערפד must indicate different varieties of bats, since the species of this genus of animals are in fact very numerous (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 335). Given the lack of reliable information, however, we cannot specify the species as they occur under the various names in the Talmud.

‎The bat is entirely an animal nocturnum; it mates and gives birth only at night (Beza 7a); it flies about at night, for which reason the cock calls to it: “What use is the light to you? It belongs to me!” (Sanhedrin 98b), on which Raschi makes the false statement that the bat has no eyes. Or did Raschi perhaps mean to indicate that the bat needs no eyes? As is well known, the most ingenious natural-historical and physiological experiments have been made with it. It was blinded and its eye sockets stopped with sticky wax, and yet its movements were as sure as before, and it flew skilfully about without bumping into anything, so that the famous Abbé Spallanzani (see Gren's: Neues Journal der Physik 1. B. 4. H. p. 399) concluded from this that [p. 104] it must have, near the head, yet another organ replacing the eye. Voigt (l. c. 1, p. 336) explains this by the heightened development of the sense of touch, just as some sensitive people perceive the approach of metallic bodies with their eyes closed.

‎In any case they are affected by daylight and therefore live during the day in dark, hidden places.

‎Chulin 134b it is said that the owner of a field, in places where there are no poor, is not obliged to leave the corners of his field unreaped, since he is obliged to do this toward the poor, but not toward the ravens and the עטלפים. Here ravens and bats are taken as the general designations for night and day birds, although neither of them lives on cereals.

‎Striking is the false statement of the Talmud that bats lay eggs and yet suckle their young at the breasts (Bechoroth 7b). Pliny expressly says (h. n. 10, 61, 91): “Among winged creatures the bat alone bears a live young”*), and in another passage (ibid. 11, 39, 95): “Of the egg-laying creatures none has either breasts or milk, but only the viviparous ones; of the winged ones, the bat alone”**). In both passages and frequently elsewhere Pliny calls the bat merely an animal volucre (flying animal), whereas the Talmud, guided by the circumstance that עטלף is also counted among the birds in Scripture, regards the bat outright as a bird and accordingly also as egg-laying.

‎Luzzatto aptly remarks (on Jes. 2, 20): “The bat is viviparous and does not belong to the birds; the people, however, err in this and count it among the birds, which is why the Torah deemed it necessary to mention עטלף at the end of the birds enumerated.”

‎The only question is how חנשמת, which likewise denotes a species of bat and nevertheless occupies the 14th place among the birds, is to be explained? About this חנשמת the Talmud says (Chulin 63a): באות שבעופות קיפוף “The באות (i.e., חנשמת) among the birds is קיפוף.”

‎This latter word Rashi explains by: ציאיט, Chouette, owl, or by bat; but the former explanation is wrong, since the owl has a different name (see article Owl and Eared Owl), and Rashi [p. 105] himself translates תנשמת on Lev. [=Leviticus] 11, 18 and on Deut. [=Deuteronomy] 14, 11 as bat; accordingly Luzzatto's explanation does not suffice for תנשמת.

‎I therefore believe the twofold mention of the bat among the birds is simply to be explained by its ability to fly; it is called עוף with the same justification as the flying serpent שרף מעופף, without therefore sharing the nature of birds in all respects, whereby, to be sure, the sacred writer is not responsible for the error of the Talmud.

‎Sabbat 78a it is said: “The blood of the כרושתינא serves for the disease which is called ירוד. According to Rashi this disease consists of white spots on the eye, probably the so-called white cataract. From the following parallel passages, however, it seems to follow that antiquity used the blood of the bat for an eye disease arising from ingrowing eyelashes. Pliny (h. n. 30, 13, 46) says: “The blood of the bat has the power of the herb psilothrum, which removes the hairs on the human body; on this Harduin cites the following passage of

‎D. Serenus:

‎Namque oculos infesta pilorum telorum tela lacessunt, Ergo locum crinis vulsi contige cruore, Quem dat avis tremulis simulatquae pellibus alas. *)

‎In this sense ירוד may well also be taken; in any case, as we see, the blood of the bat served in antiquity for the preservation of the eye, which is why Rashi's explanation of the word כרושתינא as mole is incorrect, since the use of its blood for eye diseases is nowhere mentioned; only for the insane is the blood of the mole said to be beneficial, according to the opinion of the Magi (Pliny, h. n. 30, 10, 24), of which, however, there is no question in our Talmud passage.

### ‎§. 137.

### ‎The Mouse.

‎עכבר (Sabbat 121b), pl. עכברים (Pesachim 10b), fem. עכברין (Kelim 15b), emph. עכברא (Erachin 30a), dimin. עכברתא (Beza [p. 106] 36b). In contrast to the water mouse (a kind of fish, §. 347), the house or field mouse is called: עכבר דיבשה (Chulin 127a). The house mouse is also called עכברא דמתא (A. Sara 68b).

‎The basic colour of the animal is well known, only there are varieties: yellow, pied, spotted and entirely white mice (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 450). The Talmud knows the black, i.e., the grey, and the white (Pesachim 10b). The mouse is defiling and belongs to the eight animals expressly designated by Scripture (3. B. M. 11, 29—30) as defiling (Chulin 127a). One feels disgust at it (Succa 36b), yet it did indeed happen that someone seized by the tail a dead mouse he found in spices and threw it out, but then used the spices (Beza 36b; see §. 272, note). It has many enemies: the cat, the Pomeranian dog, the hedgehog, the weasel (B. Kama 80a) and man, who sets traps for it (Kelim 15b). “Why,” it is asked, “are mice so persecuted?” “Because,” it is answered, “an evil inclination dwells in them; they gnaw garments from which they have no enjoyment at all” (Horajoth 13a*). Another adds that they even gnaw at wood (ibid.). Meat must be hung up in the air so that the mice cannot get at it (Beza 28a). Bread they hollow out but do not crumble it (Pesachim 10b); gourds too they hollow out (קרוט, Creuser) (Chulin 9a), and even human corpses must be protected from their gnawing (Sabbat 151b), which is recommended for attention even on ships (Berachoth 18a). From animal hides they eat out pieces (Bechoroth 29b), which is why one seeks to guard the phylactery straps from them (Rashi on Berachoth 24a). Yet no mouse robs another (Pesachim 10b). Their drinking is by lapping (Para 9, 3), which Aristotle also states (Thierg. 8, 8, 1). If one eats of what a mouse has gnawed, this is said to be harmful to the memory (Horajoth 13b).

‎They are driven away by the smell of cat excrement (Pliny h. n. 10, 73, 94), which is why the cat seeks to bury its excrement (Erubin 100b; §. 118).

‎Mice are wont to carry off shiny objects such as rings, coins and the like, which is why a miser who buries his money is called a mouse lying on the denarii [p. 107] (Sanhedrin 29b). Cf. §. 128*).

‎Another proverb is: “Not the mouse but the hole is the thief” (Erubin 30a).

‎By overeating on mice the cat can perish (B. mez. 97a).

### ‎§. 138.

### ‎The Field Mouse.

‎עכבר (M. Katan 6b). In a dry year field mice are at times extraordinarily numerous and very harmful to the fields. To fields planted with trees they are even more so than to sown fields, which is why on the intermediate festival days it was permitted to exterminate the mice on the former in the ordinary manner, on the latter in an unusual manner. The ordinary manner consisted in trapping, by digging hollows in which traps were set. The unusual manner consisted in ramming a spit or pole into the mouse holes (M. Katan 7a). The dwelling of field mice has, namely, two tunnels, one for entrance, another for exit (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 358); one therefore sought to destroy these dwellings and if possible to kill the mice. See, however, Tur and Sch. Ar. Orach Chajim §. 537, 13, where the manner of trapping is explained differently.

‎In the Jerusalem Talmud (B. mez. 9b) it says: “The wicked mice (עכברייא רשיעיא; cf. on this expression Horajoth 13a), as soon as they see much produce, call their companions and devour it together”; that expression עכבר רשיעא probably underlies the formation of the Judeo-German word “Achborisch.”

### ‎§. 139.

### ‎The Rat.

‎The name עכבר designates the whole mouse genus, to which the rat also belongs, which is why some of what was said above (§. 137) about the mouse may also apply to the rat, in particular the carrying off of shiny objects, the biting through of clothes, the gnawing of wood, and the harmfulness and voracity of the [p. 108] animal in general. The rat also has the same enemies as the mouse; yet the following Talmudic passage seems to deal only with the rat. In B. mez. 97a it is related: “Someone borrowed a cat from another, but the עכברי banded together against it and killed it. When, in consequence, the former owner brought an action for compensation, the judge declared that the defendant was to be acquitted of liability for damages, ‘for a man who lets himself be beaten to death by women has forfeited all right.’”

‎Now it is an impossibility that mice, whose character is timidity, could overcome the cat; the rat, however, is indeed bold, irascible and daring (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 350 and 353). It even leaps at a person's hands and face, and the cat can overcome it only when it has seized it by the throat, strangling it, whereas in any other attack it must sustain a terrible fight with the rat, in which it not infrequently perishes itself from the many wounds received.

### ‎§. 140.

### ‎The Dormouse.

‎This animal, which is also called Rellmaus, Bilchmaus, Rasselmaus, further Schlafratze, and by Blumenbach Glis esculentus, was well known to the Talmud, but was misunderstood by Rashi and explained by no commentary. In Ab. Sara 68b a distinction is made between עכברא דמתא (town mouse, i.e., house mouse, Mus domesticus) and עכברא דברא (mouse living outside, or rat). The former is loathsome, the latter a delicacy, and as such comes onto the tables of the great (עולה על שלחן מלכים). Rashi explains the animal by the foreign word אשקורל, which one would be inclined to explain as Ecureuil, for Gerson too (in the שער השמים ed. Heidenheim 24b) says of the squirrel: ויש מהם בעלי תנועה רכה כמעט שלא ינוחו כמו אישקורול “Some are of light, scarcely resting movement, like the Ecureuil”;

‎but apart from the fact that Rashi explains with the same word the zoophyte, the mouse consisting of flesh and earth (Sanhedrin 91a) (§. 494), which is surely too unappetizing to be considered a delicacy, the squirrel too is not edible, and least of all a titbit for gourmands.

‎In our passage, however, surely nothing other than the dormouse [p. 109] is meant. This, as is well known, belongs to the mouse genus, and the ancient Romans had special enclosures, called Gliraria (Varro: De re rust. 3, 15), in which they fed the dormouse and, when it had grown fat, ate it (see Harduin on Plin. h. n. 36, 2). The present-day Italians, too, hunt the dormouse and take it during its sleep, whereupon they prepare it as a favourite dish.

‎The Talmud, in contrast to the house mouse, calls it עכבר דברא “the mouse living outside,” since the dormouse lives in trees like the squirrel. Perhaps one should also think of the Glirem esculentum in the passage Jes. 66, 17; cf. Winer R. W. [=G. B. Winer, Biblisches Realwörterbuch] 2, p. 64.

### ‎§. 141.

### ‎The Hare.

‎ארנבה (Uxin 3, 3), ארנבא (Nedarim 65a) and ארנבתא (in the Aruch), pl. ארנבים (Menachoth 39b *).

‎The hare has upper teeth, but is nevertheless a ruminant (Chulin 59a). For above it has two incisors and two other smaller ones, in youth even four teeth (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 372). Büffon, it is true, doubts whether the hare ruminates, because it has not four stomachs, like the true ruminants, but only one. It has, however, been proven by observation that rumination in hares undoubtedly takes place. Rosenmüller deals with this in detail (Bibl. Alterth. vol. 4, Part 2, p. 212).

‎The hairs of the pelt are called צמר (Menachoth 39b), and rightly so, since the hare has a woolly pelt (Voigt l. c. p. 373). The hairs were used for weaving (Menachoth ibid.). On the woman's name ארנבה cf. §. 109; on the name Ταχίνα [Tachina] see §. 176, and on לנאי §. 521.

‎In Nedarim 65a it is related that Nebuchadnezzar ate raw hare's flesh; Pseudo-Rashi translates ארנבא חייא as “living hare”; likewise Matnat Kehuna on Rabba Echa fol. 73, c. Samuel Edeles remarks that rough warriors are indeed accustomed to eat raw meat, but Nebuchadnezzar even ate piece by piece from the living animal. It should be noted, however, that the ancient naturalists give numerous reports on the manifold medicinal uses of the [p. 110] hare; among others, that eating hare's flesh promotes lust, that it serves against epilepsy, gout, cancer etc., and so the eating of hare's flesh mentioned in our passage (of which, incidentally, Nebuchadnezzar was ashamed) may also be explained.

### ‎§. 142.

### ‎The Rabbit.

‎According to the thorough investigations of Bochart (Hieroz. P. 1, lib. 3, cap. 33, col. 1001 ff.) and Rosenmüller (Bibl. Alterth. vol. 4, Part 2, p. 213 ff.) it is now beyond doubt that the biblical שפן (Lev. 11, 5) does not mean rabbit; cf. also Winer (R. W. part 2, p. 506). In Scripture it rather means a kind of jumping mouse, or jumping hare, called Jarboa, designated by Blumenbach “Klipdachs,” which dwells under rocks, has toes on its feet and is a ruminant, so that this animal possesses all the properties assigned by Scripture to the שפן.

‎Among the Jewish scholars it is also Saadias who translates שפן by وبر [wabr], i.e., jerboa*). But it is just as certain that שפן in later Hebrew means rabbit, see above article Ferret and Lindau (in the ראשית למודים p. 27, 2), and so we may probably take שפן in Mishna Uxin 3, 3 as rabbit. It is mentioned both here and in Chulin 59a alongside the hare, with which, as is well known, it has much similarity.

‎The main objection to translating the biblical word as "rabbit" is that this animal (שפן, Hispania) is supposed to originate from Spain, and therefore was probably unknown in the Orient in that early period; but apart from the fact that the later Talmudists could well have known the animal, it is altogether questionable whether that derivation of the name is correct; why should not, conversely, the land of Spain have received its name from the rabbit, which is frequently found there? We find a similar kind of hysteron-proteron of a place name in the case of Mount Asasel; cf. on this my contribution in the L. Bl. d. Orients [=Literaturblatt des Orients (Leipzig 1840–51), the literary supplement of Der Orient] 1849, col. 184.

‎In any case the Talmudic statement (Chulin ibid.) fits the rabbit: it has upper teeth and is [p. 111] nevertheless a ruminant; for although, like the hare, it has only one stomach, it nonetheless brings the chewed food back into the mouth through the gullet; one notices in particular how, when the food is finished, it keeps moving its mouth, which, as with the hares, is surely a function of rumination (Rosenmüller l. c. p. 212, note 6).

### ‎§. 143.

### ‎The Deer.

‎צבי (Aboth 5, 20), טביא (Ketuboth 103b), the young deer בר טביא (Chulin 59a), pl. צבאים (Kilajim 1, 6) and צבים (Sabbat 128a).

‎fem. צביה (Chulin 132a), אילה (Erubin 54b) and אילתא (Bechoroth 7b).

‎The male has horns that spread into several branches (מפוצלות, Raschi: פורקיירש, Fourches, forked, Chulin 59b), which are called antlers. Every year these gain one more tine (Joma 29a). Since the deer often loses its antlers, the proverb "He laid his money on the horn of the deer" served to denote loss of money (Ketuboth 107b). From the hide, which shrinks greatly when flayed (Ketuboth 112a), excellent parchment is prepared (ibid. 103b). The female, or hind, has no horns (Raschi, Joma 29a). She has a narrow vagina, for which reason she always possesses the love of the male (Erubin 54b); but for this very reason the deer also sometimes feels moved to mate with the female of the fallow deer (§. 145), which, however, expels the semen again in the form of eggs (Bechoroth 7b); these eggs are called חלי דיחמורתא (ibid.) and (according to Aruch) pass out together with the excrements.

‎For the aforementioned reason the hind does not willingly receive the male (ibid.), and for the same reason has much pain in giving birth (B. batra 16b). Winer (R. W. part 1, p. 494) does not consider this statement sufficiently proven, and strange indeed is the Talmudic statement (ibid.) that the dragon (§. 298) assists the birth by biting and widening the vagina of the hind. As regards the first point, however, Pliny too says: "The females of deer do not endure the strain of copulation, for which reason they walk on during it, which is also the case with cows" (h. n. 10, 63, 83); and [p. 112] Aristotle explains the fertilization of cows and hinds, received while walking, by the sinewy, stiff penis of the male (Thierg. 5, 2, 3).

‎The flesh of the deer resembles in taste that of the heifer calf (Bechoroth 29b).

‎They gather in herds, forming a line in such a way that the head of the hind deer comes to rest on the back of the one in front (Sabbat 119). It is caught with nets (Ketuboth 103b); it is then fed with חצב (Sabbat 128a). Raschi explains the word: "A plant that roots deeply, whose straight-running root does not spread, and with which Joshua marked the boundaries at the division of the land." Likewise Bartenoro (Kilajim 1, 8). This plant is translated by some as "ivy", by others as "sugar cane". Pliny mentions several times the plant elaphoboscon, which served as deer fodder (h. n. 22, 22, 37); likewise it eats קולקסייא, i.e., colocasia, Indian water lily (Jerusalem Talmud Schebiith fol. 34b) and קינמון, cinnamon. Bereschit Rabba fol. 73, c relates that the goats and deer could reach up to the top of the cinnamon tree and eat it bare. Moreover, this tree is not very large (see Rosenmüller: Alterthümer 4, 1, p. 271); and furthermore what is meant here is probably the olive-like fruit which, according to Tavernier's report (Reisen, 2, 2, 12), grows on the cinnamon tree.

‎The deer does not eat meat and bread (B. Kama 19b).

‎It is one of the swiftest-footed animals (Ketuboth 112a) and serves as a model of stimulation and encouragement to the good (Aboth 5, 20).

‎Goat and deer, as well as he-goat and hind, interbreed with one another (Chulin 132a); this, however, seems to apply only to the smaller kind of deer, perhaps the roe deer, since in general nothing is mentioned by the ancient naturalists about the crossing of the said animals.

‎The statement of the Rabbis that the deer has its gall not on the liver but further toward the tail (Schul. Ar. Jore Dea §. 42, 8) agrees with Aristotle, who says: "The Achaean deer are said to have the gall in the tail" (Thierg. 2, 11, 4), while Pliny (h. n. 11, 37, 74) denies them a gall altogether.

‎Superstition fears meeting it at a crossroads, or its crossing the path (Sanhedrin 65b).

‎The deer family too is counted among the small animal kinds (חיה דקה) (B. Kama 80a). It is said of the deer that it keeps one eye open while sleeping (Tos. Chulin 59a. s.v. והרי), which, as is well known, is the case with the hare.

‎On [p. 113] an alleged property of deer horn see §. 289, note **.

‎It can become so tame that it goes out to pasture with the flock of sheep and returns to the fold (Rabba Bamidbar fol. 225a).

‎The female bears a pair each time (Samuel b. Meier on the Song of Songs 4, 5).

### ‎§. 144.

### ‎The Roe Deer.

‎The Hebrew name צבי, or איל, is the generic name for all species of the deer family, to which the roe deer also belongs. Some statements of the Talmud, however, seem to have to be understood as referring only to the roe deer; thus, that the goat crosses with the צבי, or the female of the latter with the he-goat (Chulin 132a), since the roe deer has certain similarities to the goat, which is why it is asserted that its rutting season coincides with that of the buck and not with that of the stag (Voigt 1. p. 399). Further, the Talmud mentions a צבי דאין מצולות "a deer that has no forked antlers", which Rashi mistakenly takes to be the ibex (שטיינבוק, Chulin 59b), an explanation which the Tosafot already reject, and which is incorrect for the very reason that the ibex has a different name (see that article); here, however, the roe deer is most probably meant, whose antler has only one fork and is sometimes entirely simple. Also in the Mishnah (Kilajim 1, 6), where the similarity of the goat to the צבי is emphasized, one should rather think of the roe deer. For the other characteristics that it otherwise shares with the deer, see the preceding article.

### ‎§. 145.

### ‎The Fallow Deer.

‎According to the agreement of most commentators, the biblical יחמור signifies the fallow deer, whose female is called יחמורתא in the Talmud (Bechoroth 7b). The female of the fallow deer is wont to mate with the stag, but expels the semen together with the excrements, which then has the form of eggs, called חלי דיחמורתא (see §. 143).

‎With the French name of the fallow deer, Daim, Rashi (Chulin 59b) explains the קרש mentioned there, which, however, as we shall show in the following article, is a species of antelope.

### ‎[p. 114] §. 146.

### ‎The Antelope.

‎Chulin 59b states: The קרש, although it has only one horn, is nevertheless permitted to be eaten; Rabbi Jehuda added: the קרש is called עילאי רבי טביא "stag of the forest Ilai" (see §. 115). Its hide is 16 spans long. Rashi explains the word by Daim (דייִן), fallow deer; but this always has two horns. Cf., however, Aristotle (l. c. 2, 2, 9); he says: The oryx (Ὄρυξ [Oryx]) has one horn and two hooves.

‎This is the Antilope Oryx, which is found in Egypt and Libya and is undoubtedly meant in our Talmudic passage. It is designated here as an animal permitted for eating, i.e., as one that is horned and cloven-hoofed; it is further counted among the deer family, which fully corresponds to the antelope, since it is altogether deer-like.

‎It is therefore unnecessary, with Buxdorf, Bochart and others, to take קרש as unicorn (apocopated from Μονόκερως [Monokerōs]), since the unicorn is in any case named differently in the Talmud (§. 174). The Talmud further says that the horn of the קרש dyes black (B. batra 16b), with which one may compare Oppian (in Rosenmüller loc. cit. p. 202); he says: "The horns of the oryx are black in colour, harder than bronze, oaks and stones".

‎Here the words "horns of the oryx" are not to be taken in the sense that the oryx has more than one horn, but in that of "oryx horns", just like the biblical קרני ראם (5. B. M. 33, 17), for which the Talmud already has more precisely קרני ראמים (A. Sara 3b).

‎One might be inclined to regard קרש, for רקש, as related to Oryx, just as אנקה may perhaps be related to Ἐχῖνος [Echinos]; a similar transposition of letters is גמליניתא חפשותא from Μεγάλην [Megalēn] (see article Cockchafer); but etymology matters little here, since קרש in its natural characteristics undoubtedly suggests the oryx. Cf. §. 175 and §. 355.

### ‎§. 147.

### ‎A Species of Antelope.

‎For the sake of completeness, the animal זמר may be mentioned here, which is referred to incidentally and only a single time (Chulin 80a), without any natural-historical information. Its investigation belongs solely to the field of biblical zoology, and, as is well known, [p. 115] the word occurring in 5. B. M. 14, 5 is translated either as elk, or giraffe, but most probably as antelope, whose species, admittedly, cannot be determined precisely.

### ‎§. 148.

### ‎The Ibex.

‎Of the biblical אקו (5. B. M. 14, 5) the fem. is יעלה (Proverb. [=Proverbs] 5, 19), and in the Plur. יעלי סלע (Job 39, 1), to which the German name of the animal, and still more the Latin Rupi-caprae (Plin. h. n. 8, 53, 79), corresponds. In the Talmud the fem. is likewise called יעלה (B. batra 16b), pl. יעלים (Kilajim 1, 6). Maimuni (on the latter passage) says: בערבי אלמעול "in Arabic: الوعل [al-waʿl].

‎Rashi explains (R. ha-Schana [=Rosh Hashanah] 26b) יעל by שטיין בוק, Steinbock, ibex, just as he also uses it on 5. B. M. 14, 5 for אקו; to this Nom. masc. corresponds the talmud. יעל (R. ha-Schana ibid.) and יעלא (Onkelos on Deut. ibid.).

‎In the Mishnah (Kilajim 1, 6) the ibex is designated as an animal similar to the deer, for, as is well known, it has a deer-like head and legs just as thin as the deer's. From its large, heavy horns wind instruments were made, with which the Jubilee year was proclaimed (R. ha-Schana ibid.); see, however, Bartenoro (on ibid. 3, 5).

‎Chulin 59b Rashi does indeed wrongly translate צבי as ibex, but his statement is correct that the horns of the ibex are not forked; they are crescent-shaped, curved obliquely backward, and furnished with knotty transverse rings.

‎The female is said to be unloving toward its young; at birth it climbs the highest rocks, unconcerned whether the young might fall down; but the eagle catches them when they fall and brings them back to the mother (B. batra 16a and b). On the last, fabulous part of this passage, cf. the similar statement about the birth of the hind (§. 143). The statement יעלה זו חשה (Jalkut Schim. part 2, fol. 122, c), together with the glossator there, still requires explanation.

### ‎§. 149.

### ‎The Buck-Deer.

‎It will hardly be possible to determine with certainty the כוי, which occurs so often in the Talmud; there is no other animal [p. 116] about which so many different and contradictory opinions have been expressed, and we shall therefore first list these opinions. They are found for the most part in the principal source: Chulin 80a. כוי is: 1) according to one opinion, the wild ram; 2) the hybrid of the he-goat and the hind; 3) a primary creature (בריה בפני עצמה), about which there is only doubt whether it belongs to the class of wild animals or to that of domestic cattle; 4) likewise, as in the last opinion, that כוי is a primary creature, but it belongs to the class of domestic cattle. 5) The Tosephot of R. Meier of Rothenburg (on Joma 74a, s.v. קסבר) hold that there is a כוי which belongs neither to the class of wild animals nor to that of domestic cattle, i.e., an animal that mates neither with an individual of the domestic class nor with one of the wild class. How vague and unsatisfactory this statement is need not first be demonstrated. Cf. also Tos. on Keritot 21a, Nidda 28a and Jebamoth 83a.

‎This uncertainty is further increased by the striking contradictions of Maimuni. In his commentary on the Mishna Chulin 6, 1 he adopts the third opinion mentioned above, whereas in the Jad ha-Chasaka (Hil. Maachaloth assurim cap. 1, §. 13) he accepts the second opinion, and in his Mishna commentary on Biccurim 2, 8 he conflates the second and third opinions, as though they complemented each other and formed a single opinion (see also Heller on the last passage). Rashi (B. Kama 78a) seems to accept the second opinion. We may therefore be permitted, independently of the various interpretations of the animal name in question, to put forward yet another opinion. The first opinion, that the כוי is the wild ram, lacks all probability for the very reason that the latter animal has its own name

‎איל הבר

‎; why, moreover, should another name have been chosen, since otherwise the compounding of the original animal name with בר was customary, cf. שור הבר, אוז הבר, הבר, חויר הבר, ברא תרנגול and many others.

‎The second opinion, too, that כוי is a hybrid, appears improbable, since in the above Talmud passage it is related that the family of the house of Doschai raised this animal in great numbers, in herds; apart from the fact that crossbreeding is forbidden by law, hybrids in general do not exist in such numbers that they could be raised in herds. But even supposing that כוי is in general the hybrid [p. 117] of domestic and wild animal (in contrast to כלאים, which is the hybrid of two different kinds of domestic cattle, see Rashi on B. Kama 78a), the investigation still has a further task in discussing the third opinion. The question is: which primary animal, present, as it seems, here and there in great numbers, is meant here? The name כוי easily tempts one to think of the maned stag (Brandhirsch); this is what the stag is called when in old age it acquires a mane on its neck and breast, at which point it is wild and dangerous (see Voigt l. c. 1, p. 395). But then it would remain incomprehensible why the Talmud, because of the doubt as to which class (wild animals or domestic cattle) it is to be counted among, assigns to it all the rigorous regulations (of a ritual nature) that are prescribed for both classes (Biccurim 2, 8—11). Surely the maned stag, which originally belonged to the class of wild animals (§. 143), cannot become doubtful with regard to its class merely on account of its age?*) That doubt could, however, well arise from the external appearance of the animal, and indeed Pliny describes an animal that is on the whole stag-like but has a beard like a goat; thus one could easily entertain a doubt in ritual respects about the class of the animal and so pursue the further consequences in legal determination. Pliny (h. n. 8, 33, 50) says: Eadem est specie, barba tantum et armorum villo distans, quem Τραγέλαφον [Tragelaphon] vocant, non alibi, quam juxta Phasin amnem nascens, "The so-called tragelaphos (goat-stag) is of the same species (as the stag), differing only in the beard and the shaggy hair of the shoulders, and is found nowhere but near the river Phasis." This double name already shows how the Greeks regarded the animal as, in a sense, a species composed of goat and stag, and כוי may well be identical with it. Admittedly, one would have to attach little weight to Pliny's statement that the animal is found only in one particular place, all the less so as Voigt (ibid.) likewise calls the above-mentioned maned stag, which is found everywhere, tragelaphos; besides, the tragelaphos known to the ancients could later also have spread elsewhere and in some places even have been raised in herds. [p. 118] Thus the primary animal assumed by the third and fourth opinions may well be the goat-stag.

‎On the hybrid of the stag and the she-goat see Ture Sahab on Jore Dea §. 28, 4.

### ‎§. 150.

### ‎The Musk Deer.

‎Berachoth 43a states: Over all fragrant things one pronounces the blessing: "Blessed be He who creates trees, who creates spices!" but not over מושק, because it is the fat of an animal.

‎Rashi explains the word by מושקא, Musc, musk, which, as is well known, is a reddish-brown substance, electuary-like when fresh, which the male of the musk deer carries in a pouch on the abdomen in front of the prepuce of the genital organ. See Voigt l. c. 1, p. 403.

‎The Talmud calls it משך "oil", for even when dried it feels greasy to the touch. Aruch cites a passage from Ketuboth 75a, which, however, is not found in our edition of the Talmud; according to it, musk is said to be a remedy against affliction with foul-smelling sweat, cf. §. 94.

‎It is remarkable that the mention of musk does not occur in any Greek or Arabic writer until the 11th century, whereas the Talmud already knew it. Cf. L. Bl. des Orients [=Literaturblatt des Orients (Leipzig 1840–51), the literary supplement of Der Orient] 1849, col. 62.

### ‎§. 151.

### ‎The Sheep.

‎Masc. איל (B. Kama 96b), זכר של רחלים (Jebamoth 121b), pl. דיכרי (Chulin 39b).

‎fem. רחל (Bechoroth 3b), רחלה (Ketuboth 68a), pl. רחילים (Jebamoth 121b *).

‎General names for both sexes are: שה, אימרי (Sabbat 77b), אימרי רברבי (Chulin 53a), בני אימריא, בני מרון (R. ha-Schana 18a) and יובלא (ibid. 26a).

‎With regard to ritual sacrifice the sheep had different names at different ages: in the first year of life it is called כשב, כבש or כבש; in the first month of the second year פלגס, נוקד and פרכריגמא (Para 1, 3 **); in natural-historical respect the saying holds: [p. 119] איל בן יומו קרוי איל "A sheep bears this name already when it is one day old" (B. Kama 65b).

‎Otherwise the little lamb is called: אימרא (Keritot 28b), more often טלא and טליה (Menachot 107b), pl. טלאים (Chulin 84a).

‎A large ram is called ברחא (Sabbat 18b) and in the pl. עתוזים, by which, however, the sheep kind in general is sometimes loosely designated (Chulin 84a). Finally, the goat-like monstrous birth dropped by a sheep is called נדמה (B. Kama 77b), and the small, lean lamb פטרוזא (Bechoroth 11a). The usefulness of the sheep is so great that the Talmud recommends selling fields and buying sheep in their stead (Chulin 84a). Owning them brings wealth (ibid. b).

‎The ram in particular provides manifold uses for musical instruments. From its horns wind instruments were made, from its hollow thigh bones flutes, from the large intestines strings for the lute, from the small intestines strings for the harp; the hide served for the drum, the wool for balls in the form of pomegranates, which were then fastened between the golden bells on the robe of the high priest, so that the bells rang by striking against those balls; hence it was laconically said: "The ram is one-toned in life and seven-toned in death" (Kinim 25a).

‎Lamb's meat was praised as something very tasty (Keritot 28b). The greatest value of the animal, however, lies in its wool, the obtaining of which required great care. The sheep were led into water in order to wash the wool frequently (Chulin 137a), for those that bore fine wool (see esp. §. 154) had covers tied around them so that it would remain clean (Sabbat 54a).

‎Sheep are either white, black, or variegated in colour (Chulin 135a). The wool, however, is not always of equal value, since it is sometimes coarse-haired and hardly usable for working (ibid. 137a).

‎After the sheep had been shorn, a [p. 120] sponge, or a woollen rag previously dipped in oil, was tied before their forehead so that they would not catch cold until new wool had grown (Sabbat 54b).

‎The sheep thrives better on dry pasture than on moist (Sota 34b); yet its fattening can be promoted by tying up its udder, whereby the development of milk is prevented and the animal gains in fatness (Sabbat 54a); sometimes, however, this tying was done with the intention of not letting the milk flow off (ibid.). When the flock goes out to pasture, the rams go ahead; they often look around as they do so, whereby they, as it were, provoke their arch-enemy, the wolf, and therefore become his prey first (ibid. 53b). That beast of prey usually seizes the sheep by the breast, which is why a leather collar was often tied on it (ibid.).

‎In a mixed flock the goats go ahead of the sheep (ibid. 77b). The latter have the habit of walking one behind another and all following the bellwether; from this arose the proverb: "As the sheep after the sheep, so the daughter after the mother", i.e. the way of life of the mother is naturally inherited by the daughter (Ketuboth 63a).

‎The sheep is often attacked, especially on the pasture, by the sheep botfly (§. 436), this insect penetrating through the animal's nostrils as far as the brain; then the rams usually butt each other with their horns, whereby the botflies fall out again; to the female sheep, on the other hand, juniper (קיסמא דריחמא) or hellebore (קיסמא דחנין; according to others the latter species is the Chinese Veratrum) was tied before the nose, whereby the sheep sneezes and expels the insects (Sabbat 54b).

‎As a rule the sheep becomes pregnant only at two years (Bechoroth 19b), cf. also Voigt l. c. 1, p. 412.

‎Its gestation lasts five months (ibid.), and since it is like the goat both in this and in the manner of copulation by mounting, the two species can therefore copulate with each other (ibid.). Copulation of the sheep can be prevented if the tail of the ewe is tied fast downward, and can on the other hand be promoted if the tail is tied upward, so that in the one case the vagina is covered, in the other exposed (Sabbat 54a). At birth, during which the sheep is if necessary assisted by laying warming rags dipped in oil on its forehead and on its abdomen (ibid. 54b), the young appears not, as with the goat, ears first, but [p. 121] lips first, sometimes, however, also with the temple side (טיפלש temporals, Bechoroth 35a).

‎Sometimes the sheep drops a goat-like monstrous birth (B. Kama 77b). Of particular quality were the sheep of Hebron (Sota 34b), of Kedar (Beza 20b) and of Miletus (§. 154), the former for their size*), the latter for their wool.

‎The bellwether, which goes ahead of the flock, wore a bell (משכוכית, or קרקשהא) around its neck (B Kama 52a), and is called נגרא (ibid.).

### ‎§. 152.

### ‎The Long-tailed Sheep.

‎In the Mishnah (Sabbat 5, 4) it says: “On the Sabbath the male sheep may not go out with the little cart under the tail.”

‎Bartenoro says: “These are sheep whose tails are long, and the little cart serves to keep the tails from being injured on stones and rocks.”

‎Rashi, more fully: “The tip of the tail of these rams is broad like a small saddle, boneless, fatty, thick and heavy, and since the tails are dragged along the ground, they could easily be injured; therefore one ties them to a little cart, by which they are easily drawn along.”

‎Finally, Maimuni says: “There is a kind of sheep with a long tail, such as those found in Egypt, whose tails are long and heavy and look like a large piece continuing from the body. For these tails one prepared a little cart, so that they would not chafe on the ground and lose their skin.”

‎Rosenmüller (Bibl. Alterth. vol. 4, section 2, p. 75 ff) has described this kind of sheep in detail. Incidentally, in our Mishnah we may also think of the broad-tailed and the fat-tailed sheep; certainly, however, the little cart is still used today with the long-tailed sheep; see Voigt l. c. 1, p. 409**).

### ‎[p. 122] §. 153.

### ‎The Wild Ram.

‎The Talmud (Chulin 80a) mentions an איל הבר, which according to an opinion expressed there is supposed to be identical with the כוי (see article Bockhirsch, §. 149). Although this is not the case, the איל הבר is nevertheless a ram that lives and grows up not among the flock of sheep but outside (בר), according to Rashi: in the forest. The sheep genus, however, is precisely the animal that has been most domesticated, and only a few species, such as the mouflon in Africa and the argali in the central and northern mountain ranges of Asia, live in high mountains; the latter in flocks that are not numerous, and it, the argali, is probably what is meant in our Talmudic passage; that one may not read אַיִל (deer) here is certain if only because the deer lives in the open anyway, and therefore the addition בר would be superfluous.

### ‎§. 154.

### ‎The Melitensian Sheep.

‎In the Talmud the word מילת, or מילתא, frequently occurs, which denotes the wool of the Milesian or Melitensian sheep, or also the garments made from this wool. Miletus, a famous city in Ionia, possessed sheep that already as lambs yielded the finest wool. See in detail Bondi (Or. Esther s.v. מילת). Several Talmudic passages thereby acquire their correct understanding. B. mez. 78b relates that the fur moth אבוקתא (§. 442) got into the מילתא that belonged to a king. The latter word corresponds to the Greek Μηλωτή [mēlōtē], which means sheepskin; this bears its name because it was made from the skins of the fine-wooled Melitensian sheep.

‎Schebuoth 6b says: One tied a covering around the lamb only one day old, so that the wool would remain clean and white for the use of מילת, i.e., for garments made from that aforementioned wool.

‎Sabbat 10b says: “Someone clothed a man with a [p. 123] valuable מילחא, which Rashi translates by מעיל “upper coat”; here too the valuable sheepskin is probably meant. איצטלא דמילחא (M. Katam 28b) is the stole of Milesian wool.

‎Several other Talmudic passages are cited in the Aruch (s.v. מל num. 14); here Mussaphia explains מילח as the former name of the island of Malta, namely Melita, which in a poem of Silius Italicus (De bello punico secundo 14. c. 52) is called the “wool-bearing” (lanigera), about which Landau speaks in detail in the note.

‎It should be noted that there was a famous island Μελίτη [Melitē] in Sicily and a city Μέλιττα [Melitta] in Africa; cf. Spannheim: Introductio ad Geog. sacram etc. p. 194, and it still remains to be investigated whether one of these names was not also meant in the passages of the Talmud concerned.

### ‎§. 155.

### ‎The Goat.

‎Masc. תיש (Chulin 79b), fem. תיישה (ibid.); M. שעיר (Menachoth 107b), F. שעירה (ibid.); M. צפירא (Joma 66b), F. צפירתא (Gittin 69b); Com. עז and עיזא (Chulin 59b), pl. עזים (Kilajim 1, 6) and עיזין (Rabba Wajikra fol. 170, d); ברחה (B. Kama 48a).

‎A bald goat: ברחא קרחא (Sabbat 152a).

‎The young: גדי (Menachoth 107b), גדיא (Keritot 28b).

‎A kid with drooping ears: גדיא חיוזא (Bechoroth 44a).

‎A young goat that has not yet become pregnant: צפירתא דלא איפתח (Gittin 69b). The lead goat: משכוכית and קרקשתא (B. Kama 52a*).

‎The goat can endure great drought and heat, in which no other animal can hold out any longer, and even then still give milk (Voigt l. c. 1. p. 416); therefore the Talmud says: Among the small cattle the goat is the most hardened (אנדריש, Endurci, Beza 25b).

‎The horns are flat and notched (קרנייר, Créneler, Chulin 59b). Nevertheless, one used to file notches into the horns in order to fasten the rope to them, because otherwise the lively, restless animal throws off the rope (Sabbat 52a). The ears are rather long and appear first at birth (Bechoroth 35a).

‎The he-goat, like the she-goat, has a long beard on the chin [p. 124] (Sabbat 52a). There are white and black goats (Bechoroth 8b); but the latter are the most common (Sabbat 77b).

‎The hair is called שער (Jebamoth 102b), but more often נוצות, either because goat hair is harder than wool and other hair of the domestic animals (Menachoth 39b), or because it is not shorn but plucked out like feathers (Chulin 137a).

‎In the goat the dung comes out thick at first, then faster and thinner (Tanit 9b).

‎Because of their courage, goats walk at the head of a mixed herd (Sabbat 77b); the lead goat, for which the most courageous and most excellent one is chosen, wears a bell around its neck, and the proverb belonging here is telling: “If the shepherd hates the flock, he blinds the lead goat” (B. Kama 52a). Another proverb runs: “If the shepherd is lame, the goats run away” (Sabbat 32a), by which it is indicated that a herd of goats is less easily led than a flock of sheep.*)

‎Fabulous is the famous strength of the goats that Job possessed, which were even able to overcome wolves (B. batra 15b), and of others it is related that they brought back bears on their horns (B. mez. 106a).

‎They leap (B. Kama 21b) and climb (ibid. 20a), and even the young makes playful leaps (ib. 21b).

‎Their food consists of fig-beans (Sabbat 121 b), fig berries (Ketuboth 111b) and especially of turnips, for which, when these lie somewhere high up, they climb up and fetch them down (B. Kama 20a). According to the Jerusalem Talmud (Peah C. 7, fol. 20a) also קנמון Κίννάμον [Kinnamon] cinnamon), which they eat off the top of the cinnamon tree (Rabba Bereschit fol. 73, c); probably the olive-like fruit of the cinnamon tree is meant here, see Rosenmüller (Alterthümer 4, 1. p. 272). Nor do they scorn barley laid out to dry (B. batra 36a). Once the goats devoured a raw dough, which caused their death (B. Kama 48a), probably through the swelling of the dough mass and the resulting obstruction.

‎The voracity of the goats belonging to the family Tarbu is notorious (ib. 23b).

‎[p. 125] Goats destined for slaughter were fattened (ibid.); one could promote fattening by tying up the udders, whereby the milk is lost (Sabbat 53b); yet this was sometimes done with the intention of not letting the milk be lost (ibid,).

‎The he-goat sires with the hind a hybrid, which some take to be the כוי occurring (Chulin 80a and frequently) (see §. 149). Goat and sheep can interbreed, because both species have both the same gestation period (five months) and the same manner of copulation by mounting (Bechoroth 8a). Precisely, the gestation period amounts to 21 weeks.

‎It also happened that the sheep bore a goat-like, and the goat a sheep-like monstrosity (ibid 3b).

‎Before one year a goat cannot become pregnant (ibid. 19b).

‎The usefulness of the goat is manifold. Its flesh is eaten, and it is brought to market as slaughter cattle (B. Kama 23b). From its hide shoe soles were made, for which the hide of the he-goat is especially recommended (Beza 15a); the horns and hoofs serve for the making of vessels (Chulin 25b); from goat hair shirts were made, which penitents wore for the mortification of the body (Mikwaoth 9, 2); such a shirt was called קלקי (Italian Cilicio); according to Rashi (Sabbat 64a), however, the word is explained by פיטראל, i.e., poitrail, breast-strap, with which foreign word he also explains the מסריכן occurring ibid. 53a; here, however, it is remarked that this strap was made of horsehair.

‎The milk, when drunk fresh from the udder, serves against chest disease (Temura 15b); the milk of a white goat is said to possess special healing power (Sabbat 109b). Against disease of the spleen the spleen of a goat that has not yet become pregnant is prescribed (Gittin 69b). From the milk cheese is prepared (Bechoroth 8b*), and the flesh of the kid, though inferior to that of the lamb, has a delicious taste (Keritot 28b).

‎It is related that in a goat, through drinking the water which smiths use for cooling iron, the spleen shrank and at last dissolved entirely, so that it was not found (Gittin 69b). By שטף של עזים (Rabba Bereschit fol. 74b), whose stench [p. 126] is very great, either goat urine is meant, or, probably more correctly, the well-known stench of the he-goat.

‎Finally it may be remarked that the blood of the he-goat is in colour most similar to human blood (Rabba Bereschit fol. 94, d).

### ‎§. 156.

### ‎The Forest Goat.

‎The עזא דבאלא mentioned by the Talmud (Chulin 80a) is the forest goat, called by Pliny: Capra silvestris (h. n. 20, 13, 51). The word באלא, which in our passage is also combined with תור (ox), Rashi explains by לבנון, i.e., the forest located on the Lebanon mountains; Buxdorf (Lex. p. 251) takes it for a forest which is supposed to be located in Babylon and to be called באלא, or as forest simply.

‎Münster and others take באלא, or בלא ברא for ברא (outside; thus Bochart [Hieroz. P. 1, lib. 3, cap. 1, col. 717] takes חורברא for “forest ox”); according to all these explanations, our Talmud passage means the forest goat, which more recent zoologists call the wild goat. It is grey or reddish-grey in colour, black along the line of the back and on the tail. The female has no horns. It lives in herds in the Caucasus, perhaps also in the Alps (Voigt l. c. 1. p. 414).

‎In the Talmud opinions differ as to whether it belongs to the class of domestic cattle and is therefore also fit for sacrifice, or whether it is counted among the wild animals, and its tallow is therefore permitted to be eaten, cf. §. 22 and p. 123, note.

### ‎§. 157.

### ‎The Gazelle.

‎In the Talmud passage (Chulin 59b) mention is made of an עיוא כרכם, "goat of Kerkus." Rashi reads: כרכות, and Karo (on Tur Jore Dea §. 80) throughout: כרכין. Most probably a local reference is indicated here, only כרכם can hardly be determined with certainty; it may mean Kerkusia, Cercusium (biblical כרכמיש*), Jes. 10, 9), Circassia, Kirghizia, Χερχίς [Cherchis] (Sachs: Beiträge etc. no. 2, p. 100); see also Luzzatto on Jes. ibid.; we shall therefore have to keep to the natural-historical features of the animal that are given, [p. 127] which are indeed suited to let the species meant be recognized with some certainty. The עיזא כרכוז, it is said, has horns which are הדורות and חרוקות. Of the various explanations of these determinations, among which Rashi, Tosephot, Sal. Adrat, Alfasi and Nissim are divided, we properly hold to the final result set out by the Sch. Aruch [=Shulchan Aruch] (Jore Dea §. 80, 1), according to which הדורות means round horns (not broad, as in the goat) and חרוקות ringed and notched horns.

‎The gazelle (in Linnaeus: Antilope Dorcas) has thick, round, black-ringed horns shaped like a lyre (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 419), and is probably identical with the עיזא כרכוז; the Talmud rightly counts it among the genus of wild animals and accordingly permits the eating of its tallow (§. 22). We add the words of Rosenmüller (Bibl. Alterth. vol. 4, Part 2, p. 171): "The chief characteristic of the antelope genus consists in the nature of the horns, whose bony core or peg is, according to the usual account, solid, without cavity or pores, and which in their outward form are without exception simple and slender, in almost all not flattened but roundish, or variously curved, mostly furnished in their lower part with projecting rings or spiral ridges."

‎We therefore believe that we are not mistaken in the identification of the animal.

### ‎§. 158.

### ‎The Aurochs.

‎The biblical תאו (5. B. M. 14, 5) Onkelos renders by תורבלא, which the Talmud (Chulin 80a) renders with שור הבר. Maimuni (Kilajim 8, 6) translates שור הבר by שור המדברי, "ox of the wilderness," i.e., the wild, non-domesticated ox. The Maimonidean expression is not identical with the Talmudic שור המדבר (B. Kama 44b), since in the latter passage it designates any ox that has become ownerless (see §. 160). The aurochs is counted among the genus of cattle (§. 19) (Kilajim ibid.), for which reason Rashi's statement (B. Kama 117a) that the aurochs is a חיה is not to be taken in the proper sense of the meaning of the word חיה.

‎It was caught with snares (ibid.); here the Talmud refers to Jesaias 51, 20, where it says: The תוא lies entangled in snares; on this Kimchi remarks that תוא, through transposition of the letters (as כבש and כשב), is the biblical תאו, but Ibn Esra considers תוא [p. 128] a bird, probably because he finds it strange that the strong aurochs should be caught with snares. According to Aristotle (l. c. 9, 32) it is killed with missiles; at present, when the animal is confined to only a few spots in Russia, it is caught in pits; yet there is no reason to doubt the method of capture stated by the Talmud, since even now the method of capture for many animals differs in different countries; how manifold, e.g., is the manner of catching the whale.

‎The translation of the biblical word (Deut. 14, 5 and Jes. 51, 20) in Zunz's Bible translation by "buffalo" is wrong, since the latter animal is never called שור הבר, with which תאו is identical; cf. the following article *)

### ‎§. 159.

### ‎The Buffalo.

‎For this animal no name is found in the Talmud, but later authors made use of several names to designate the buffalo, in which one has to think of a kind of provincialism. Thus the Tosephot (Sebachim 113b. s.v. אוריולא) say: ואותם בופלש שאנו קורין ראם, "Those buffaloes which we call Reem," but they themselves add that the buffalo is by no means identical with the ראם, for the latter belongs to the genus of wild animals, whereas the buffalo belongs to the genus of cattle.

‎Thus there was no special name for the buffalo, and another animal's name was borrowed for it.

‎Likewise some called the buffalo: [p. 129] שיר הבר (Mos. Iserels on Jore Dea §. 28, 4), which, however, is rightly rejected by Alexander Schor (שיר תבואות ib. §. 28, 6), since שיר הבר is only the aurochs; the addition בר fits the buffalo all the less, since it does not live outdoors like the aurochs, but is kept in stables like other cattle.

‎Others even call the buffalo by the biblical מריא (Karo on Tur Jore Dea §. 28*), still others call it טור (Taurus) and דוביא (Lorje in Alexander Schor ibid.). One sees from this that the buffalo originally had no distinguishing name, and we should almost assert that it was entirely unknown to the Talmudists, for in Bechoroth 8a, in the specific statement of the gestation period of many animals, the general assertion is made that the large cattle (בהמה גסה) carry for nine months, whereas precisely the buffalo cow carries eleven months (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 429), which the Talmud would certainly not have passed over in silence if the buffalo had been known to it.

### ‎§. 160.

‎Masc. שיר and תור (Taurus, Sanhedrin 18b), pl. שוורים (B. Kama 14a), and שוורים (ibid. 37a).

‎fem. פרה, pl. פרות (Para 1, 1).

‎The suckling calf: עגל הרך (Chulin 122a), then in the first year: עגלה and עגל (Para ib.), in the second year: עגלת בקר (ibid.).

‎The third-born calf is called: עגלא תילתא (Sabbat 11a**).

‎The fighting bull is called: שיר האיצטדין (B. Kama 39a), and the ox declared by its owner as free property (הפקר): שיר המדבר (ibid. 44b), which, incidentally, is of validity only in a regulation of penal law.

‎The calf is born with its hooves (Chulin 60a), and its tail reaches at once to the ankle joint (Bechoroth 40b), but later lengthens and extends further downward (ibid.). The long tail then serves the animal to drive away the stinging flies by which it is plagued especially at pasture [p. 130] (Sabbat 77b).

‎Although the cow can become pregnant already at one year, nevertheless, in order to obtain good breeding, she was not covered until three years old (in this sense one should take the passage Bechoroth 19b *).

‎The cow carries for 9 months (ibid. 8a). In a difficult birth one assists the cow by pressing the fetus out, or by pouring a handful of salt into the opening of the vagina, which as a stimulant promotes the birth. One also holds the calf so that it does not fall to the ground; if necessary one blows into its nostrils when they are blocked with mucus (Sabbat 128b).

‎The third-born calf is prized, because at that point the cow is at her best strength (Sabbat 11a), which is why such a calf was preferably served at banquets (ibid. 136a).

‎The cow likes to have the calf suck the milk from her, and so there was a proverb: “More than the calf wishes to suck, the cow wishes to suckle” (Pesachim 112a).

‎If one wished to promote copulation, one tied the cows' tail over the back, so that the vulva was exposed, while on the other hand one fastened a piece of leather in front of it if one wished to prevent copulation (Sabbat 53b).

‎Especially valued was the Egyptian breed of cattle, of which the oxen in particular were extraordinarily broad-bellied (Succa 21b), and the cows, before being exported from Alexandria, were castrated, so that the breed could not be obtained elsewhere. The castration was performed by cutting out the cows' womb (Sanhedrin 93a; cf. on this in detail the article: Egyptian Pig).

‎As regards size, the distance from the belly of the ox to the ground is as a rule 4 fists (B. Kama 51a); the measure of a full-grown cow from the head to the greater half of the trunk amounts to 2 cubits (Erubin 19a); in all, then, probably 3 cubits. The diameter of the cow's breadth amounts to 1²⁄₃ cubits (ibid.). It is [p. 131] natural that the various breeds also differ in size, and so the Mishnah (Para 2, 2) could speak of a dwarfish cow (פרה ננסת); one need only think here of the German breed, the Hasli cow, or of the French one, the Boefs de haut crû.

‎As regards individual parts of the body, the horns and hooves are as a rule whitish-gray, sometimes also black (Para ibid.); the horn core (זכרות) ends two or three fingers' breadth before the tip of the horn (Bechoroth 44a). The horn itself is formed in layers (like the onion, S. Ar. Jore Dea §. 80, 1) (כרוכות, Chulin 59b). Cf. §. 44.

‎In color cattle are either white, or black, or red. The white ox is preferable for the plow, the black for its hide, and the red for its meat (Nasir 31b). As is well known, Linnaeus excludes the colors of animals from classification and regards them as something purely accidental, whereas later naturalists regard color as the ultimate expression of the inner nature and character of animals (cf.: Die Farben der organischen Körper. Wissenschaftlich bearbeitet von F. S. Voigt), and the Talmud already had this more correct view; to this belongs also its statement that the black ox is wilder than any other (Berachoth 33a), or that white cats (§. 118) are more prone to bite than others, and it is precisely the determination of colors that formed an essential factor in Talmudic medicine; thus in it the dung of a white dog (§. 122), the embryo of a white she-ass (§. 165) and the milk of a white goat (§. 155) were used as medicaments, and it seems that white color was regarded as the analogue of the spiritual, as is well known to be done also by recent naturalists. A further inquiry into the determination of colors does not belong here; cf., however, also p. 55, note*.

### ‎§. 161.

‎Continuation. The ox is called the king of cattle (Chagiga 13b), probably because it was regarded as the most excellent of the cattle kind (B. mez. 86b); the owner used to call it “My soil!” (Sabbat 118b), which is why more care was devoted to its keeping than to that of the horse (Sanhedrin 98b). Its outward appearance was characterized as broad-bellied, broad-hoofed, large-headed and long-tailed (Chulin 60a).

‎Its usefulness, moreover, is extraordinarily [p. 132] manifold. It served both for food and for labor (B. Kama 46a); it was used for sowing, by tying across its back a sack filled with seed but perforated, so that the seed fell to the ground on both sides (Erachin 25a); at the same time it was led along the field.

‎Its chief use is for the plow, where it followed the call of the driver and became accustomed, to a regular chant of his, to walk in constant order along the furrows (Sota 48a). It is noted that the bull after castration (being more tame) is more suitable for work (B. mez. 90 b).

‎The cow too was used for ploughing (Chagiga 3b) and for threshing (Meïle 13a). The grain straw was laid on the hard floor of the threshing floor in a circle with the ears facing outward. In the middle stood the driver, holding in his hand the rope that was fastened to one horn of the cow; he then drove the cow around in the circle, so that its feet threshed the grain out of the ears*). During threshing one was not permitted to muzzle the animal, nor otherwise to deprive it of the possibility of eating of the grain; to this belongs the prohibition against letting the calf stay near the threshing floor during threshing, because the cow eats nothing on account of its bawling, and also the prohibition against keeping nearby a beast of prey of which the cow is afraid (B. mez. 90b). A chief benefit, further, consists in the milk**), which was protected from being sucked out by the young (or by snakes, see §. 134) by binding a hedgehog skin around the cows' udders (Sabbat 54b).

‎Of the cuts of meat, the tongue, dipped in mustard, is especially praised as tasty (B. mez. 86b†).

‎[p. 133] As a rule the cattle are driven to pasture (Sabbat 77b), but they were also fattened and given wheat (Menachoth 69a); their favourite fodder, however, is vetch (B. mez. 90a), of which, however, too heavy consumption could become harmful to the cattle; in that case they had to run a great deal until they were purged (Sabbat 53b). We treated the feeding of cattle in detail in §. 105; but let it be noted here that on the Sabbath one may crush carob for small cattle, but not for large cattle, because the former has thin and weak teeth and cannot chew that plant well (Sabbat 155a).

‎The ox is sometimes wild and given to biting; thus it once bit off a child's hand (B. Kama 84a). Its wildness is easily roused by the sound of trumpets, in consequence of which it becomes prone to goring (ibid. 37b); characteristic, therefore, is the proverb: “When the ox is lying down, sharpen the knife!” (Sabbat 32a). Tying on the hay basket also easily rouses its wildness (Berachoth 53a).

‎The bovine wore a covering over its mouth when it was driven out and had not yet reached the owner's field, so that it would not graze on other people's fields (Sabbat 53a); in Eretz Yisrael small cattle were even not raised at all, because they could easily go onto other fields and cause damage to the other owner (B. Kama 79b). In springtime the black ox in particular is unruly and wild, since the lush greenery makes it frisky (Berachoth 33a).

‎The bovine seeks to obtain relief from pain or discomfort. Thus in hot weather it is accustomed to rub itself against trees, especially in shady woods (Sanhedrin 18b); if it has been bitten on the back, it rolls in ashes; and when an ox once had a toothache, it knocked open the lid of a beer barrel, drank the beer and recovered (B. Kama 35a).

‎It happened that cows gave birth to malformed offspring resembling the ass (Bechoroth 5b), the horse (ibid. 6a) and the camel (ibid. 6b).

### ‎[p. 134] §. 162.

‎Conclusion. The cattle stall is called רפת בקר (B. mez. 32a) and אוריא (M. Katan 10b). The leather that was (unlawfully) tied over the mouth of the threshing bovine: חסום (Kelim 16, 7); the leather that was tied over its eyes so that it would thresh more eagerly: מלקוט (according to Meier Rothenburg: מקלוט); according to Maimuni, however, it was the leather that was tied under the anus so that the excrement would not fall onto the grain (ibid.).

‎During threshing it was customary to couple three to four bovines side by side (רבקה, Raschi: קופלא, Couple, Pesachim 26a).

‎The fodder basket tied on the bovine while walking: קלוסטור (Kelim 20, 1); the manger in the stall: אבוס (Kelim 20, 4), and אקורפיטא (Raschi: מנייטורה see §. 164).

‎As a goad one had a long, round staff (מרדע), at one end of which was a broad, sharpened iron (חרחור), with which the weeds were rooted out, and at the other end of which was a pointed iron (דרבן), by which one could keep the cattle in the furrows (Kelim 9, 6). The whip was called מטרקא, פקיע and מדרא (Joma 23a); the rope by which the cattle were led: מוסרה (Para 2, 3). Shoes were made for the cows so that they would not slip (ibid.); against flies a blanket was laid over them (ibid.).

### ‎§. 163.

### ‎The Camel.

‎For the one-humped camel or dromedary and for the two-humped one the general name is גמל, also גמלא (Rabba Bereschit fol. 84, c), pl. גמלים (Kilajim 8, 4) and גמליא (Rabba Bereschit fol. 41, d); but with the names גמלא טייעא “Arabian camel” and גמלא פרסא “Persian camel” one should probably think first of the dromedary, since it is found chiefly in Arabia and Persia (B. Kama 55a*); certainly, however, by גמלא פרחא “flying camel” (Jebamoth 116a) only the dromedary is meant, since it runs much faster than the two-humped camel.

‎[p. 135]

‎The female of the dromedary is called נאקה, or נאקתא (Raschi: דרומול, Dromadaire, Sabbat 51b). On אינקא see §. 520.

‎The suckling camel: הונן (Sanhedrin 52a); the young camel: בן גמל (Chulin 59a); the young camel that has not yet carried a load is called: גמל רך (ibid. 122a).

‎The camel that has gone mad: גמלא פריצא (Berachoth 54a).

‎The camel has short ears, which is expressed in the form of a fable thus: “The camel wanted horns, and so they cut off its ears as well” (Sanhedrin 106a). As a ruminant animal it has no upper teeth, but on each side a biting tooth (ניבי, which according to another explanation denotes the protruding gum, יינציבש, Gencives), which the young camel still lacks (Chulin 59a).

‎The neck is thinner in the Arabian camel than in the Persian (B. Kama 55a). The fatty hump is called חטרא, or חטטרת, which Rashi (Chulin 122a and Jes. 30, 6) explains by חולדרובא; for the conjectural explanation of this foreign word see §. 51. This hump is sometimes injured and suppurates (איישרא, ichoreux), for which reason it was smeared with honey, and for reasons of cost spoiled honey was used for this (B. mez. 38b). The tail is short, which is of advantage to the animal in that it does not injure its tail on the shrubs that make up its usual food (Sabbat 77b). The owner used to tie a rag to the tip of the camel's tail as a mark of identification; sometimes this mark connected the tail with the hump (Sabbat 54a and Maimuni on ibid. 5, 3).

‎Its usual food is, as mentioned, shrubs, since it is content with the meagerest fare (Sabbat 77b), but it was also given barley (Menachoth 69a) and sometimes spoiled honey (Sabbat 154b). In Arabia the cardoon (קרדינש, Cardon, ibid. 144b) was planted as special camel fodder. Moreover, it eats a great deal and is able to consume a kor of grain at one time (ibid. 155b). Cf. Aruch s.v. גרגרניות.

‎Its chief use consists in carrying loads, and since not all camels are equally strong, people used to say: “According to the camel is the load” (Kethuboth 67a), and the Arabian camels [p. 136] (גמלים של ערביא) are described as especially excellent (ibid.). From its hair blankets (Kilajim 9, 1) and garments (Menachoth 39b) were made. The animal is subject to madness (Berachoth 54a); it is also otherwise sometimes wild, and the female dromedary must be fitted with a muzzle (מזוי, Muselière) (Sabbat 51b). The camel also wears a halter (אבסר, ibid.) and a saddle (עביט, Kelim 23, 2). The saddle of the dromedary, which is especially suited for riding, was called אוכף (ibid.). The camel was led by a rope (Sabbat 54a). The fear of passing between two camels, or under one, was considered superstition (Horajoth 13b), yet it may simply have its basis in the fear of being crushed, in the first case, by the collision of the two clumsy animals, and in the latter case, by the heavy animal lying down.

‎According to the Talmud the pair mates back to back (אחור כנגד אחור, Sanhedrin 37b); differently according to Aristotle (l. c. 5, 2, 4), according to whom the female crouches down and is embraced by the male.

‎During mating (and in general during the rutting season) the male is very wild and dangerous (Sanhedrin ibid.).

‎A blind camel grazed off the grass only on the side on which it could see (ibid. 104b). It lives in monogamy (§. 40).

‎It has two callosities on the knees and one on the chest, so that in kneeling down it lowers itself twice, first onto the knee callosities and then onto the chest callosity; and Samuel b. Meier (רשב"ם, on Gen. 24, 11) thinks that the Talmudic expression כנגדו בגמל ניכר is to be understood as referring to the knee callosities; Rashi (Chulin 76a) calls the knee callosity: עצם בירך הגמל.

### ‎§. 164.

### ‎The Horse.

‎The generic name for the stallion, the mare and the gelded horse is סוס, סוסא and סוסיא (Megilla 16a); but the mare afflicted with a weak sexual drive is called רמך (Kilajim 8, 5 and Maimuni thereon), Arabic مكة [makka]. On רכש see the end of the article.

‎The horse is characterized by the following enumeration of its qualities: it has 1) a strong sexual drive, it is 2) warlike, 3) of proud nature, 4) not sleepy, 5) a heavy eater, 6) staling only little in proportion to its eating[p. 137]*) and 7) likes to keep to the sides of the road (Pesachim 113b).

‎As regards the first quality, it is remarked that the stallion tends to become lustful at the sight of a woman, especially when she hands it fodder (Ketuboth 61b); the lustfulness of the horse is, moreover, already emphasized in Ezech. [=Ezekiel] 23, 20.

‎As for the second quality, the courage of the war horse is known from the magnificent description in Job (ch. 39); the horse, wherever it was available, was always an indispensable requisite in war, and special mention is made of the masses of war horses of the Medes and Persians (Sanhedrin 98b).

‎The third quality, its proud nature, in that it proudly carries its head aloft with outstretched neck, is already attributed to it in the biblical writings (Job 39, 19); the proud gait of the horse may have been taken into account in Song of Songs 1, 9.

‎As regards the fourth quality, it is said in one passage that the horse sleeps only for as long a time as it takes to draw 60 breaths (אלנש, Halenées) (Succa 26b**); on the other hand Rashi (Berachot 3, 6) says that the horse has no sound sleep, but only a slumber-like one.

‎Because of its fifth quality the horse (rather than the ox) was driven out to the meadow (Sanhedrin 105b), though admittedly it had also found hospitable reception at the manger (מנייטורה, Italian Mangiatora) (M. Katan 10b); regarding its sixth quality, let it be remarked that undigested barley is frequently observed in the excrement of the horse, which the Talmud (Ketuboth 66b) had in mind when it reports that out of hunger a girl of Jerusalem picked barley out of the excrement of the cattle of the Arabs, and by this (ibid. 67a) the horse is expressly understood; finally, as concerns the last-mentioned quality, it is well known how both the riding horse and the draught horse must be kept in the track of the road by the [p. 138] bridle, since otherwise it readily veers to the side.

‎With reference to the biblical passage Zech. 1, 8, the Talmud mentions red horses (chestnuts, which occur most frequently, Bartenoro on Bechoroth 1, 2), yellow ones (שרוקים, duns, see Rosenmüller biblical Alterth. vol. 4, Abth. 2, p. 28) and white ones (greys, Sanhedrin 93a). A favourite adornment of the white horse was a red bridle (Chagiga 9b); the white horse is also an auspicious apparition in a dream (Sanhedrin ibid.).

‎In general the horse is one of the most useful domestic animals. "Do not live in a city in which one does not hear the neighing of the horse!" is recommended in one passage (Pesachim 113a), and its uses are manifold. Together with birds of prey such as the hawk, the falcon, etc., it serves for fowling (Sabbat 94a); from the manes and the tail hairs blankets were made (Raschi, Succa 20b). Non-Israelites also ate its flesh (Schekalim 12, col. 2); above all, however, it served for riding (Bechoroth 2a). Accordingly much effort and expense were devoted to the breeding of the horse; a master of the stable was kept specially for this purpose (Sabbat 113b), it was curried (M. katan 10b), and its hooves were frequently trimmed so that it would feel no pain when walking, since otherwise the hoof becomes injured and the horse is hindered from a sure gait (ibid. 10a). From this it follows that the Talmud, whose nursery was Asian soil, did not yet know the shoeing of hooves with iron. According to Bracy Clarke this dates from the 6th century, and although the Talmud received its final completion only in the middle of the 8th century in the school of the Suranic Gaon R. Jehuda b. Nachman, it is nevertheless to be assumed that the European invention of horseshoeing had not yet become known in Asia at that time. Striking, on the other hand, are the words of Raschi's commentary on the above Talmud passage; they read: כדרך שעושין עכשיו לסוסים שלנו "as we are accustomed to do with our horses"; thus the practice of shoeing must not yet have been general even in the time of that commentator. As Jacob Reifmann has shown (in Frankel's Monatsschrift für Ges. u. Wissensch. des Judenth. [=Monatsschrift für Geschichte und Wissenschaft des Judenthums, ed. Zacharias Frankel (Dresden, from 1851)] 1854, p. 229), this commentary belongs not to Raschi but to the older R. Gerson b. Jehuda. (I gave further proofs against Raschi's authorship of that commentary in the Univers israélite vol. 10, p. 31).

‎Gerson lived in Metz [p. 139] and flourished around the year 1030; thus that invention was still not generally widespread in the 11th century, and only the Tosephot (M. Katan 10b; s.v. למשקל) mention that invention.

‎When the rider had to flee, metal shoes were bound about the hooves of war horses, whereupon they could then hasten safely over all obstacles (Sabbat 59a). Scarlet ornaments were hung between the horse's eyes; superstition likewise hung a fox's tail between its eyes as a protective charm (ibid. 53a). On its neck it wore a band to which was fastened a ring by which it was led (ibid. 51b).

‎It can hold itself up on its hind legs, in contrast to the ass, which can hold itself up on its forelegs for some time (Sabim 4, 7); the opposite, but incorrectly, is stated in Sabbat 93a.

‎When a king died, the sinews of the hooves of his riding horse were cut, so that no one else should ride it (Raschi: אפוגרמט? A. Sara 11a). State criminals (Sanhedrin

‎26b) and enemies were customarily tied to horses' tails, whereupon they were dragged over thorns (Joma 69a).

‎The loud neighing of a horse once shattered glass vessels (Kiduschin 24b).

‎The stallion with the she-ass produces the hinny, whereas the mare with the ass produces the mule (§. 168). On the various objects belonging to the horse's harness see §. 162, and we add here that the bridle is called פרומביא, רסן, מתג; the bit עקרב (Kelim 11, 5). The horse collar was called עין (ibid. 21, 2, קוליר, Collier).

‎It is questionable what the רכש occurring twice (B. batra 152a and Ketuboth 55b) means; there it is said of a claimant who has more than one valid legal ground for his assertion: ארכביה אתרי רכשי "he makes him ride on two רכשי", and here we must return to the many explanations of the biblical word (Esther 8, 14). Kimchi in the Lexicon translates it as hinny, others (Levita) as dromedary, still others (Bochart) as breeding stallion, and others (Gesenius) as racer, of which the last explanation appears most suitable for the two Talmud passages. The claimant supported by good grounds rides on two racers, i.e., he reaches his goal quickly and surely.

‎On אחשתרנים see §. 518; an interesting natural-historical statement about the trotting horse is found in Midr. Jalk. Schim. part 2, fol. 122, c.

### ‎[p. 140] §. 165.

### ‎The Ass.

‎חמור (Kilajim 1, 6), חמרא (Sabbat 53a), fem. חמרתא (Berachoth 58a). The Avites (or Gavites) called the ass תרתק (Sanhedrin 63b).

‎A young she-ass: בת חמרא (B. batra 45a), a young ass: עילא בר חמרא (Sabbat 110b; עיל = עייר*); on the frequent confusion of ל and ר see my Aphorisms in the L. Bl. des Orients 1849, p. 302), otherwise also בר חמרא (Berachoth 56a), סיח**) (B. batra 78b) and רמך (Tanit 23a); finally, the Arabs (according to the Aruch, cited by Simson on Para 12, 9) are said to call the ass קנהל. Maimuni (ibid.), however, takes the word as the designation of a kind of basket that is placed on the beast of burden, in favour of which the Greek Καυθήλιος [kauthēlios] seems to speak; however, the Καυθήλια [kauthēlia] are pack-saddles that are placed on the Καυθήλιος [kauthēlios], the large pack-ass, so that both views find their explanation.

‎Very young asses are called: עילי וזטרא (Sabbat 155a). Finally, let it be noted here that according to the Massora the biblical חמור was written four times without ו (Berachoth 56a). For חמור there is also found חמורחא (Rabba Bereschit fol. 84, c).

‎The tame ass has long ears (Kiduschin 33a); it is further distinguished from cattle by not being so broad-bellied, so broad-hoofed, so large-headed and so long-tailed as these (Chulin 60a). Its belly is as a rule white (Gittin 45a). With its hoof it can hollow out a considerable depression in the ground (Mikwaoth 5, 6), and the hoof, as with the horse, is often trimmed, since it is easily injured in milling, for which the ass was used. The ass namely went around in a circle to turn the millstone (M. Katan 10b). It can hold itself up for some time on its forelegs, but not, like the horse, on its hind legs (Sabim 4, 7; the opposite, but incorrect, statement [p. 141] is made in Sabbat 93a). It eats without much selectiveness, namely shrubs, thistles, etc. (ibid. 140b); out of hunger it once devoured a fish (B. Kama 19b), and since in eating it does not, like cattle, let saliva run off, the ox may eat the remainder of the fodder that the ass leaves, but the ass may not eat the remainder of the fodder left by cattle (Sabbat 141a). It was also customary to give it barley (Menachoth 69a). For a very young ass the fodder must first be ground up (Sabbat 155a). The ass brays as soon as the manger is empty (Temura 16a), yet the scrupulous gastronomy of a (genuine) ass is well known (Sabbat 112b); and on this, see §. 36 at the end.

‎The usefulness of this animal is manifold. People used to say: “The ox is created for the plough, the ass for carrying burdens” (A. Sara 5b); it was also used, as mentioned, for grinding grain.

‎Its urine is turbid and milk-coloured and is prescribed against jaundice (Bechoroth 7b); likewise the eating of its flesh is recommended against this disease (Joma 84a).

‎The barley found in its dung (Sabbat 110b), as well as the embryo in the body of a white she-ass, have healing power (ibid. 109b). Finally, the whip for convicts was made from straps of ass's and calf's leather (Maccoth 22b).

‎Its bite is more dangerous than that of the dog, for it breaks the bone of the one bitten (Pesachim 49b), and once it crushed the arm of a child (B. Kama 84a).

‎It used to bray at the end of the first night watch, i.e., at 10 o'clock at night (Berachoth 3a), and its braying is so loud that glass vessels were shattered by it (Kiduschin 24b). It is proverbial: “The ass is cold even in the middle of summer (בתקיפת תמוז)” (Sabbat 53a), which is why a blanket is thrown over it, especially after the load has been taken off, so that it does not catch cold (Kelim 19, 3), and otherwise too it constantly wears a pack-saddle (בשטיל, Italian Basto, Sabbat 52b). For fastening the load a special instrument made of wood for this purpose was used (Kelim 23, 3). It bears the greatest resemblance to the mule (§. 168) (Kilajim 1, 6). Some fabulous statements about the ass have been given in §. 36. Finally, mention may be made of the Talmudic statement (Erachin 19a; but probably highly problematic) that the flesh of the ass has the specific gravity of human flesh, as well as of the statement (Bechoroth 19b) that the she-ass does not become pregnant before the third year of life.

‎If a pregnant [p. 142] woman steps on ass's blood, the children are said to become bald-headed or scabby (Ketuboth 60b).

### ‎§. 166. The Libyan Ass.

‎The לובדקים, or לובדקם, occurring in Sabbat 51b and Kilajim 8, 4, is explained in various ways. Maimuni and Bartenoro on the latter passage say: This ass is found in לודים and resembles the camel (in size).

‎Maimuni on the former passage says: לובדקים are a kind of half-tamed ass and stand midway between the wild and the common ass.

‎Bartenoro on the same passage explains: An ass that comes from the land of לוד; it is stubborn and strong, so that it requires greater watching than the common ass. On the other hand, Raschi and the Tosephot on the first passage and Jeruschalmi on the last explain: “An ass that comes from לוב”; the Talmud itself calls it חמרא לובא (Sabbat ibid.) and in the pl. חמורים לובים (Bechoroth 6b); since לודים and לובים are not identical, לובים is in any case to be preferred. Commonly this word means Libya, a country in North Africa. Hitzig takes it (Jesaias p. 643) as Nubia, through confusion of ל and נ, and indeed in the Jeruschalmi (see Simson on Kilajim 8, 4) there is a reading נובדקם; but we have no reason to abandon the twice-occurring reading לוב and לובים in the Babylonian Talmud, and we therefore translate with Münster: Asinus libycus “Libyan ass”, and not with Burdorf and others “Lydian ass”.

‎The Libyan ass is particularly suited for carrying burdens because of its size and strength (Bechoroth 5b), and because of its partial wildness it wears a breaking bridle (קיבצייל, Cheville, Sabbat 51b). In the Talmud this bridle is called פגי דפרולא, which Schul. Aruch Orach Chajim §. 505, 1 explains by ברינה (Italian Freno).

‎In the Mishna (Kilajim 8, 4) it is ordained that the לובדקים may not be yoked together with camels. This ordinance does not necessarily presuppose a resemblance between the two animals, as is already evident from the same ordinance concerning the horse and cattle; nevertheless Maimuni, as mentioned, assumed a resemblance between the camel and the Libyan ass, which is probably to be understood as regards size, perhaps also colour. In any case the asses [p. 143] under discussion seem to be identical with the Asinis silvestribus, of which Pliny (h. n. 8, 30, 46) reports that Africa possesses a multitude of these asses. Some are accustomed to add the males of this species to their herds of asses; they are thus already half-tamed asses which are easily domesticated, and which, as mentioned above, are taken by some for our לוברוקים; one could therefore also translate לוברוקים as “forest ass”.

### ‎§. 167.

### ‎The Wild Ass.

‎ערוד (Kilajim 1, 6; Aruch also reads ארוד), pl. ערודי (A. Sara 16b), fem. ערודיאות (Menachoth 103b).

‎On the whole it resembles the tame ass (Kilajim 1, 6), but it is more silver-coloured, larger and more handsome (Voigt, l. c. 1, p. 444). In ritual respects it belongs to the class of wild animals (Kilajim 8, 6 and above §. 19), and it is the only animal whose flesh below the hip bone (אנקא, Hanche) can be torn both lengthwise and crosswise (Chulin 59a). It was used for grinding grain (A. Sara 16b*), and its flesh served as fodder for the lions in the menagerie of the kings (Menachoth 103b). It lives on cereals, and therefore protective fences were put up around the fields and some grain was left to grow outside them, so that the animal would not break into the fields (B. batra 36a**).

### ‎[p. 144] §. 168.

### ‎Hinny and Mule.

‎The she-mule מולאה, pl. מולאות (Mula, Sabbat 52a), כודנייתא (ibid. 110b); the white ones כודנייתא חיוורתא and ימים, the latter name on account of the wildness of the animal (Chulin 7b). The last word, which is biblical (Gen. 36, 24), is explained by the Midrash (Rabba Bereschit fol. 92, col. 2) as המיונם, i.e., Ἡμίονος [Hēmionos].

‎The male is called: כידנא חיוורא (Sabbat 110b), also פרד (Chulin 79a); the female: פרדה (ibid.). The young: גירדונא ווטרתא (Nedarim 41a), for which Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf, Lexicon Chaldaicum, Talmudicum et Rabbinicum (Basel 1640)] (Lex. talm. p. 394) reads גירדונא (which in vulgar Greek is Γαιδαρο [Gaidaro]); in the Jerusalem Talmud (B. mez. 11a) it reads: גיידור קטן.

‎Striking is the opinion of the Tosephot (B. mez. 78b. s.v. במילתא) that in Aramaic only כודנייתא is the name for פרד.

‎Of פרד the pl. is פרדים (Jozar: Sabbat ha-Gadol s.v. כרם חמד).

‎The hinny is the hybrid of the she-ass and the stallion, whereas the mule is the hybrid of the ass and the mare; the former has a coarser voice, longer ears and a shorter tail than the latter (Chulin 79a). Although both species were created a priori at the first creation like all other animals (Pesachim 54a), they nevertheless cannot reproduce, since the she-mule has no womb (Bechoroth 8b); she is therefore barren (Sabbat 67a); thus a childless spouse was proverbially called: כודנא עקרה "barren she-mule" (B. batra 91a); cf. with this the Latin proverb: Quum mula peperit "When the she-mule gives birth", by which impossibility is ironically denoted; see, however, the following §.

‎If, however, the she-mule nevertheless comes into heat, one should satisfy her sexual drive by means of the male mule (Chulin 79a). In general the bite of a white mule is described as extremely dangerous (ib. 7b*), [p. 145] but elsewhere (Joma 49a) this statement is restricted to that species which is red overall and white only from the knees downward.

‎In its youth the animal is skittish and thus often throws its rider (Nedarim 41a). It is less hardy than the common ass and ages quickly when it has to climb up and down mountains, as in the region of Biri and Narasch (Erubin 56a and on this §. 132), but it was nonetheless a popular beast of burden (Pesachim 119a). The dung of the white mule (see §. 160) is used in medicine (Sabbat 110b). When urinating, the she-mule kneels down (Erubin 100b). Because of its descent it may be yoked neither with the horse nor with the ass (Kilajim 1, 6). Cf. §. 518.

### ‎§. 169.

### ‎The Indeterminate Mule.

‎In the case of the animals discussed in the preceding article, the distinguishing marks stated allow one to recognize whether they descend from a she-ass (hinny) or from a mare (mule); but if the said marks are so weakly marked that one remains in doubt about the animal's descent, it is called, as a separate species: פרוטה, pl. פרוטיות (Kilajim 8, 5). It says here הפרוטיות אסורות, i.e., one may not yoke together two of these animals of which one does not know whether one of them was born of a she-ass and the other of a mare, for this is forbidden in the preceding Mishna in the case of a hinny and a mule; by law one may only yoke together two mules or two hinnies.

‎Pliny (h. n. 8, 44, 69) makes the following distinction: the male animal born of the stallion and the she-ass is called Hinnus (to this corresponds פרד), the male animal born of the ass and the mare is called Mulus (in Aramaic likewise פרד); then there is a third kind, an animal born of mixed horse and ass stock but resembling neither of its parents; this is barren, and this third kind is probably what is designated by פרוטה. The word may perhaps mean "exception", "something special".

‎Pierius Valerianus relates (in Harduin on our passage) that in the year 1518 a she-mule in Rome gave birth to a foal, which was interpreted as referring to Luther's apostasy; likewise Scaliger relates (ibid.) that in his time a she- [p. 146] mule gave birth twice.

‎That third kind has no special name in Latin, and so פרוטיות may be merely a hardening of the sound for פרודיות.

### ‎§. 170.

### ‎The Pig.

‎חזיר and חזיר של ישוב (Chulin 122a), fem. חזירה (A. Sara 20b), and חזירתא (Rabba Echa fol. 68, d), pl. חזירין (R. Bereschit fol. 70a). A euphemistic name is דבר אחר, by which leprosy (Sabbat 129b) and coitus (Berachoth 8b) are otherwise also called; thus a spotted pig is called דבר אחר חוטראנא (Sabbat 110b), and the leprous pig (afflicted with measles) is therefore with double justice called דבר אחר (Ketuboth 61b, which Raschi renders by שור"שמין (?)*).

‎Its well-known uncleanness gave rise to the proverb: “Hang a palm branch on the swine (דבר אחר), and it will still do its own thing,” i.e., it will still wallow in the filth (Berachoth 43b). Its snout, which usually roots about in filth, is therefore regarded in ritual respects as equal to filth itself (ibid. 25a). Because it thus finds its food everywhere, it is called the richest of animals (Sabbat 155b); and since, furthermore, the swine quickly grows fat, so that the yield from breeding them is great, swineherds are compared with usurers (Berachoth 55a). Moreover, the breeding of swine was forbidden to the Israelites in consequence of a historical event (Menachoth 64b).

‎It most likes to eat worms and other small creeping creatures (Aristoteles Thierg. 9, 25, 3: snails), which is why its snout is often stained with blood (Nidda 58b); but out of hunger it also eats other flesh (Tos. B. Kama 19b), and it probably happened that it dug up human corpses (M. Katan 24a**).

‎Of all animals it is subject to the most diseases (Kiduschin 49b, which [p. 147] Aristoteles l. c. 8, 21, 1 also states). Cf. §. 484.

‎Its entrails resemble those of humans most closely in their formation, which is why prayer and fasting were ordained in the event of swine epidemics (Tanit 21b).

‎Aristoteles (l. c. 1, 14, 6) states this external resemblance with regard to the spleen. It carries its young for 60 days (Bechoroth 8a), and a young pig once ran after a sheep and suckled from it (ibid. 24a). The older it gets, the stronger it becomes (Sabbat 77b); however, as long as the sow suckles the young, she stops growing (Rabba Echa fol. 68, d).

‎The uses of the swine are manifold. Dried ham (Raschi: בקונש, Old French Bouchons) is held to be the most tasty thing (כל טוב) (Chulin 17a). The back bristles (§. 75) are used as tips for threads (Sabbat 90b), and tanners used its dung (Berachoth 25a). Non-Israelites used to smear cheeses with its lard (§. 92) so that they would acquire a fine appearance (A. Sara 35b*).

### ‎§. 171.

### ‎The Egyptian Swine.

‎In Egypt the swine are of excellent quality, which is why only castrated sows were permitted to be exported from Alexandria, so that this breed would not spread elsewhere. Smugglers, however, knew how to evade the prohibition and exported many a young Egyptian pig (Sanhedrin 93a). The sow was made infertile by cutting out its womb (מטריצה, Matrice), and despite the operation the animal could still live on for a long time (ibid. 33a). Aristoteles, on the other hand, describes the castration thus: “After the swine have been made to go hungry for two days, they are hung up by the hind feet, and an incision is made in the abdomen at the place where the testicles lie in the male. Here the female ovary is found, of which a little is cut off, and then the whole is sewn up” (Naturg. 9, 37, 4).

### ‎[p. 148] §. 172.

### ‎The Wild Swine.

‎The boar or wild swine is called חזיר הבר (Chulin 122a) and חזיר היער (Bartenoro on Aboth 5, 20*). In the former passage there is a dispute about the softness or hardness of the boar's hide; this difference can be explained by the fact that originally the wild swine too has a soft hide, which, however, it gradually hardens to be almost bulletproof by rubbing against resinous trees, cf. Voigt 1, p. 447.

‎It loves thickets and marshy regions (Pesachim 118b). It crushes its prey and tramples the rest with its feet (ibid.). Raschi (Kiduschin 47a) states that acorns (אלון = אגלנט, Glands; אייכל"ן) serve as food for the wild swine.

‎The leopard was (but erroneously) held to be the hybrid of the boar and the lioness (Bartenoro on Aboth 5, 20, which commentary, as is well known, has Simeon b. Zemach Duran as its author).

### ‎§. 173.

### ‎The Elephant.

‎פיל (Berachoth 56b), פילא (ibid. 55b), pl. פילים (ibid. 56b). It is the largest of all known land animals, hence the ironic proverb: “Does an elephant perhaps pass through the eye of a needle?” (B. mez. 38b). The trunk gives it an ugly appearance, on account of which its appearance in a dream was regarded as an unfavourable omen (Berachoth 57b); since, furthermore, the trunk replaces the nose in a most peculiar form, the blessing “Blessed be He who varies the creatures!” was spoken at the sight of it (ibid. 58b). It likes to eat grapevines (Sabbat 128a) and swallows willows and shrubs without chewing them (Menachoth 69a**). But however large and strong this animal is, it nevertheless fears [p. 149] the gnat (§. 432), which flies into the opening of its trunk and stings it painfully there (Sabbat 77b). In ritual respects it belongs to the genus of beasts (Kilajim 8, 6), and its gestation lasts three years (Bechoroth 8a). Aristoteles too states this duration in one passage (Thierg. 6, 25, 2), but contradicts himself in another, which was discussed in detail in §. 17.

‎On the etymology of פיל see §. 516.

‎Ivory is called שן דפיל, Hebrew שנהב (1. Kön. 10, 22*). The Syriac [?] corresponds more closely to the German word.

### ‎§. 174.

### ‎The Unicorn.

‎Just as little as the biblical ראם (Reem) has been identified with certainty, just as little can the Talmudic Reem be precisely identified. On the whole there are four main views on this. Some (LXX) take it for the unicorn, whose existence has been now asserted, now disputed; some (Bochart) for the gazelle species Oryx, the so-called Antilope Leucoryx; some (Schultens) for the buffalo; and others (Bruce), finally, for the rhinoceros.

‎This is treated in detail by Rosenmüller (Bibl. Alterth. vol. 4, Part 2, p. 188 ff.) and Winer (Bibl. R. W. s.v. Einhorn). Most later investigations follow that second view, and probably with the most justification, since not only does the Arabic رئم [riʾm], or رئم [riʾm], denote a species of gazelle, but the Talmud also has ארוילא, or אורוילא for ראם (Sebachim 113b), and the former word corresponds all too closely to the Arabic غزال [ghazāl]; the North African Arabs even [p. 150] pronounce the word Rgazál.

‎However, we have here only to examine the Talmudic view of ראם, and in this regard we must above all emphasize one principal point, namely the fabulous size which the Talmud attributes to the animal in many passages. As the extreme of size it is set in contrast to the nits of lice. Sabbat 107b and frequently elsewhere it says: “God tends and nourishes everything, from the קרני ראמים to the ביצי כינים.”

‎We shall come back to these names. It is further said: the ראם is so large that it could not enter Noah's ark; it had to be tied to the ark by its horn, so that it could keep its nose free and breathe (Sebachim 113b). In another passage there is fabulous talk of the size of an אורזילא בר יומא (an אורזילא one day old, so reads Samuel

‎רש"בם —; the Tosephot, on the other hand, read: אורזילא דבר ראימא “young אורזילא”) (B. batra 74b); in this passage the biblical התנינים הגדולים (sea monsters) are also rendered by ארזילי דימא, which Samuel in turn explains by ראמים שבים. Thus throughout, size is the characteristic feature attributed to the animal by the Talmud*), whereas the Bible ascribes to it agility (Ps. [=Psalms] 29, 6), wildness (Job 39, 9), and strength (Deut. 33, 17) as its characteristic. If we now take that point given on the Talmudic side as the starting point of the investigation, then one must most probably think of the unicorn, not only because, owing to its being so little known, it was the most suitable subject for fable, but chiefly on account of its size. The Antilope Oryx at most equals the ox in size and is well known in Egypt, but especially in inner Africa; this latter animal therefore fits the Talmudic passages in question too poorly. That size, however, was considered above all is evident from the Tosephot (Sebachim 113b; s.v. אורזילא), according to which in some places the buffalo (בופלש §. 159) was also called ראם, which surely happened only because the animal, on account of its size, in which it surpasses the ox, seemed suited to the name ראם; the Tosephot at the same time remark, however, that the buffalo cannot be the actual ראם, since it falls far short of the supposed size of the latter*). [p. 151]

‎Raschi (A. Sara 3b) too can translate ראם only as “a large animal”; one may therefore probably assume that the Talmud understood by ראם only the unicorn.

‎Onkelos uses it to translate the biblical דישן (Deut. 14, 5), which belongs to the clean animals; accordingly it must have had cloven hooves, which is indeed said to be the case with the unicorn (Rosenmüller ibid. p. 194). Its tallow (§. 92) is therefore also eaten (Tosephot Sebachim ibid.). Cf. §. 22.

‎We have only to press the matter of the name. Usually the animal is called ראם, or רימא (Sebachim ibid.), whereas in Sabbat 107b and in the parallel passage A. Sara 3b it is called קרני ראמים, which, however, is already biblical and a poetic phrase (Deut. 33, 17; Ps. 22, 22); likewise Oppian (in his poem on hunting with hounds: Κυνηγετικά [Kynēgetika] 2, v. 474) names the black horn of the oryx: Κερόεντα μέτωπα [keroenta metōpa]; evidently the Talmud designates with קרני not the plurality of horns but of animals, just as in German “Einhörner” means only several animals of the uni-

‎corn kind. The Talmud uses the plural of the unicorn as of the nits: ביצי כינים, for which otherwise אינבא is used; the אינבא arises from the ביצה כינים (louse egg, §. 451), so that that expression too is used of the mass, or plurality, of the nits. We must not, however, conceal that in Midr. Jalk. Schim. part 2, §. 845 קרנותיו occurs in reference to the Reem; but our task is only to establish the view of the Talmud, and the reading of the Midrash should certainly be emended.

### ‎§. 175.

### ‎The One-Horned Ox.

‎In three passages (Chulin 60a Sabbat 28b and A. Sara 8a) it is said: “The ox which Adam, the first man, had sacrificed had only one horn on its forehead.”

‎This animal cannot be identical with the unicorn treated in the preceding article, if only for the reason that the latter belongs to the class of wild animals and thus, as a species unfit for sacrifice, would certainly not have been substituted by the Talmud as that first sacrifice. In that [p. 152] second Talmudic passage the sex of the one-horned ox is even especially emphasized. Now already Pliny (h. n. 8, 21, 30 and 31) reports that one- and three-horned oxen exist in Ethiopia, and expresses himself thus: In India et boves solidis ungulis, unicornes “In India there are also solid-hoofed, one-horned oxen”, and we should probably think here of this Indian ox, although, to be sure, on account of its uncloven hooves it could not have been sacrificed according to later legal prescription. In any case it is more readily to be assumed that Adam (we of course separate legend from natural history) would far rather have sacrificed the easily caught and subdued ox than that fabulous, huge and unconquerable unicorn. The Talmud too, drawing on Ps. 69, 32, calls it שור פר, which fits that Indian ox better. It is therefore an error when D. Oppenheim (in Frankel's Monatsschrift für Ges. u. Wissensch. d. Judenth. vol. 4, p. 281 ff.) identifies our animal with the unicorn, just as he also erred in his interpretation of the קרש (§. 146).

‎Our animal is also mentioned by Aelian (Thierg. 3, 34); it says there: It is told that a horn was brought from India to Ptolemy the Second which held three amphorae (a measure in which liquid is measured); this was probably from an ox on which such a horn grows. (Οὗτος ἄρα βοῦς ἦν, ὡς ἐκπεφυκέναι οἱ τηλικοῦτον κέρας [houtos ara bous ēn, hōs ekpephykenai hoi tēlikouton keras]).

### ‎§. 176.

### ‎The Tachash.

‎Of the numerous interpretations of the biblical תחש, none has yet been proven conclusively. We content ourselves merely with mentioning them. תחש is supposed to be 1) the seal (Sebastian Rau), 2) the sea cow (Theodor Hase), 3) the badger (Rashi on Ezech. 16, 10: טיישון, Taisson), 4) hyacinth-coloured skins (LXX), or finally 5) a kind of morocco leather (Niebuhr)*). Here we have only to press the view of the Talmud. It (Sabbat 28a) gives three features: the תחש has a horn on its forehead, is multicoloured like the ferret (§. 130); it is in any case a clean animal, and the only doubt is whether it belongs to the wild-animal or the cattle kind. [p. 153] From these features, however, the animal cannot be precisely determined; this much is certain, that because of its own distinct name it is not to be confused with the one-horned antelope (קרש, §. 146), nor with the unicorn (ראם, §. 174), nor finally with the one-horned ox (שור פר, §. 175), and one should perhaps think of a variety of one of the three animals mentioned, since in the earliest antiquity each of them may well have comprised several species; yet admittedly one cannot proceed with certainty here. The statement of the Talmud that the animal died out (נגנז) and in general existed only in the time of Moses is evidently to be regarded only as ironic, since Ezekiel too mentions the animal. Entirely arbitrary and without any foundation is the translation of the word as zebra (Pinchas in the ספר הברית p. 68), since the תחש could least of all have been an unclean, i.e., forbidden-to-eat animal.

‎But let me be permitted to add one more to the many conjectures. Well known is the assumption of some that תחש should be compared with the German Dachs because of the similarity of sound, surely a mere play on words that hardly deserves refutation; but might one not rather compare the Hebrew word with the Greek Ταχύς [tachys]? Antelopes are, as is well known, swift-footed and lively animals, and our animal may have had, besides its ordinary name, not mentioned in the Bible, an additional epithet תחש. Thus the Lacedaemonians call the hare, besides its proper name, also Ταχίναν [tachinan], and indeed because of its swiftness of foot (Aelian: Thierg. 7, 47), and תחש could denote a species of antelope especially distinguished by swiftness of foot.

### ‎§. 177.

### ‎The Mermaid.

‎Bechoroth 8a states: The דולפנין (dolphins) reproduce after the manner of human beings, כבני אדם; Rashi reads: מבני אדם, i.e. they are impregnated through human intercourse.

‎Thereupon the Talmud asks what דולפנין are, a question which Rabbi Jehuda answers with the words: Dulphenin are sons of the sea. On this Rashi: There are sea fish, one half of which resembles a human and the other half a fish, called שריינא (Sirène).

‎One is otherwise inclined to relegate all reports about sirens, mermen, sea-women or mermaids to the realm of legend, and yet in the legend, [p. 154] richly exploited since Homer, there lies a solid kernel of natural-historical truth. In the "Beiträgen zur Literatur und Sage des Mittelalters" (Dresden 1850, 4) Dr. J. G. T. Gräße gives, under the title "Von den Meermännern und Meerfrauen," a detailed chronological compilation of the fish caught or seen from time to time that had abnormal formations and gave occasion to those names. The data extend from Aelian (who lived around 220 C.E.) to the year 1832. The conclusion of the interesting treatise reads: "Proceeding by natural means, one would then have to think of the sea-monk or monk-fish as a kind of dolphin, such as has been encountered and described by several travellers on the northern coast of Africa*). Likewise the so-called mermaid would in any case coincide with a fish frequently found in the rivers and lakes of Angola and Congo, called Ambize Angulo, i.e., the pig-fish, which has two short arms and hands which it can bend but cannot close as a human does. Their long fingers are joined by flesh grown between them, like the feet of ducks; the head is oblong-round with small eyes, a flat nose, a wide mouth, but no recognizable chin or ears. The males have genitals like those of horses, the females, however, two full breasts with teats, which are dark grey; below, however, they end like an ordinary fish in a forked tail. The back is covered with a thick skin, which is however perforated, and which they can open and close like a cloak. Their length is 8 feet and their flesh is edible and similar to pork. Evidently this is the fish which the Portuguese, as we have seen above, call Pesce-Mugger, woman-fish."

‎We see from this that both names, דולפנין and בני ימא, have a natural-historical identity, and only the reading מבני אדם, as if the partly human-shaped fish could be impregnated by human beings, forms the fabulous part of the Talmud passage. The other reading, כבני אדם, can be explained by the fact that dolphins [p. 155] (called "Tummler" by seafarers) are very ardent and amorous; they probably rather leap onto the ships (Voigt l. c. 1, p. 463). It is a fact that they like to stay near ships and swim playfully around them; the ancients therefore ascribed to these animals a sexual inclination toward humans, although the approach takes place only for the sake of voracity, since dolphins devour everything that is thrown overboard (Beumer: Populäre Naturgeschichte der drei Reiche, p. 151). We must therefore assume in our Talmud passage a mixture of fable and truth, on which cf. Kirchheim in vol. 1 of the "Zion".

### ‎§. 178.

### ‎The Whale.

‎While the biblical לויתן is translated by some as whale, by most as crocodile, the same word in the Talmud is certainly nothing other than whale. Bochart (Hieroz. P. 2. lib. 5. c. 18. col. 794) asserts that the Talmud, too, takes לויתן for the crocodile, but has for this only a single reason, and the least tenable one at that. It is said, namely (Sabbat 77b): "The לויתן fears the כלבית; now Bochart takes כלבית as a diminutive of כלב and understands by it the ichneumon (Pharaoh's rat, Pharaoh's mouse), which resembles the dog. The same animal is supposed to be also the כלבא טונא, of which (B. batra 73b) it is related that it crept into the air holes of an enormously large fish. Since, then, in that first passage כלבית is the ichneumon, לויתן must be the crocodile. But the premise is false, and therefore the conclusion must be so as well.

‎As regards, first of all, that second passage, in which Bochart reads כלבא טונא, text and gloss have אוכלא and not כלבא; so also read Ascheri, who cites the passage on Chulin 67b, while the Tosephot ibid. s.v. דרני read merely טונא without further addition.

‎Moreover, אוכלא טונא means whale louse (see §. 448).

‎As regards the first passage, however, Bochart cites the Aruch. He says: כלבית is a small, unclean fish that gets into the mouth opening of the Liviatan. The ichneumon, Bochart adds, belongs to the amphibians (!) and can therefore properly be called a fish (!!). One must indeed be astonished at the many errors that have crept in here. First, the ichneumon does not belong to [p. 156] the amphibians, but to the genus of the viverrids, the elongated small mammals. It is as large as the cat and even more elongated than the marten. It seeks out the eggs which the crocodile buries in the sand, without having to go into the water for that reason; indeed, it is even kept in Egypt as a house animal for catching rats and mice.

‎Second, the Talmud would have given it a name more reminiscent of its greater resemblance to the cat or marten than of its resemblance to the dog, which hardly approaches it even remotely; כלבית is rather the spiny fish (see §. 357), or, if we drop the above-mentioned statement of the Aruch, the sawfish, whose name כלבית we shall explain in its place (ibid.).

‎Third, it is incorrect that the crocodile fears the ichneumon, on which precisely our Talmud passage lays the emphasis.

‎Fourth, finally, Raschi and the Aruch cannot possibly have been thinking of the ichneumon, since the former explains כלבית as an insect that creeps into the ears (properly, the air holes) of the Liviatan, and the latter, as mentioned, explains כלבית as a fish that penetrates into the throat of the Liviatan; neither can be said of the ichneumon. Bochart's explanation is therefore incorrect, all the more so since the Talmud has a special name for the crocodile (see this article).

### ‎§. 179.

‎Continuation. The following reasons speak for our assumption that לויתן in the Talmud is only the whale. First, it is expressly stated (Chulin 67b) that the לויתן is a clean fish, for it possesses scales and fins*). This statement does indeed present a difficulty, for while the crocodile has no fins, the whale has no scales; in any case, however, one should think here of the latter rather than of the crocodile, for the crocodile, properly speaking, also has no scales; its skin is regularly divided into quadrangular fields which are fitted exactly against one another; those on the back and tail form into button-shaped humps or bosses, and thus can be identified with scales neither in nature nor in significance, since these lie not side by side, [p. 157] but upon one another. Also, the whale rather than the crocodile may have been called a fish; and how should the crocodile, as an animal furnished with short legs (שרץ), have been regarded as clean? Incidentally, little weight is to be placed on the talmudic statement about the scales of the whale, since it consists only of an artificial derivation from a Bible passage and can therefore easily be given up.

‎Second, the following Talmud passage leaves no doubt that in it לויתן must be the whale. B. batra 73b it says: "Once we were travelling by ship and saw a fish in whose air holes*) sat an אוכלא טונא (whale louse, §. 448). The sea cast the killed fish onto the shore, in consequence of which 60 cities were destroyed; 60 cities ate of it, 60 cities salted pieces of it, and from one eyeball of it 300 measures of train oil were obtained; and when we returned after 12 months, the 60 destroyed cities had made beams from the bones of the fish for rebuilding the houses."

‎Here we must above all note the conception of the enormous size of the animal. While the largest species of crocodile, namely the Nile crocodile, reaches a length of at most 30 feet (Voigt l. c. 3, p. 31), the true whale is twice as long (60–61 feet; Voigt l. c. 1, p. 471 **). Furthermore, the obtaining of train oil can apply only to the whale, and finally the whale, whose flesh, when it is young, has the taste of pork, is more edible than the flesh of the crocodile, which has a musky odor and is therefore, though edible, nevertheless unpalatable. Also, the whalebone of the whale is thicker and longer than the ribs of the crocodile, so that the embellishment of our passage, which is in any case fabulously exaggerated, particularly regarding the making of the beams, still fits the whale far better than the crocodile.

‎This passage is followed by several others, in which fables are told about the enormous distance between the individual fins of the sea monster, but from which it at least emerges that it must be the whale, since, as mentioned, the crocodile has no fins. Admittedly, Raschbam (ibid.) errs in placing those fins on the [p. 158] back of the animal.

‎Finally, the said passage states expressly that the fish had two fins, which is indeed the case with the whale.

‎However, the Talmud also designated a fabulous animal by לויתן, about which we shall speak in detail in §. 505. Cf. also §. 347.

### ‎§. 180.

‎Conclusion. It is questionable which cetacean is meant in the Tosephot (Bechoroth 7b; s.v. רוב). It says here: One may eat the morays (מרנש, Muraenae) that are found in the דג בליינא. For מרנש the corresponding Piske Tosephot have הרינגן “herrings,” which do indeed form a main part of the whale's food; but Balaena is the generic name of various cetaceans; thus the cachalot is called Φάλαινα [phalaina] in Aristotle (Thierg. 1, 4, 1); yet it is more likely that the whale is meant in the Tosephot, since it has no teeth, which makes it possible for the herrings pressed down through the narrow gullet to remain whole and recognizable, whereas the cachalot (Cachelot) has pointed and somewhat curved teeth and therefore chews its food.
