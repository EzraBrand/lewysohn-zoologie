# §§ 114–115: The lion, the tiger (pp. 68–71)

### ‎§. 114.

### ‎The Lion.

‎Generic name: ארי (Aboth 5, 20), אריא (B. Kama [=Bava Kamma] 40a) and אריה (Jebamoth 122b). The male lion is called: ארי זכר (Sabbat 150a), the female: לביה (B. Kama 16b) and גורייתא (Sabbat 67a).

‎From אריא the pl. is אריות (Chulin 3b) and אריותא (Sanhedrin 59b*).

‎The young lion or whelp is called: גוריא (ibid. 64a).

‎ארי is derived from ראה (the seeing one, Bochart [=Samuel Bochart, Hierozoicon, sive de animalibus Sacrae Scripturae (Lewysohn used the Frankfurt a. M. 1675 edition)]), or ארה (to tear off, to tear apart, Gesenius [=Wilhelm Gesenius, Hebrew lexicon (probably Thesaurus linguae Hebraeae et Chaldaeae Veteris Testamenti, 1829–58)]).

‎לביה from لَبَأَ [labaʾa] (to suckle, Rosenmüller [=E. F. K. Rosenmüller, Handbuch der biblischen Alterthumskunde, vol. 4, part 2 (Leipzig 1831)]), or from לבי (to roar, Fürst [=Julius Fürst, Hebräisches und chaldäisches Handwörterbuch über das Alte Testament (Leipzig 1857–61)]).

‎גוריא from גור (to suck, Fürst).

‎The lioness was called כלבא by some (Jalk. Schim. [=Yalkut Shimoni] 2nd part §. 721). The lion is the king of the animals (Chagiga 13b). He is the symbol of strength (Pesachim 112a) and therefore serves as a title of honor for distinguished scholars (Sabbat 111b). The call of God, too, is poetically compared to the lion's roar (Berachoth 3a**), which, as travelers in Africa relate, so terrifies herds and other animals that they then crowd together and, trembling, fear the king of the forests while he is still far off.

‎The mighty spirit of seduction to idolatry, too, appears in the [p. 69] form of a young lion whose roaring is heard 400 miles away (Sanhedrin 64a); this hyper-symbolism is in any case based on the truth that the lion's roar is very loud and far-reaching.

‎Other passages that denote the majesty and fearsomeness of the lion are: “Be a tail among lions and not a head among foxes!”, for there strength rules, here cunning (Aboth 4, 15); “Do not fight the dead lion!” (Gittin 83b), i.e., have respect for former greatness.

‎If one feared to approach an object, one said: The lion crouches upon the object (Schebuoth 22b*). His voracity was denoted by the proverb: “A handful does not satisfy the lion” (Berachoth 3b), and his satisfied rapacity, which he makes known by joyful roaring while devouring his prey, was alluded to by the proverb: “The lion does not roar over a heap of straw, but over a heap of meat (ibid. 32a**). He was kept in menageries and fed with the meat of wild asses (Menachoth 103b).

‎The Talmud seems to indicate the nature of the lion's voice through the various expressions for its modulation. When the lion is hungry and goes out for prey, his roaring at that time is called שאג, which is why God cries out over the loss of the Temple like the lion (Berachoth 3a); when the lion catches sight of the prey, or is already near it, the voice modulates and is called עוא (Chulin 53a); while devouring he growls (Berachoth 32a), which is called נהם.

‎The front parts of his body are strikingly more developed than the hind parts, so that he appears narrow behind and broad in front (Middoth 4, 7). His gestation period is three years (Bechoroth 8a); but erroneously, which was discussed in §. 17; it is 180 days, see Voigt [=F. S. Voigt, Lehrbuch der Zoologie (Stuttgart 1835–40); the text names it (p. 9: "Voigt, Lehrb. d. Zool.")], l. c. [=loco citato: in the work cited above] 1. p. 268***).

‎When he seizes [p. 70] prey, he devours it at once without waiting for its death (Pesachim 49b), which agrees completely with the statement of more recent naturalists, for the lion, like all beasts of prey that feed on living animals generally, thrusts his head into the prey and burrows in it in order to enjoy the hot food with relish.

‎Sometimes, however, the lion lingers among herds without touching them (Chulin 53a), or his greed abates once he has torn one of them apart (ibid.). From the wound he has made with his teeth or with his claws flows a yellow fluid (Arist. [=Aristotle] l. c. 9, 31, 3), which is called ויהורא (Chulin ibid. and on this §. 86).

‎He attacks humans only when very hungry (Jebamoth 121b), but in general never attacks two people who are together (Sabbat 151b).

‎Of his prey he sometimes drags a part into his den in order to have a supply (Sanhedrin 90b), or as food for the lioness and the young (B. Kama 16b). Whoever has passed safely by a lions' den (גוב של אריות) is to recite a blessing of thanksgiving (Berachoth 57b).

‎He is tameable (Sanhedrin 15b), but because of his dangerousness he is kept in a cage (Sabbat 106b), and trade in him is forbidden (A. Sara [=Avodah Zarah] 16a). He is especially dangerous in the mating season, hence the proverb: “Who would spread a garment between lion and lioness when they are mating?” (Sanhedrin 106a).

‎For all his strength, however, he fears the Ethiopian gnat (Sabbat 77b), which will be discussed in detail in §. 433.

‎Lion's milk is used in medicine (Midr. Jalk. Schim. [=Midrash Yalkut Shimoni] 2nd part §. 721).

### ‎§. 115. The Tiger.

‎טגריס (Τίγρις [Tigris]) and, by circumlocution: אריא דבי עילאי “lion of the forest Ilai” (Chulin 59b). Equal to the lion in size is the tiger, an animal little known to the ancients. Aristotle is the first to mention the tiger. He says: The Indian dogs are said to be born of the tiger and the dog (Naturg. [=Aristotle, History of Animals (Historia animalium; German title Naturgeschichte)] 8, 27, 8), from which passage one sees at the same time the inaccurate knowledge of the animal, since Aristotle attributes to the tiger the size of a dog. According to Aelian (Thierg. [=Aelian, On the Nature of Animals (De natura animalium; German title Thiergeschichte)] 8, 1) the tiger is born of the Indian bitch, which likewise attests the incorrect [p. 71] knowledge of the animal. Later naturalists, on the other hand, describe it as an animal most similar to the lion, thus Oppian (Κυνηγετικά [Kynēgetika] book 3, cited in Bochart l. c. P. 3. lib. 1. col. 721 and ibid. lib. 8 col. 792): “After all this you will regard it as a mountain lioness, except for the hide, which surrounds it with colorful spots.”

‎Thus Varro (De ling. lat. [=Varro, On the Latin Language (De lingua Latina)] lib. 4) also places both animals side by side: “Tigris est ut leo varius.”

‎Likewise, as we have seen, the Talmud too calls it, besides טגריס, also אריא דבי עילאי.

‎Rashi takes the word עילאי as the proper name of a certain forest; but perhaps the two words בי עילאי are to be read as one word בעילאי, which, like באלא (Chulin 80a), designates either the forest of Lebanon or simply a forest. Or might עילאי perhaps be related to Ὑλη [hylē], Silva? Compare article Ferret.

‎Münster [=Sebastian Münster (1488–1552), Hebraist; probably his Dictionarium Chaldaicum (Basel 1527)] translates עילאי as major.

‎Concerning the natural history of the animal, the Talmud has communicated only a little, and moreover only in fabulous garb. Chulin 59b it is said that the tiger is so large that the distance between the individual lungs is nine fists wide, which the Tosephot would even understand of the lungs lying side by side! This much can be gathered from the passage: that the Talmud ascribes to the tiger greater strength and fearsomeness than to the lion, of which at least the latter is correct, for the tiger is in fact the cruelest and most fearsome beast of prey (Voigt l. c. 1. p. 269*).
