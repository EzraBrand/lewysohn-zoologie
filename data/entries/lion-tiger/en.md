# §§ 114–115: The lion, the tiger (pp. 68–71)

### ‎§. 114.

### ‎The Lion.

‎Generic name: ארי (Aboth 5, 20), אריא (B. Kama 40, a) and אריה (Jebamoth 122, b). The male lion is called: ארי זכר (Sabbat 150, a), the female: לביה (B. Kama 16, b) and גורייתא (Sabbat 67, a). — From אריא the pl. is אריות (Chulin 3, b) and אריותא (Sanhedrin 59, b*). — The young lion or whelp is called: גוריא (ibid. 64, a). — ארי is derived from ראה (seeing, Bochart), or ארה (to tear off, to tear apart, Gesenius). — לביה from لَبَأَ [labaʾa] (to suckle, Rosenmüller), or from לבי (to roar, Fürst). — גוריא from גור (to suck, Fürst). — The lioness was called כלבא by some (Jalk. Schim. 2nd part §. 721). The lion is the king of the animals (Chagiga 13, b). He is the symbol of strength (Pesachim 112, a) and therefore serves as a title of honour for distinguished scholars (Sabbat 111, b). The call of God, too, is poetically compared to the roaring of the lion (Berachoth 3, a**), which, as travellers in Africa relate, so terrifies herds and other animals that they then crowd together and, trembling, fear the king of the forests while he is still far off. — The mighty spirit of seduction to idolatry, too, appears in the [p. 69] form of a young lion whose roaring is heard 400 miles away (Sanhedrin 64, a); this hypersymbolism is in any case based on the truth that the lion's roar is very loud and far-carrying. — Other passages that describe the majesty and fearsomeness of the lion are: “Be a tail among lions and not a head among foxes!”, for there strength rules, here cunning (Aboth 4, 15); “Do not fight the dead lion!” (Gittin 83, b), i.e., have respect for greatness that has been. — If one was afraid to approach an object, one said: The lion is crouching on the object (Schebuoth 22, b*). His voracity was described by the proverb: “A handful does not satisfy the lion” (Berachoth 3, b), and his satisfied rapacity, which he makes known while devouring his prey by joyful roaring, was alluded to by the proverb: “The lion does not roar over a heap of straw, but over a heap of meat (ibid. 32, a**). He was kept in menageries and fed with the flesh of wild asses (Menachoth 103, b).

‎The Talmud seems to indicate the nature of the lion's voice through the various expressions for its modulation. When the lion is hungry and goes out for prey, his roaring at this time is called שאג, which is why God cries out over the loss of the Temple like the lion (Berachoth 3, a); when the lion catches sight of the prey, or is already near it, the voice modulates and is called נער (Chulin 53, a); while devouring, he growls (Berachoth 32, a), which is called נהם.

‎The front parts of his body are conspicuously more developed than the hind parts, so that he appears narrow behind and broad in front (Middoth 4, 7). His gestation period amounts to three years (Bechoroth 8, a); but this is erroneous, as was discussed in §. 17; it amounts to 180 days, see Voigt, l. c. 1. p. 268***). — When he seizes [p. 70] prey, he devours it at once, without waiting for its death (Pesachim 49, b), which agrees completely with the statement of more recent naturalists, for the lion, like all beasts of prey that feed on living animals in general, thrusts his head into the prey and rummages in it, in order to enjoy the hot food with relish. — Sometimes, however, the lion stays among herds without touching them (Chulin 53, a), or his greed abates once he has torn one of them (ibid.). From the wound that he has made with his teeth or with his claws there flows a yellow fluid (Arist. l. c. 9, 31, 3), which is called ויהורא (Chulin ibid. and on this §. 86). — He attacks man only when very hungry (Jebamoth 121, b), but never attacks two people who are together (Sabbat 151, b). — He sometimes drags part of his prey into his den, in order to have a store (Sanhedrin 90, b), or as food for the lioness and the young (B. Kama 16, b). Whoever has safely passed by a lions' den (גוב של אריות) is to recite a blessing of thanksgiving (Berachoth 57, b). — He is tameable (Sanhedrin 15, b), but because of his dangerousness he is kept in a cage (Sabbat 106, b), and trade in him is forbidden (A. Sara 16, a). He is especially dangerous in the mating season, hence the proverb: “Who would spread a garment between lion and lioness when they are mating?” (Sanhedrin 106, a). — For all his strength, however, he fears the Ethiopian gnat (Sabbat 77, b), which will be discussed in detail in §. 433. — Lion's milk is used in medicine (Midr. Jalk. Schim. 2nd part §. 721).

### ‎§. 115. The Tiger.

‎טגרים (Τίγρις [Tigris]) and, by circumlocution: אריא דבי עילאי "lion of the forest Ilai" (Chulin 59, b). Equal to the lion in size is the tiger, an animal little known to the ancients. Aristotle is the first to mention the tiger. He says: the Indian dogs are said to be born of the tiger and the dog (Naturg. 8, 27, 8), from which passage one sees at the same time the inexact knowledge of the animal, since Aristotle attributes to the tiger the size of a dog. According to Aelian (Thierg. 8, 1) the tiger is borne by the Indian bitch, which likewise attests to the incorrect [p. 71] knowledge of the animal. Later naturalists, on the other hand, describe it as an animal most similar to the lion, thus Oppian (Κυνηγετικά [Kynēgetika] book 3, cited in Bochart l. c. P. 3. lib. 1. col. 721 and ibid. lib. 8 col. 792): "After all this you will regard it as a mountain lioness, except for the hide, which surrounds it with colourful spots." — Thus Varo (De ling. lat. lib. 4) also places the two animals side by side: "Tigris est ut leo varius." — Likewise, as we have seen, the Talmud too names it, besides טגרים, also אריא דבי עילאי. — Rashi takes the word עילאי as the proper name of a certain forest; but perhaps the two words בי עילאי are to be read as one word בעילאי, which, like באלא (Chulin 80, a), denotes either the Lebanon forest or simply forest. Or might עילאי perhaps be related to Ὑλη [Hylē], Silva? Compare the article Ferret. — Münster translates עילאי as major.

‎Concerning the natural history of the animal the Talmud has communicated only little, and moreover only in fabulous garb. Chulin 59, b it is said that the tiger is so large that the distance between the individual lungs is nine fists wide, which the Tosephot even want to understand of the lungs lying next to each other! This much can be gathered from the passage: that the Talmud ascribes to the tiger greater strength and fearsomeness than to the lion, of which at least the latter is correct, for the tiger is in fact the cruelest and most terrible beast of prey (Voigt l. c. 1. p. 269*).
