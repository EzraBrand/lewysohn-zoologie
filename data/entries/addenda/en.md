### ‎Addendum.

### ‎§. 509.

### ‎The Silkworm.

‎First, by way of addendum, we give the explanation of a passage in Rashi in which the silkworm is mentioned. In Menachoth 39b and Sabbat 20b three kinds of silk are mentioned. The first is called שיראים, the Seric silk, which, as is well known, the Seric merchants, who traversed almost the entire breadth of Asia, brought in processed form to the fairs at Nisibis in ancient Mesopotamia (Voigt [=F. S. Voigt, Lehrbuch der Zoologie (Stuttgart 1835–40); the text names it (p. 9: "Voigt, Lehrb. d. Zool.")] l. c. [=loco citato: in the work cited above] 5, p. 95).

‎The second is called סיריקון; this is the silk [p. 359] that the caterpillar Noctua serici spins. This silk is not as serviceable as that of the Bombyx mori.

‎The third is called כלך, which is also called כילכא and גושקרא. On Sabbat Rashi explains: פסולת של משי העשוי ככובעים והוא בית התולעת וקורין לו פולוייל ונופצין אותו וטווהו ועושה ממנו בגד "The waste of the silk, in the shape of little caps; it is the casing of the caterpillar and is called פולוייל; one separates and spins it and makes garments from it."

‎The foreign word is miswritten here; in the first Rashi passage (Menachoth l. c.) it reads: פלוציל, i.e., Filoselle, floss or floret silk. For when spinning itself in, the caterpillar first weaves a raw web of coarser threads, from which floret silk is made, then the silk proper, which forms a thread of more than 900 feet in length, and finally the cocoon, a dense texture which is used for silk wadding, a more flocculent substance.

‎According to Rashi on the second passage, the three kinds of silk named appear to be made from those three aforementioned components of the web. שיראים is the silk proper, כלך the floret silk, and סיריקין the silk spun from the cocoon; Rashi says: סיריקין is the soft, divisible kind of silk found in the silk, which in our place is called יר"ש. The foreign word will probably read ירש, i.e. Vers, the silkworms. Yet I would not wish to assert this interpretation of Rashi as certain.

‎Difficult and in any case corrupt is the Rashi passage on Menachoth l. c. It reads: [הוא בית החולעה] כלך, מעיל שעושין מאותן דגים [ במינן שקורין פלוצ"יל שמצויין כמיניי

‎A gloss already reads for כמיניי.

‎For דגים one must undoubtedly read דקים, for also for the הרג occurring in Tos. [=Tosafot] ibid. s.v. בגד, the Tos. Sabbat 27a. s.v. ונוצה already correctly read: הדק.

‎For כמיניי one should read במשי, as Rashi Sabbat 20b also has המצוי במשי, or else בנימין "in the threads", and if we omit the later bracketed addition entirely, Rashi means to say that כלך is silk prepared from the thin, i.e., not very solid and flocculent threads. Admittedly, it is precisely floret silk that is coarser] but it is not surprising that Rashi explains כלך differently here than at Sabbat l. c., since similar divergences in Rashi's explanations are not rare.

### ‎§. 511.

‎In Sota 42b it is said: The giant Goliath was בר מאה פפי וחרא "the son of a hundred פפי and one נאנאי." The word נאנאי פפי Rashi explains by פרשטי"רא, more correctly in Ain Jacob: פדרש"טרוש, i.e. Pédérastes, pederasts, and נאנאי by: father; the sense is: the mother of that giant was a venal person; she gave herself to many (a hundred is an indefinite round number; see Rashi) libertines, even to the pederasts who are otherwise contemptuous of women; only one was the actual father of the giant. Buxdorf [=Johannes Buxtorf, Lexicon Chaldaicum, Talmudicum et Rabbinicum (Basel 1640)] (Lex. p. 1350) did not understand this passage and translates פפי by Pape as a proper name. (A compilation of the most striking errors in the work of this lexicographer I shall give in Kobak's Jeschurun.) The glossator on Jalk. Schimoni part 2, fol. 17, d explains the word נאנאי differently, namely by כלב, dog. The Tosephot (on the Talmud passage s.v. מאה) take the word in precisely this sense and justify it on the ground that Goliath said to David: "Am I then a dog?" (1. Sam. [=Samuel] 17, 43). I believe that the word, like the other names for the dog נבחן and למם (§. 122), is formed after a characteristic of the animal. The dog is called both in the Bible (Jes. [=Isaiah] 56, 11) and in the Talmud עז נפש, or עז פנים, the impudent, the unruly one, and so it may also have been called ἀνήνιον [anēnion] [p. 361]; the α [a] was lost, as in פנטי, equivalent to ἀπάντησις [apantēsis]; גחמא from ἄγασμα [agasma]; קרקע from ἀγκροικία [ankroikia] and פטטרכא from ἀπόπατος [apopatos]; see p. 38, note **.

### ‎§. 512.

### ‎The Stone Bumblebee.

‎In the Midrash (Rabba Kohelet fol. 82, col. c) it is said: צלן של דבורים שאין בו ממש של כלום "the shadow of the bees is almost completely imperceptible", to which Matnat Kehuna adds: I found in the work Ot Emet, in the name of Rashi, the explanation that the wings of the bees cast no shadow.

‎We should probably think here of a species belonging to the bee genus whose wings, because of their light colour, cast no perceptible shadow; thus the stone bumblebee (Bombus lapidarius) is black with a rust-red rear end and glass-clear on the wings (Voigt l. c. 4, p. 279).

### ‎§. 513.

### ‎The Sea-Nail.

‎We treat here, as a supplement, the sea-nail (onycha). According to Dioscorides (B. 2. ch. 10) there is in some marshy regions of India a snail that resembles the purple snail (§. 366) and feeds on the nard growing there, whereby its operculum acquires the well-known fragrance.

‎Under the name שחלת (Exod. [=Exodus] 30, 34) the sea-nail was used for incense, since in mixture with other incense ingredients it lends strength and permanence to the scent (Rosenmüller [=E. F. K. Rosenmüller, Handbuch der biblischen Alterthumskunde, vol. 4, part 2 (Leipzig 1831)] Bibl. Alterth. [=E. F. K. Rosenmüller, Handbuch der biblischen Alterthumskunde (Leipzig 1823–31)] 4. 2. p. 455). In the Talmud the sea-nail is called ציפורן (Keritot 6a), corresponding to the Aramaic טופרא, by which the Chaldee paraphrase translates שחלת. The sea-nail or onyx shell was first rinsed in vetch lye (בורית כרשינה) in order to cleanse it, then soaked in caper wine (יין קפריסין) to take away the harshness of its smell, since in itself, when placed on coals, it at first smells like burnt shrimps, and later it smells like castoreum.

‎It is remarked (Keritot ibid.) that for the last-mentioned purpose urine would be very suitable, which, however, was naturally not done out of respect for the sacred purpose*).

### ‎[p. 362] §. 514.

‎We have often had occasion to use the fabulously embellished passage Sanhedrin 108 b; there the fire-worm (§. 499), the phoenix (§. 501) and the chameleon (§ 275) are spoken of; here we take occasion to touch more closely on one statement of it, which receives its correct understanding through a different reading that is to be adopted. The passage reads in context: האי זיקותא לא הוה אבא ידע מה אכלת יומא חד הוה יתיב קא פאלי רמונא נפל תולעתא מיניה אכלה מיכן ואילך הוה גביל לה חיזרא כי מחלע אכלה

‎אריא אישתא זנתיה דאמר רב לא בציר משיהא ולא טפי מהריסר זינא אישתא.

‎The first part of this passage, on the chameleon, we translated in §. 275, with which compare §§. 468 and 469.

‎As regards the second passage, it is understood by the commentaries as referring to the lion, which was nourished for 6 days (or months, see Raschi) by sunlight. I have, however, nowhere found this fabulous statement about the lion among the ancient writers, whereas Leo Africanus does say of the chameleon that it lives on the rays of the sun (see Naturgeschichte aus den besten Schriftstellern mit Merianischen und neuen Kupfern [Heilbronn 1774 f.] 6th section, p. 50); I therefore take the liberty of connecting the second part of our passage with the first by reading אי"דא (abbreviated from איכא דאמרי) for אריא, so that in the first part the correct natural-historical statement is made that the chameleon lives on insects and worms, while in the second part a fabulous statement is made, to which the fable of the phoenix is immediately attached.

### ‎§. 515.

‎In the Schulchan Aruch Jore Dea §. 84, Seif 15, mention is made of עופות הגדילים באילנות, of birds that grow on trees. We must not charge this ridiculous fable to the author of that work, but rather to the limited state of natural science in general at that time. Thus Bede, in the well-known natural-historical work Ortus Sanitatis, relates in all earnestness that [p. 363] the goose Barliata grows on rotten wood by the sea, from which it hangs by its beak until it falls off. The beak thereby takes the place of the stalk, and the goose develops on it like the flower on its stem. The Greek bishop Jacobus Athenienfis assures us, in the description of a journey to the East, that he saw such birds growing on trees on seashores and hanging by their beaks, as the pear hangs by its stalk.

‎Given such views, it need not be surprising that many a Jewish scholar also took the fable for truth; cf. ספר הברית fol. 62, p. 2. Moreover, only recently the superstition arose that the praying mantis (Mantis religiosa), whose wings deceptively resemble the lemon leaf, is the transformation of a plant leaf that detaches itself from the trunk, and is an animal; Voigt l. c. 5. p. 346.

### ‎§. 516.

‎The diversity of creatures and the great difference in their size gave occasion to designate the extremes in various ways. From the realm of fish the proverb was formed: לויתן בחכה הועלה מה יעשו דגי רקק "If the whale is drawn up with the hook, what are the fish in the mud to do?" (M. Katan [=Moed Katan] 25b). From the realm of birds, the giant bird (בר יוכני, §. 502) is set against the hummingbird (ציפוריתא, §. 328). From the realm of the four-footed mammals and of the insects, the unicorn (§. 174) and louse nits (§. 451) are designated as the extremes of size.

‎Here also belongs the proverb: "Does an elephant go through the eye of a needle?" (§. 173*). The Hebrew name of the elephant, פיל (Arabic فِيلٌ [fīlun]), may be related to נפילים "giants," since the elephant is the largest land animal. The skink is said, according to some (§. 277), to be called בן נפילים "son of the giants," though בן נפילא would indeed seem more fitting, since a single crocodile cannot be called נפילים; yet בן נפילים is formed after the pattern of עיר בן אתונות (Zechariah 9, 9), in the sense of "the son of one of the giants." The Talmud (Berachoth 55b) identifies פיל with פלאות "wonders."

‎Another proverb is: "In Media [p. 364] a camel dances in the inner space of a Metze (a small measure)" (Jebamoth 45a), by which boundless exaggeration is designated.

### ‎§. 517.

‎The last verse of the Book of Isaiah offers much of interest from a zoological point of view. It reads: ויצאו וראו בפגרי האנשים הפשעים בי כי תולעתם לא תמות ואשם לא תכבה והיו דראון לכל בשר. According to Rashi, תולעת is the maggot of corpses, and according to Kimchi דראון is identical with דירה (§. 413); but what sense would the phrase "their maggot shall not die" have? Must not the maggot that develops from the remains of the corpses also finally cease along with them? Furthermore, דירה is a dry, sapless insect, which is not the case with that maggot. If we add that Jonathan [=Targum (Pseudo-)Jonathan] strikingly renders that phrase by ארי נשמתהון לא ימותון, the question arises how תולע can be explained by נשמה. Pollak's explanation (in the Hebrew Jeschurun, issue 2, p. 61), that Ψυχή [psychē] means butterfly, and that just as the butterfly is enveloped in the chrysalis, so the soul is enveloped in the body, must appear far too artificial. I would read גשמהון for נשמתהון; the human body is elsewhere also called a worm, see Job. 25, 6; Ps. [=Psalms] 22, 7; אשם is (according to the explanation of most commentaries) a designation of the soul and thus in our passage the opposite of תולעה, i.e., the body. The sense of our passage is: The wicked, through the horrifying state of their being, will become an abhorrence to all beings. Their soul will not be extinguished and so their body will not die off; they will therefore find themselves in a state of agony. The translation of the verse would therefore be: "People will go out wishing to see the corpses of those wicked men; but their body will not die off, since their soul is not extinguished, and thus they will become an abhorrence to all beings."

‎כי here has the meaning of contrast, as very frequently elsewhere, and already Kimchi relates תולעת to the body.

### ‎§. 518.

‎While the Talmud (Megilla 18a) states that it does not know the meaning of the biblical אחשתרנים (Ester 8, 10), the word is explained by more recent scholars as couriers (Bensey: Monatsnamen etc. p. 188), or royal messengers (Fürst [=Julius Fürst, Hebräisches und chaldäisches Handwörterbuch über das Alte Testament (Leipzig 1857–61)] in the dictionary). The old exegetes take the word as the name of an animal, [p. 365] and we will here merely cite their views.

‎Rashi (on the biblical passage) says: A kind of swift-running camels.

‎Ibn Ezra takes the word as the name of mules, foaled by the ass and the mare, which are better than hinnies (§. 168).

‎In the commentary on the Book of Esther (which consists of excerpts from the commentaries of R. Jos. Kara and R. Sam. b. Meir and was edited by Jellineck [Leipz. 1855]) it says: "In a marginal gloss it was remarked that רכש האחשתרנים are horses with eight feet, which run swiftly." A stately counterpart to the well-known Greek Pegasus.

‎The Targum translates אחשתרנים by ערטליוני "naked ones," i.e., messengers who lay aside all superfluous garments so that they may make their way as unhindered and swiftly as possible; but perhaps in the sense that the offspring of slave women (בני הרמכים) were employed in the courier service only scantily and half clothed.

### ‎§. 520.

‎The second Targum on the Book of Esther (ch. 1, v. 2) deserves to be called a classic passage, particularly with regard to animal voices, which we dealt with in §. 38. We give its content here, since it is moreover of interest for textual criticism. Solomon's throne had six steps; on the first, facing each other, were the lion and the ox; on the second the sheep (אימרא) and the דיבא (if this reading is correct, the word means: wolf; in the passage cited further below [p. 366] the animal is called דובא and may perhaps also mean bear; cf. Mich. Geiger in the אגרת מלאכת מחשבת p. 19); on the third step the leopard (נימרא, as the commentary פששגן הכתב read, and which is also correct, as emerges from the passage cited below; in the edition Frankf. a. d. Oder a 5544 stands נשרא, eagle, as Buxdorf Lex. p. 147 also read) and אינקא, which Passegen ha-Ketab translates as camel; Buxdorf takes it for the name of a bird; in the passage cited below the pl. of this name is אנקין. If the reading נימרא or נמרא is the correct one, then אינקא is indeed more probably to be taken as a mammal than as a bird; on the fourth step, facing each other, stood the eagle and the peacock; on the fifth the cat and the cock, and on the sixth the sparrowhawk (בר נצא) and the dove.

‎Now if, the Targum continues later, a false witness stepped before Solomon's judgment throne, the said animals raised a cry, which is designated differently for the different animals: the oxen געיין, the lions נהמין, the sheep פעיין, the leopards בעיין, the camels בכיין, the cats טייסין, the peacocks מיללין, the cocks מקרקרין; of the wolves and the sparrowhawks it says מחטפין and חטפין (they rushed forward), and of the doves (צפורין) it says, as of the peacock, מיללין.

‎On the expression בכה for the howling of dogs see p. 24, note *. The word טייסין, which Buxdorf (Lex. p. 865) claims not to be able to explain properly, is the Greek θωύσσειν [thōyssein], which means to bark, buzz, cry etc.

### ‎§. 521.

‎The Midrash passage (Rabba Bereschit fol. 24b) is zoologically interesting. The garments of skins (כותנות עור) which Adam and Eve had made for themselves were said to have been either איגיאה, or אנגיה, or לגאי, or סיסרטון, or גלא קסינון, or צמר גמלים, or finally צמר ארנבין. Evidently the skins of various animals are meant here. איגיאה is, as Buxdorf (Lex. 21) conjectures, αἰγεία [aigeia], of goats; אנגיה I take to be derived from anguis, snake, since a passage was already cited in §. 288, note, according to which those garments were said to have consisted of snakeskin; לגאי, Buxdorf (l. c. p. 1122) conjectures, is the Greek λαγεία [lageia], of hares; סיסרטון, for which the Aruch reads סיסרנון, is the Greek σισύρνα [sisyrna], goat leather; גלא קסינון I take to be γαλῆ [galē], weasel; the addition קסינון means small, [p. 367] cf. §. 123, the same as גלאקטין*). The last two opinions take not skins but wool, or hair, to be the material of those garments.

‎In Midr. Rabba Kohelet fol. 85, d there stands (probably incorrectly) אלטינין for טיינין and גלטינין for גלאקטינון; this latter word designates either the small weasel or the sable, on which cf. §. 521.

‎דואר: in Rabba Schemoth fol. 133b, alongside the בן הפילים (skink) and the צב (toad), דואר is also mentioned, which is said to be a kind of toad; but there is still obscurity on this point, and the word still requires further investigation.

‎המיסו: in Rabba (Bereschit fol. 92b) and the Jerusalem Talmud (Berachoth fol. 12b) it is asked: What are the ימים (Gen. 36, 24)? One said: they are המינוס, i.e. Ἡμιόνοι [hēmionoi], mules; another said: they are המיסו, i.e. ἥμισυ [hēmisy], half-animals, which are half horse, half ass.

‎Aristotel. (Thiergesch. 6, 29, 4) also mentions two kinds of half-asses, one of which reproduces among its own kind, while the other is sterile (see §. 168), and these two kinds are meant here; המיסו is the kind that was first described again by Pallas in the „Neue Schriften der Petersburger Akademie“ vol. 19, p. 394 as Equus hemionos.

‎Cf. Frankel: Monatsschrift etc. vol. 3, p. 386.

‎חלזון; in the Jerusalem Talmud Sabbat C. 1. fol. 3b it is said of the locust חלזון that it has neither veins nor bones; with [p. 371] this word Jonathan (as Aruch read) translates the Hebrew צלצל, which denotes the whirring locust (§. 387).

‎ירודאתא. On the biblical passage (Echa 4, 3): Even the תנין give the breast to their young, the Midrash (Rabba Echa fol. 78. c) adds by way of explanation: These are the ירודאתא, which veil their face with a kind of covering, so that they do not see their young red while suckling and devour them.

‎The author of Matnat Kehuna explains the word as a kind of snake, which is wrong for the very reason that snakes do not suckle their young.

‎It seems that the Midrash was thinking of the marsupial, and that addition is to be referred not to the mothers but to the young. As is well known, the young of marsupials, when they are born, are still quite undeveloped and look almost like a naked little lump of flesh; the mother then puts them into the pouch, closes it tight, and now lets the young suck until they are fully developed. For the teats are located inside the pouch.

‎If we add the words of Rashi on our biblical passage (which agree with Midr. Tanchuma on Bechukotai), our explanation is supported still more; they read: Although the תנין is cruel, it nevertheless bares its breast when it sees its young coming along hungry; it bares the teat from its container, for it has a pouch over the breasts, and it stretches the teats out so that the young one does not shrink back from the pouch.

‎What is true and what is invented in that explanation is easy to recognize; the marsupials are cruel predators, and a single one strangles 10—20 chickens and ducks at once in order to be able to drink fresh blood (Voigt, l. c. 1. p. 446); these animals are now at home only in America and some in New Holland, but formerly were also present in Europe (ibid. p. 345), and their cruelty [p. 372] may have earned them the name תנין and accordingly ירוראא; both names serve to designate cruel and monstrous animals (see §. 299).

‎ירודות. In the Jerusalem Talmud Kilajim C. 8. H. 5 it is said that the ירודות and the ostriches (נעמית) are to be regarded as birds in all ritual respects. If we compare with this the Chaldean translation of תנים ובנות יענה (Jes. 43, 20) by ירודין ובנת נעמין, then evidently our ירודות and תנים are identical. We have also seen in §. 299 how Rashi explains תנין as a bird that constantly groans and wails, and the pairing with the ostrich strengthens us in the conjecture expressed there, that by יארוד the eagle species Perknopterus is to be understood. Aristotle (Thierg. [=Aelian, On the Nature of Animals (De natura animalium; German title Thiergeschichte)] 9, 22, 2) describes it thus: This bird has a white head and is the largest of all eagle species, has short wings and a long tail; it resembles the vulture and also bears the name Ὀρειπελαργός [oreipelargos] (mountain stork) and Γυπαιετός [gypaietos] (vulture-eagle), lives in groves and has all the bad qualities of those, but

‎none of their good ones; for ravens and other birds catch and pursue it. For it is sluggish, and therefore does not know how to feed itself well, carries carrion about with it, and cries and wails when it is hungry.

‎The quality of its size and the shortness of its wings explain its pairing with the ostrich, and the aforementioned wailing is, as mentioned, also noted by Rashi.

‎כלכיד. In the Jerusalem Talmud (Nedarim C. 6, H. 2) it is said that if someone makes a vow not to eat any דגה (fish, collective), then only the fish כלכיד is forbidden to him.

‎It follows from this that this small fish (as is expressly stated there) was usually sold in quantity, not individually. It is added: if someone says to another, "Buy me a fish," the other will certainly not understand a כלכיד by this.

‎This is probably the small herring species Χαλκίς [Chalkis], which is mentioned in [p. 373] Aelian (Thierg. 1, 58) together with the small Mainis (Μαινὶς ὀλίγη [Mainis oligē]).

‎מוסכין (Jerusalem Talmud Berachoth ch. 6), the same as מושק (§. 150).

‎מרטיסא, see חוורנם.

‎סיליי. We have already shown in §§. 285 and 286, from a Midrash passage, that כילי is the tortoise and סילי the crab; they are also mentioned in the Jerusalem Talmud (Schekalim C. 6, H. 2). It says there: A spring issued forth within the Temple; the stream of water formed from it was, from the Holy of Holies to the curtain, like the thickness or breadth of the קרני סיליי וכיליי (horns, palps, feelers); from there to the golden altar it was like the קרני חגבים (locust palps, §. 384); from there to the vestibule like the warp thread of the weaver, and from there to the threshold of the Temple like the weft thread; from there, finally, like the mouth of a jug*).

‎We note above all that this mention of קרני סיליי וכיליי is entirely absent in the parallel passage Joma (Babylonian 77b); likewise the addition וכיליי is missing in the Jerusalem tractate Schekalim that is appended to the Babylonian Talmud, both in the Amsterdam edition (of 1645) and in the Basel edition; and indeed it is strange that precisely for the first part of the stream of water two objects of comparison (סיליי וכיליי) are used, whereas for all the other parts only one object of comparison (חגבים, חוט, פך) is used.

‎But even if we retain the old reading, the question in any case arises what is to be understood by כיליי and סיליי. The commentary דרבוני זהב (Fürth 1785, 4), written specifically on the tractate Schekalim, explains כיליי as יבחושין (gnats) and סיליי as סדק של זרע לוף "husk of onion seed," whereby it mistakenly confused our word with סליון.

‎Without doubt we must here likewise think of the tortoise and the crab. [p. 374] The palps of the crab are generally known, but some tortoises too, e.g. the alligator snapping turtle (Voigt l. c. 3. p. 88), have on the beak two barbels which are specially developed for feeling (Voigt l. c. 1. p. 57), and both these barbels and those palps are here called קרנים.

‎Admittedly, both, as far as they are known to us, are stouter than locust palps, which in our passage are supposed to surpass them in stoutness; yet we know the Oriental species of locusts too little to have definite ideas of the size and stoutness of their individual body parts.

‎סלפיתא. In the Jerusalem Tam. A. Sara C. 2, H. 10 the סלפיתא is determined to be unclean, i.e., an animal forbidden to be eaten; this is the tortoise, which in Arabic is called سُلَحْفَاة [sulaḥfāh] and in Syriac ܫܠܚܦܬܐ [שלחפתא]; see §. 285.

‎עכברא דטור, "rat of the mountain." In the Jerusalem Talmud Sabbat C. 1. fol. 3b the metamorphosis already mentioned above (§. 119) is discussed; here it says: "from the עכברא דטור a pig comes into being"; the marmot is still today called by many the mountain rat (by Linnaeus: Mus marmota), and resembles the pig insofar as its nose and lips are blunted as in the pig.

‎פאפליון. In Bamidbar Rabba fol. 247, d it says: Make a פאפליון for the king's daughter, i.e. a pavilion, a tent erected in the form of outspread butterfly wings, from the Latin Papilio.

‎The form of animal shapes gave their names to other objects made after them; thus Bereschit Rabba fol. 67a designates by קונכי a precious object shaped like a shell, from Κόγχη [Konchē], the shell.

‎Thus a Roman legion was called alauda because its helmets were shaped at the top in the manner of the lark's crest (Harduin on Plin. h. n. [=Pliny the Elder, Natural History (Naturalis historia)] 11, 37, 44).

Rabba (Wajikra f. 178, c; Bamidb. 237, c and 240b) the phrase occurs: צפעון מפריש בין מיתה לחיים "the צפעון decides between death and life," by which the malignancy of the basilisk is indicated; indeed [p. 375] this genus is described as the most venomous snake, see Rosenmüller (Alterth. ‎שבלון. In the Jerusalem Talmud (Berachoth C. 9. fol. 13, c) there is found in greater detail the passage, also occurring in Sabbat (Babylonian 77b), on the usefulness of the lowly animals; there it says: The שבלון serves against חיוות; the fly against the sting of hornets (§. 426), the bedbug against leeches (עלוקתה*), the snake against scabies (§. 292).

‎I am of the opinion that the above two words are corrupted from שבליל and כחית; cf. the passage in Sabbat 77b and §. 361.

‎תולעתא. In the Jerusalem Talmud (Sabbat C. 1. fol. 3b) it is said: In seven years the תולעתא דסוסייא (maggot from the carcass of the horse) becomes אירעי (hornets), and the דתורתא (maggot from the ox) becomes דברי (bees); with this cf. Aelian (Thiergesch. 1, 28), who says the same of the wasps (Σφῆκες [Sphēkes]) and (ibid. 2, 57) of the bees (Μέλισσαι [Melissai]); the latter, according to an ancient belief, are said to arise from the remains of the carcass of the ox. On the various opinions concerning the origin of the bees, cf. also Aristotle (Thierg. 5, 18); in accordance with that legend, that the bees arise from the putrefaction of oxen, they are also called in Greek Βούπαιδες [Boupaides], Βουγενές [Bougenes] and Βουποιητοί [Boupoiētoi].

‎In our passage it further says: רב פדה חד אפר מתעביד שר מומית' דרוש' מתעביד עקרבי ודמניא שממי, words which I am unable to explain; they will presumably read differently in a correct edition. Should דמיא perhaps be read [p. 376] for דמיא? The Μαῖα [Maia] (Aristotle l. c. 4, 2, 2) is the sea crab, which some call the sea spider because of its resemblance to the spider. Thus in French it is called Araignée de mer; the Ligurians called it outright Aranea; see Scaliger on Aristot. 4, 3, 2, and thus that fabulous notion of the metamorphosis may have arisen.
