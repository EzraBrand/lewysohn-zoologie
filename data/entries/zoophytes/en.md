# Eighth class: Zoophytes (pp. 342–349)

### ‎Eighth Class.

### ‎Zoophytes.

### ‎§. 489.

### ‎The Sponge.

‎Already in the most ancient times the sponge was considered a zoophyte. Aristotle (loc. cit. 1, 1, 8) says: The sponge, too, seems to have some sensation; at least this is suggested by the circumstance that, as is said, it is harder to tear loose if one does not approach it quietly enough.

‎Aelian (loc. cit. 8, 16) attributes the movements of the sponge to the little animals [p. 343] that dwell in its cavities.

‎Furthermore Aristotle reports (l. c. [=loco citato: in the work cited above] 5, 14, 2, whom Pliny h. n. [=Pliny the Elder, Natural History (Naturalis historia)] 9, 45, 69 copies) that there are three kinds of sponges, a loose one, a denser one and a very dense one. The Talmud, too, has three names for sponges, by which those three kinds are probably designated, though without our being able to determine them precisely.

### ‎§. 490.

### ‎A Kind of Sponge.

‎Sanhedrin 108b relates: The contemporaries of the Flood asked Noah: Old man, what is the ark for? He replied: God will bring a flood upon you.

‎What sort of flood is this to be? they asked. Is it to consist of fire? Against that we have as a protection the fire-worm (§. 499); or of water? Where is this to come from? If from the earth, we shall cover the earth with iron plates; if from heaven, we have as a protection the עקוב, which some also call עקוש.

‎Aruch explains the word as sponge, which those wicked people intended to place on their heads in order to catch the rain.

‎What is meant here is one of those sponges that have hollows on one surface, so that one can put one's head into them; perhaps Alcyonium cydonium (Voigt [=F. S. Voigt, Lehrbuch der Zoologie (Stuttgart 1835–40); the text names it (p. 9: "Voigt, Lehrb. d. Zool.")] l. c. 6. p. 172).

### ‎§. 491.

### ‎A Kind of Sponge.

‎Of the עוק, for which Aruch reads עדק, it is said that one dips it in oil and then lays it on the forehead of the shorn sheep so that it does not catch cold (Sabbat 54 b, and on this above p. 120). Here too Aruch explains the word as sponge; Rashi explains it as cotton (צמר גפן); if the explanation of Aruch (with which Münster [=Sebastian Münster (1488–1552), Hebraist; probably his Dictionarium Chaldaicum (Basel 1527)], Aruch, s.v. עדק also agrees) is correct, we must think here of a loose sponge.

‎Two sponges (עוקין) were used in the difficult lambing of a sheep, by placing them on its forehead and its belly, so that it would be warmed and give birth more easily (ibid.).

### ‎[p. 344] §. 492.

### ‎A Kind of Sponge.

‎Most frequently occurs ספוג, for which Nidda 62a also has צפון. The latter Rashi explains by שבון, Savon; the former is the Greek Σπόγγος [spongos], or Σπογγιά [spongia].

‎Rashi (on B. Kama [=Bava Kamma] 93b) also explains צפון by שיון, which is likewise Savon. The צפון was used to wipe off paints (ibid.).

‎Of ספוג it is said: The diligent pupil resembles the ספוג (Aboth 5, 15). Maimonides explains: ספוג is צמר הים שבולעת הכל „the wool of the sea, which swallows everything.“

‎As is well known, in the cavities of sponges are found mussels, snails, crabs and other zoophytes, which the ancients (though erroneously) believed served as food for the sponges.

‎Here Rashi explains the word by שפונגא, Spongia.

‎At Kelim 9, 4, where a sponge that has absorbed water is spoken of, Maimonides explains it by אספונגא, Spanish Esponia.

‎On a martyr's breast was laid a sponge dipped in water, so that he would die slowly while burning (A. Sara [=Avodah Zarah] 18a). Here the sponges are called ספוגים של צמר; these are dense, strong sponges, called by Aristotle the Achillean, since Achilles placed it under his helmet so that a blow upon it would resound less; this sponge seems also to be meant at Sabbat 134b, since it is said there that the dry sponge was used to lay on a wound so that it would not rub against the clothes.

### ‎§. 493.

### ‎Coral.

‎Metal rings were made with set coral stones, or they were made of this itself (Kelim 13b). Coral is here called אלמוג and is probably the precious coral (Corallium rubrum, Voigt l. c. 6. p. 151). Maimonides says: אלמוג is קורל (Spanish Coral), which grows on the sea floor, where it is still very soft; when it comes into the air, it becomes stony.

‎In R. ha-Schana [=Rosh Hashanah] the word is taken in the pl. אלמוגין and explained by כסיתא, to which Rashi adds: A kind of tree that grows on the sea floor and is called קוראלו*).

‎Here one must all the more think of the [p. 345] precious coral, since in its branching form (see Oken's Atlas, Polyp plate 2, 2nd tribe, Fig. 1) it is rightly called a tree.

‎At Ketuboth 98a too Rashi explains כמ־תא as corals, which had a value of 4—600 Sus; probably those of dark red colour, which, hard as marble, are the most expensive and most valuable coral.

‎Cf. Rashi on 1. B. Kön. [=Kings (German Könige)] 10, 11 and Pseudo-Rashi on 2 Chr. 9, 10; in both biblical passages the variants are אלגומים and אלמוגים.

‎A string of coral is perhaps called חומרי פחכייהא, see Rashi on Kiduschin 9a. and below §. 495.

### ‎§. 494.

### ‎The Earth-Mouse.

‎Chulin 126b speaks, in a ritual connection, of a mouse that consists half of earth and half of flesh. Rashi understands it as though one side (right or left) consisted of earth, the other of flesh; but incorrectly, for this creature is divided, as regards its composition, into the front and the hind part, since the head, breast and forefeet are fleshy, while the rest of the body is of earth.

‎Maimonides (on the above Mishnah passage) says: The coming into being of the mouse from mere earth, so that one part consists of flesh and the other of earth and clay, is a well-known matter, and exceedingly many people (אין מספר לרוב; Heller needlessly deletes the word אין) have seen it and told me of it; although such a coming into being of living beings is most remarkable and its cause unknown to me.

‎Lipmann Heller (ibid. 9, 6) does not venture to express himself at length, but throws out the words: וזה לדעתי טענה גדולה על מאמיני הקדמות „This, in my opinion, is a significant objection against those who believe in the primordial origin.“

‎Heller takes it to mean primordial creation by God, since it might well be possible that living beings originally developed out of the earthy matter.

‎But who created the primordial matter, the earth, out of which the further development was to take place? It cannot, however, be our intention to enter here into a metaphysical inquiry, since our [p. 346] task is rather to demonstrate the existence of that zoophytic creature, and for this there are many weighty witnesses. Diodorus Siculus (Bibliothek B. 1, ch. 10) says: The Egyptians maintain that the first human beings came into being in Egypt, by virtue of the good climate of the country and the fertility of the Nile. The first origin of animals among them they prove by the fact that even now in the Thebaid, at certain times, the land produces so many and such large mice that those who see it marvel at it. Some of them are formed as far as the breast and the forefeet and acquire motion, while the remaining part of the body is still a clod of earth*). Aelian (Thierg. [=Aelian, On the Nature of Animals (De natura animalium; German title Thiergeschichte)] 2, 56) says: As I hear, when hail and rain fall in the Thebaid, mice come into being which consist partly of muddy filth, partly of flesh**).

‎Aelian speaks further and in more detail about this zoophyte in book 6, ch. 41.

‎Jacobs, in the scholia on the first passage, also cites Basilios (the Saint, who lived in the 4th century of the Christian era and was thus a contemporary of the Talmudists), who says: Around Thebes and Egypt the ground fills with earth-mice as soon as it rains heavily in the hot days***); he further cites Pliny h. n. 9, 58, 84†), Plutarch part 2, p. 637 and Philopon. in Gen. [=Genesis] ch. 1, p. 196; cf. also Ovid (Metam. 1, v. 422 ff.). With so many witnesses, the Talmud can surely not be reproached for speaking in definite terms of this zoophyte, and Leopold Löw (see Ha-Chalutz part 3, p. 168) would have done well to read the above sources before losing himself in irony about the Talmud.

‎(Appendix.) In Sanhedrin 91a it is said: A doubter of the resurrection of the dead was told to go into the field and see how the mouse, today half of flesh and half [p. 347] of earth, is already wholly flesh on the very next day.

‎Here Rashi says: “The mouse is called אשקוריל”; but this foreign word can only be the French Ecureuil, which however means squirrel (§. 140), as it is also used in this sense by Gerson in the passage cited there. Rashi knew neither our zoophyte nor the nature of the dormouse, which is why this foreign word is also inapplicable to the latter animal (A. Sara 68b).

‎The statement that the mouse detaches itself from the earth and becomes wholly flesh I have found mentioned nowhere.

### ‎§. 495.

### ‎The Pearl Mussel.

‎We treat the pearl mussel at this point, and the fan mussel in the following article, because both attach themselves by their byssus, either, like the former, to the bottom of the sea, or, like the latter, to stones, and thus in a certain sense belong to the zoophytes; we have not, however, treated them above among the shellfish or molluscs, because they are not mentioned as animals in the Talmud; it mentions only the pearl, and only in a mineralogical respect. The phrase אבנים טובות ומרגליות “precious stones and pearls” occurs frequently, from which it follows that the Talmud regards the pearl not as a stone consisting of earthy matter but, as is correct, as the hardening of an animal substance; which is why one need not, with Jellinek (Nachträge zu Sefer Chachamim p. 6), think of the Arabic and Persian دُرّ [durr] (pearl) in the Talmudic words אבן טובה יש בכרכי הים ודרה שמה (Megilla 12a); for apart from the fact that pearls were certainly not used for laying floors, the pearl is also not to be regarded as a stone. What is presumably correct is, as Rosenmüller [=E. F. K. Rosenmüller, Handbuch der biblischen Alterthumskunde, vol. 4, part 2 (Leipzig 1831)] (Bibl. Alterth. [=E. F. K. Rosenmüller, Handbuch der biblischen Alterthumskunde (Leipzig 1823–31)] 4, 1, p. 23) has shown, that דר means a kind of alabaster with a mother-of-pearl lustre; cf. also Winer (R.-W. s.v. Marmor).

‎In Joma 75a it is said: The pearl is white, i.e. milk-white*); yet it is known that oriental pearls tend toward [p. 348] flesh colour, and also that those that have grown old, after some fifty years, appear yellowish. See Voigt l. c. 3, p. 503.

‎A Talmudist attests (Sanhedrin 100a) that up to his time no pearl had been found the size of the egg of a species of turtledove ציצילחא (§. 252); Voigt (ibid. 3, p. 504), however, relates that someone in Moscow saw a pearl that was one inch in diameter and was valued at one and a half million paper roubles.

‎A fine proverb occurs in the Jerusalem Talmud (Berachot C, 9, fol. 12, d): מרגלית דליה לה טימי כל שמשבח בה פגמה “he who praises the priceless pearl disparages it.”

‎The name מרגלית corresponds to the Latin margarita, with interchange of the ל and the R-sound, f. §. 467.

‎A string of pearls (פוריל"ש) is called חומרי פתכייתא (Kiduschin 9a), the pearl-borer: נוקב מרגליות (Choschen Mispat §. 420, 15).

### ‎§. 496.

### ‎The Fan Mussel.

‎In the Talmud the proverb often occurs: אי לאו דלינא חספא לא משכחת מרגניתא תוחיה “Had I not brought up the stone (so Buxdorf [=Johannes Buxtorf, Lexicon Chaldaicum, Talmudicum et Rabbinicum (Basel 1640)] Lex. p. 1250), you would not have found the pearl beneath it” (Jebamoth 92b and frequently). The Tosephot (B. mez. [=Bava Metzia] 17b, s.v. אי) come close to natural-historical accuracy; they say: “There are on the sea bottom broad stones, looking like potsherds, beneath which pearls are found.” They refer to B. Kama 91a, where it is said proverbially of one who labours in vain over something: “You descend into the depths of the sea but bring up only a potsherd”; i.e., he brings up the shell that is without a pearl. For in many mussels, which open only ten days after being caught, one finds nothing, but on the other hand many pearls in a single one.

‎מרגניתא may perhaps specifically denote the fan-mussel pearl, as distinct from מרגליות, the pearl mussel.

‎A precious teaching is called מרגנותא (Berachoth 33b).

### ‎§. 497.

### ‎Crimson.

‎Finally, we include here the insect תולעת שני, “shining worm.” Crimson-dyed wool is called שני תולעת, which Maimuni (on Negaim [p. 349] 14, 1) explains as: צמר צבוע בכרמיז, “wool dyed in crimson.” Crimson is prepared from the swollen egg sacs of a kind of tortoise. These eggs are found on the leaves of the holly. The eggs resemble small seedless raisins and dye a brownish red. Rashi (on Jes. [=Isaiah] 1, 18) says: In the eggs lie worms (חולעים), which yield the red colour. The eggs, however, are also called dye grain or dye berry, which is why I place this article here among the zoophytes; cf. Rosenmüller (loc. cit. 4, 2. p. 447 ff.).

‎Besides our species of tortoise, the juice of the trumpet shell (p. 281), of the purple snail (p. 282), of the cuttlefish (p. 283), and the blood of cattle (§. 102) were also used for dyeing. On the dyeing of the antelope's horn see B. batra [=Bava Batra] 16b and Rashi there.

‎The Aramaic paraphrases take both שני תולעת (Exod. [=Exodus] 25, 4) and שני תולעת (Num. 19, 6) only as a colour, namely as purple. It is interesting that Jonathan [=Targum (Pseudo-)Jonathan] points the word שני only in the latter passage and the LXX only in the former.

‎On the oyster, which attaches itself to rocks, see above p. 285, note.
